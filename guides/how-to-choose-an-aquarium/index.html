How to Choose an Aquarium: Tank Size, Glass vs Acrylic, Shape and Stand

By FishCare AI Editorial TeamPublished 2026-11-199 min read

An aquarium can become a beautiful focal point in your home, but the tank you buy decides most of what comes after it: which fish you can keep, how stable the water stays, how heavy the setup is and where it can safely go. Choosing the tank is the one decision that is expensive to undo.

This guide covers that decision only — size, material, shape, stand, and whether to buy a kit or a used tank. Once you have the tank, our step-by-step fish tank setup guide walks through filling, cycling and stocking it.

Quick answer: Pick the fish first, then choose the largest rectangular tank your space and floor can carry — favoring length and width over height. Glass suits most home freshwater setups; acrylic makes sense when weight or a custom shape matters. Put it on a stand rated for the filled weight.

Start with the Fish, Not the Tank

A common beginner mistake is buying a tank because it looks good in the store, then trying to find fish that fit it. Work the other way round. Write down the fish you want and look up each one's adult size, group size, swimming style and temperature. Then choose a tank that meets the most demanding of them.

  • Schooling fish such as tetras and rasboras need horizontal swimming length for the whole group.
  • Bottom dwellers such as corydoras need floor area more than height.
  • Tall-bodied fish such as angelfish need extra height — see angelfish in a 55-gallon tank.
  • Territorial fish need room to set up separate territories and sight-line breaks.

Choose the Aquarium Size

Although small desktop aquariums may look convenient, they can be more difficult to maintain because water conditions can change quickly in a limited volume of water. Larger aquariums generally provide more stable water conditions, although they require more space, equipment and structural support.

Here are popular freshwater aquarium sizes with typical manufacturer dimensions and approximate filled weight:

Before choosing a size, also consider the available floor or cabinet space, the weight of the fully equipped aquarium, and whether you have enough room around it for filtration and maintenance.

Footprint Matters More Than Gallons

Two tanks with the same volume are not equal. The 20 gallon high and 20 gallon long above hold the same water, but the long version has 360 square inches of floor and water surface against the high version's 288 — 25% more.

  • More surface area means more gas exchange at the water surface, which helps keep oxygen levels up.
  • More floor area gives bottom dwellers and territorial fish more usable space.
  • More length gives active and schooling fish a longer swimming run.
  • Lower height is easier to reach into for planting and cleaning; very tall tanks often need long tools or a step.

When two tanks fit your space, the longer and wider one is usually the better choice for fish, even at the same gallon rating.

Glass vs Acrylic Aquariums

Aquariums are available in several materials and designs. Glass and acrylic are the two most common choices.

Glass Aquariums

Glass aquariums are widely used because they offer good scratch resistance, excellent clarity and relatively affordable pricing. They are available in many standard sizes and are suitable for most home freshwater setups.

Acrylic Aquariums

Acrylic aquariums are lighter than comparable glass models and offer greater flexibility in shaping and customization. They are often used for curved designs, specialized installations and projects where reduced weight is important. However, acrylic surfaces can scratch more easily and require appropriate cleaning materials.

FactorGlassAcrylic

Which should you pick? For a standard-size freshwater tank on a proper stand, glass is the practical default. Acrylic is worth the extra cost when weight matters (an upper floor, a large display), when you want a curved or custom shape, or when the tank will be in a busy area where a knock is likely.

Standard vs Low-Iron Glass

Glass tanks come in two grades. Standard float glass contains iron that gives it a faint green tint, most visible looking through the tank end-on or at thick panel edges. Low-iron glass, sold as ultra-clear, optiwhite or starfire, removes most of that tint and shows truer color. It costs more and makes the most difference on rimless display tanks and planted aquascapes, where the glass edges are visible.

Aquarium Shape and Design

Rectangular aquariums are popular because they provide a practical balance between viewing area, water volume and equipment installation. Other options each come with a trade-off:

  • Rimless aquariums give a clean, modern appearance but need thicker glass, a flat level surface, and a fitted lid if your fish jump. Evaporation is also faster without a cover.
  • Cube aquariums fit compact spaces, but their short length limits active swimmers.
  • Bow-front aquariums add a curved viewing area and depth, though the curve can distort the view and makes lids and backgrounds harder to fit.
  • Tall column and hexagonal tanks look striking but have little surface area and swimming length for their volume, and are hard to reach into.
  • Customized aquariums can fit specific furniture or interior designs; confirm equipment and lids will still fit.

Choose a design that meets your fish's needs first, then consider how it fits into your living space.

Choosing an Aquarium Stand

Once filled, an aquarium becomes extremely heavy. Water alone weighs about one kilogram per liter (8.3 lb per US gallon), before glass, substrate, rock and equipment. Most household furniture is not designed for that load held continuously, especially when it is concentrated on a small area.

  • Use a stand built and rated for aquariums of that size and weight, ideally the one the manufacturer pairs with the tank.
  • The stand top must match the tank's footprint, with no overhang at the edges or corners.
  • Check the stand is level front-to-back and side-to-side before the tank goes on; a filled tank should never be moved.
  • Cabinet stands hide equipment and leave room for a canister filter, cables and supplies.
  • For large tanks on upper floors, check that the floor structure can carry the total load.

Kit, Separate Parts or a Used Tank?

Aquarium kits

All-in-one kits bundle the tank with a lid, light, filter and sometimes a heater. They are convenient and often good value, but the included filter and light are usually basic. Check what is in the box and whether each part suits the fish you plan to keep; our aquarium equipment calculator shows what a given tank size needs.

Tank plus separate equipment

Buying the tank on its own lets you choose each piece of equipment, such as a quieter canister filter or a correctly sized heater from the heater calculator. It costs more up front but avoids replacing kit parts later.

Buying a used aquarium

A used tank can save a lot of money, but inspect it carefully:

  • Look for chips at corners and edges, cracks, and deep scratches on the front panel.
  • Check the silicone seams for peeling, gaps or bubbles.
  • Leak-test it filled for a day or two somewhere a leak won't cause damage, before it goes on the stand.
  • Clean it with water only, never household cleaners, and ask what was kept in it if the previous fish died of disease.

Before-You-Buy Checklist

  • ☐ I know the adult size and group needs of every fish I plan to keep.
  • ☐ The tank's length and footprint suit the most active or largest fish.
  • ☐ I have measured the space, including room above the tank for maintenance.
  • ☐ I know the approximate filled weight and have a stand rated for it.
  • ☐ I have chosen glass or acrylic for a reason (budget, weight, shape, safety).
  • ☐ The lid fits, or my fish do not jump.
  • ☐ There is a power outlet nearby and space for the filter.
  • ☐ I know which equipment comes with the tank and what I still need to buy.

Choosing an Aquarium FAQ

Is a glass or acrylic aquarium better?

Glass is the practical default for standard-size freshwater tanks: it resists scratches and costs less. Acrylic is lighter, more impact-resistant and easier to shape, but scratches easily and usually costs more.

What size aquarium should a beginner buy?

Choose the size from the adult needs of the fish you plan to keep. A 20 to 29 gallon tank keeps water conditions more stable than a small desktop tank, and a long footprint suits most small community fish.

Is a 20 gallon long better than a 20 gallon high?

For most fish, yes. Both hold about 20 gallons, but the long version has about 25% more floor and water surface area, giving more swimming length and better gas exchange.

Do I need a special aquarium stand?

Yes. A filled aquarium is very heavy, about 8.3 lb of water per US gallon before glass and rock. Use a level stand built and rated for that tank's size and weight rather than ordinary furniture.

Is it safe to buy a used aquarium?

Often, if you inspect it. Check for chipped corners, cracks and peeling silicone, and leak-test it filled for a day or two somewhere a leak won't cause damage before setting it up.
