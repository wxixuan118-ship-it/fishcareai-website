Cloudy Aquarium Water: What the Color Means, How to Fix It and How Long It Takes to Clear

🌫️ 5 causes, told apart by color  |  ⏱️ 1 day to 2 weeks to clear  |  🚨 Urgent if fish gasp or ammonia reads

Updated November 21, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

Cloudy aquarium water has five common causes, and the color of the haze tells you which one you have. Grey haze right after filling is dust from the gravel and settles in a day or two. White or milky water is a bacterial bloom, which in a new tank clears on its own in one to two weeks. Green water is free-floating algae. Brown or yellow water is tannins from wood or leaves, which tints the water but is harmless. The one to treat as urgent is white cloudiness in a tank that has been fine for months, because it usually means rotting food or a dead fish and rising ammonia.

The short answer

  • Fish gasping at the surface? Test ammonia, change 30–50% of the water and add aeration now. Work out the cause after.
  • Grey, new tank: substrate dust. Wait 24–48 hours with the filter running.
  • White/milky, tank under 6 weeks old: bacterial bloom. Leave the filter alone, feed lightly, test ammonia every 2 days. Clears in 1–2 weeks.
  • White/milky, established tank: find the waste (dead fish, overfeeding, dirty filter), do a 25–30% change and vacuum. Clears in 2–5 days.
  • Green: algae. Cut lighting to 6–8 hours, or a 3–4 day blackout, or a UV sterilizer. Clears in 3–7 days.
  • Brown/yellow: tannins. Harmless. Activated carbon clears it in 1–3 days.

Find the Cause in 2 Minutes

A quick check helps: fill a clear glass with tank water and hold it against something white. Dust settles to the bottom of the glass within an hour. A bacterial bloom stays evenly milky. Green water looks green even in the glass. Tannins look like weak tea but perfectly clear. Then test ammonia and nitrite. Those two readings decide whether you have a cosmetic problem or a fish-health one.

How to Fix White or Milky Water

In a new tank (under 6 weeks)

Milky water in a new tank is a bloom of free-floating bacteria that feed on the ammonia and organic matter while the filter bacteria are still growing. It is part of the tank finishing its cycle. Don't fight it with big water changes or a filter clean, which slow the cycle down. Instead:

  • Leave the filter media alone, and don't add new fish.
  • Feed lightly, every other day if fish are already in the tank.
  • Test ammonia and nitrite every 2 days. If either reads 0.5 ppm or more with fish in the tank, change 25–50% of the water. That is new tank syndrome and needs its own plan.
  • Expect the haze to clear within 1–2 weeks, usually as nitrite starts falling.

In an established tank

A sudden bloom in a tank that has been clear for months is feeding on something. Look for a dead fish or snail, food rotting in the gravel or behind decor, or a filter that has been switched off, clogged or cleaned too hard. Remove the source, vacuum the gravel, do a 25–30% water change and feed lightly for a few days. Test ammonia the same day. If the haze isn't clearly better in 3–5 days, the source is still there.

How to Fix Grey, Dusty Water

Fine particles from gravel, sand or crushed coral make grey haze that appears within minutes of filling. Run the filter and wait 24–48 hours. A pad of fine filter floss in the filter catches the dust faster. Replace it when it clogs. Next time, rinse new substrate in a bucket until the water runs almost clear, and pour new water onto a plate or bag so it doesn't stir the bottom.

How to Fix Green Water

Green water is a bloom of single-celled algae fed by too much light and too many nutrients. Water changes on their own rarely clear it, because the algae multiply faster than you can dilute them.

  • UV sterilizer: the most reliable fix. Usually clears green water in 3–7 days.
  • Blackout: cover the tank completely for 3–4 days with the lights off and an air stone running. Do a water change afterwards to remove the dead algae.
  • Then fix the cause: lights on 6–8 hours a day, no direct sunlight on the tank, less food, and keep nitrate under 20 ppm (see how to lower nitrates).

Green water often appears a few weeks after a new tank finishes cycling, once nitrate builds up. For algae on the glass, plants and decor rather than in the water, see how to get rid of algae.

Brown or Yellow Water

Tannins leach from driftwood, almond leaves and peat. The water turns tea-colored but stays clear, and it can lower pH slightly. It is harmless and many soft-water fish come from water like this. To remove it, add fresh activated carbon or a resin such as Purigen to the filter, which usually clears it in 1–3 days, and do your normal water changes. Boiling or soaking new driftwood for a week or two before it goes in reduces the effect.

When Cloudy Water Is an Emergency

Act now if any of these are true: fish are gasping at the surface or lying on the bottom; ammonia or nitrite reads 0.5 ppm or more; the water smells of rot; or the cloudiness came on within hours in an established tank. Change 30–50% of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, add an air stone, stop feeding for a day and look for something dead. Retest 1 hour later and again the next day.

What Not to Do

  • Don't replace or scrub the filter media in a cloudy new tank. It holds the bacteria that will end the problem.
  • Don't change 100% of the water. It resets the bloom and shocks the fish.
  • Don't rely on clarifier drops alone. Clarifiers clump fine particles so the filter can catch them. They help with dust, but they don't remove the waste that causes a bacterial bloom.
  • Don't add more fish until the water is clear and ammonia and nitrite read 0.

Cloudy Aquarium Water FAQ

How long does cloudy water last in a new tank?

Grey dust from new gravel settles in 24–48 hours. A white bacterial bloom in a new tank usually clears within 1–2 weeks as the filter bacteria establish. Leave the filter media alone while it does.

Is cloudy water bad for fish?

The haze itself is rarely harmful. What matters is the cause: a bacterial bloom in an established tank usually comes with rising ammonia from rotting waste. Test ammonia and nitrite to find out.

Should I do a water change if my tank is cloudy?

In an established tank, yes: 25–30% with a gravel vacuum after removing the source. In a new cycling tank, only change water if ammonia or nitrite reaches 0.5 ppm with fish present, because big changes slow the cycle.

Why did my tank get cloudy after a water change?

Usually stirred-up debris, which settles in hours, or a bacterial bloom from nutrients in the new water. Hard tap water and pH products can also form a white mineral haze.

Why is my fish tank cloudy after cleaning the filter?

Scrubbing or replacing media removes filter bacteria, so ammonia rises and free-floating bacteria bloom. Test ammonia daily for a week and rinse media only in old tank water in future.

Do water clarifiers work?

They clump fine particles so the filter can trap them, which helps with dust and light haze. They don't remove the waste behind a bacterial bloom or kill green water.

How do I get rid of green cloudy water?

A UV sterilizer usually clears it in 3–7 days. Without one, cover the tank for 3–4 days with aeration running, then reduce lighting to 6–8 hours and keep nitrate low.
