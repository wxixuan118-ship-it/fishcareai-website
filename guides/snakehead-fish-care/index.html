🐍 Snakehead Fish Care

Snakehead Fish Care Guide (Channa)

🐟 50+ Channa species  |  📏 4 in to 4 ft  |  🫁 Obligate air-breather  |  ⚖️ Restricted in the US

Snakeheads (family Channidae) are air-breathing ambush predators from Asia and Africa. In the aquarium trade they are usually sold under the genus name Channa — a group of more than 50 described species that runs from 4-inch dwarfs suited to a 30-gallon tank all the way up to the 4-foot giant snakehead. That range is why almost every generic “snakehead fish care” answer online is wrong for the fish actually in front of you: the correct tank size, temperature and diet depend entirely on which Channa you have.

This guide covers what all snakeheads share, then points you to the species-specific numbers. Read the legal section first — in the United States, snakeheads are federally restricted, and that is not a formality.

Legal status first. All Channa and Parachanna species are listed as injurious wildlife under the US Lacey Act, and many states ban possession outright. Check the legality section before you buy, import or rehome one.

What Is a Snakehead Fish?

Snakeheads get their name from the flattened, scaled head and the long cylindrical body — the profile genuinely looks like a snake when the fish hangs motionless under a leaf. Two genera make up the family:

  • Channa — roughly 50+ species across South, Southeast and East Asia. This is what “channa fish” means in the hobby.
  • Parachanna — three African species, rarely seen in the trade.

Three traits define how you have to keep them, and every care decision on this site follows from these:

  1. They are obligate air-breathers. A snakehead has a suprabranchial chamber above the gills that works like a primitive lung. It must surface every few minutes to gulp atmospheric air. A snakehead sealed under a tight lid with no air gap, or trapped under décor, will drown — in fully oxygenated water.
  2. They are ambush piscivores. They sit still for hours and then strike. Anything that fits in the mouth is food, and the mouth is larger than it looks.
  3. They are escape artists. Snakeheads jump, push at lids and squeeze through filter cutouts. A weighted, fully sealed cover is not optional equipment; it is the single most common cause of death in captivity.

What they are not is the tabloid monster of the “Frankenfish” headlines. They do not hunt people, they do not stalk across dry land for miles, and most species cannot survive out of water for more than a short period in damp conditions. The real concern is ecological, not personal — see legality and invasive status.

Are Snakehead Fish Legal? Invasive Status Explained

This is the most important question on the page, and the honest answer for most US readers is no.

United States

In 2002 the US Fish and Wildlife Service added all species of Channa and Parachanna to the injurious wildlife list under the Lacey Act. In practice that means:

  • Import into the US is prohibited, as is transport across state lines.
  • Possession is governed by state law, and a large majority of states prohibit it outright — including California, Florida, Texas, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Maine and Wisconsin. A handful of states have narrower rules or permit systems.
  • Because interstate transport is federally banned, even in a state with no explicit possession ban there is usually no lawful way to obtain one.

Penalties are real: fines, confiscation and in some jurisdictions criminal charges. If you already own a snakehead, contact your state wildlife agency — never release it into local water and never flush it.

United Kingdom and European Union

The northern snakehead (Channa argus) is listed as an Invasive Alien Species of Union Concern, which bans keeping, breeding, selling and releasing it across the EU, and it carries equivalent restrictions in the UK. Other Channa species are generally legal to keep in the UK and much of the EU, but rules change — verify before buying.

Elsewhere

Canada bans snakeheads in several provinces including Ontario. Australia prohibits them nationally. In much of Asia they are native, farmed as food fish, and completely unrestricted.

Why they are restricted

Channa argus became established in the Potomac River after a 2002 discovery in a Maryland pond, and the bullseye snakehead (C. marulius) is established in southeast Florida. Air-breathing lets them survive waters that would kill other predators, and they guard their fry aggressively, so populations establish easily. Field studies since have found the ecological damage less catastrophic than early coverage predicted, but the precautionary listing stands.

Are snakehead fish dangerous to humans? There are no verified records of unprovoked attacks on people. A large snakehead guarding fry will charge and can bite a hand placed in the nest, and a 3-foot C. micropeltes has teeth that warrant respect during maintenance — but they are not a danger to swimmers.

Tank Size and Setup

The working rule is tank length at least 3× adult body length and width at least 1.5×. A 5-inch C. andrao therefore wants a 30-inch tank — a standard 30-gallon breeder is a good pair tank. A 30-inch C. marulioides wants an 8-foot tank, which is why so few people should own one.

Setup priorities, in order:

  • A weighted, gap-free lid with a 2–4 inch humid air gap above the water. Block every filter and heater cutout. Snakeheads breathe warm humid air; an open-topped tank in a cold room stresses the breathing organ even if the fish does not jump out.
  • Footprint over height. They are bottom-and-mid ambushers that need floor area, not depth.
  • Low flow. Wild habitat is sluggish streams, swamps and paddy fields. Baffle strong returns.
  • Heavy cover. Caves, driftwood tangles, leaf litter and dense floating plants. A snakehead with nowhere to hide stays skittish and jumps.
  • Oversized filtration. A predator fed whole seafood produces a heavy waste load. Rate the filter for at least 1.5× tank volume and change 30–50% of the water weekly.

Water Parameters and Temperature

There is no single “snakehead fish temperature”. The genus spans the tropics to temperate China, and keeping a subtropical species at tropical temperatures year-round is one of the commonest slow-killers in the hobby.

Food and Feeding

Snakeheads are carnivores, but a diet of live feeder fish is the wrong way to feed one. Goldfish and rosy red minnows contain thiaminase, which destroys vitamin B1 and causes neurological damage over months, and feeders are the most reliable way to introduce disease into a tank.

Feed instead a rotation of thawed frozen and fresh foods: shell-on shrimp and prawn, mussel, krill, silversides, white fish fillet, earthworms, and for dwarf species bloodworm, mysis and insects such as crickets and dubia roaches. Most Channa can be weaned onto quality carnivore pellets, which makes long-term nutrition far easier.

Feed juveniles daily; adults 2–3 times a week is plenty. Obesity and fatty liver disease are far more common in captive snakeheads than starvation. Full schedule, portion sizes and a pellet-weaning method: what snakehead fish eat.

Behaviour, Aggression and Normal Weirdness

New snakehead keepers panic about behaviour that is entirely normal for an ambush predator:

  • Lying motionless on the bottom for hours is normal. It is how the fish hunts.
  • Rising to the surface to gulp air every few minutes is normal and mandatory. It is not gasping. Distress looks different — the fish stays at the surface with rapid gill movement instead of returning to its station.
  • Hiding for two to four weeks after import is normal. Dim the lights, add cover, stop staring at it.
  • Darkening or losing colour is often a mood and dominance signal in Channa and can change within hours.

Aggression is real and species-specific. C. bleheri and C. andrao are comparatively mild; C. micropeltes, C. marulius and C. argus are seriously aggressive and every species becomes far worse when spawning. The most dangerous tank mate for a snakehead is another snakehead.

Tank Mates

The realistic answer is that most snakeheads are best kept alone or as a proven pair. Where tank mates work at all, they follow three rules: nothing small enough to swallow, nothing slow with trailing fins, and nothing that competes for the same floor space.

Dwarf species in a large, heavily planted tank can sometimes live with fast mid-water dithers such as rosy barbs, tiger barbs or larger rasboras. Big species realistically get bichirs, large plecos or nothing. Oscars, arowanas and African cichlids are all common suggestions that mostly end badly — the reasons are broken down on the snakehead tank mates guide, and you can sanity-check a pairing with the compatibility checker.

Common Health Problems

Snakeheads are hardy fish that mostly get sick for three reasons: wild-caught parasite loads, injuries from jumping or fighting, and diet.

  • Not eating — usually settling stress in a new fish, wrong temperature for the species, or a natural seasonal fast. Weeks of refusal in an otherwise bright, alert fish is common and rarely an emergency.
  • Internal parasites — wild-caught imports frequently carry nematodes and tapeworms. Quarantine 4–6 weeks and treat before the fish goes in the display.
  • Bloating — overfeeding first, dropsy second. Pineconed scales change the diagnosis.
  • Ulcers, cloudy eye and fin damage — nearly always secondary to a jumping impact, a bite, or nitrate creep.

Symptom-by-symptom diagnosis, including white spots, turning black, swimming sideways and true gasping: snakehead fish diseases and symptoms.

Breeding

Channa split into two reproductive strategies. The dwarf gachua-group species — including C. andrao, C. bleheri and C. pulchra — are paternal mouthbrooders: the pair spawns in an embrace near the surface, the male collects the floating eggs and incubates them for several days, then guards the free-swimming fry. Large species such as C. striata, C. argus and C. micropeltes are nest guarders that produce a floating raft of eggs and defend the fry as a pair, sometimes for months.

Both routes need an established, well-fed pair, cover, and in subtropical species a cool period followed by warming and soft-water changes. Step-by-step conditioning, spawning and fry raising: breeding snakehead fish. Telling the sexes apart first: male vs female snakehead.

Should You Keep a Snakehead?

If you are in the US, the answer is almost certainly no, on legal grounds alone. Where they are legal, a dwarf Channa in a 40-gallon planted tank with a locked lid is a genuinely rewarding fish — intelligent, interactive, and one of the few aquarium species that will watch you back. A giant snakehead in a home aquarium is a mistake that takes three years to become obvious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are snakehead fish illegal?

In the United States all Channa and Parachanna species are listed as injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act, so import and interstate transport are federally banned and most states prohibit possession outright. Channa argus is banned across the EU and UK as an Invasive Alien Species of Union Concern, while other Channa species are generally legal in the UK. Always check your state or national rules before buying.

Can you keep snakehead fish as pets?

Where it is legal, yes - dwarf species such as Channa andrao and Channa bleheri do well in a 30-55 gallon tank with a sealed lid, low flow and heavy cover. Large species reaching 2-4 feet are not realistic home aquarium fish. In most of the United States keeping any snakehead is prohibited.

How big do snakehead fish get?

It depends entirely on species. Dwarf snakeheads such as Channa andrao stay around 4-5 inches, mid-sized species like Channa aurantimaculata reach 12-16 inches, and the giant snakehead Channa micropeltes can exceed 4 feet.

Are snakehead fish dangerous?

Not to people. There are no verified records of unprovoked snakehead attacks on humans. A large adult guarding fry will charge and can bite a hand placed into the nest, so use care during maintenance, but they pose no threat to swimmers.

Do snakehead fish need to breathe air?

Yes. Snakeheads are obligate air-breathers with a suprabranchial chamber above the gills and must surface every few minutes to gulp atmospheric air. A snakehead denied surface access will drown even in fully oxygenated water, so the tank needs a 2-4 inch humid air gap under a secure lid.

Are snakehead fish aggressive?

Most are, to varying degrees. Channa bleheri and Channa andrao are comparatively mild, while Channa micropeltes, Channa marulius and Channa argus are seriously aggressive. Every species becomes far more aggressive when spawning, and conspecific aggression between snakeheads is the leading cause of death in mixed setups.
