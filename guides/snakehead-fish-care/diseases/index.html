🐍 Snakehead Fish Care

Snakehead Fish Diseases and Symptoms

🧪 Test water first  |  🪱 Most stillness is normal  |  🐛 Quarantine wild-caught 4–6 wks

Snakeheads are physically tough fish, and that is the problem: because they breathe air and tolerate poor water, they hide trouble far longer than a tetra would. Most of what looks alarming in a Channa is normal predator behaviour, and most of what is genuinely wrong traces to three sources — wild-caught parasite loads, injuries from jumping or fighting, and diet.

Test the water before you medicate. Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, temperature. Roughly half of all snakehead symptom threads end with a water quality answer, and medicating a fish in bad water makes things worse. Run a reading through the water parameter checker first.

Normal Behaviour Mistaken for Illness

Snakehead not moving or lying on the bottom

This is what an ambush predator does. A healthy Channa will sit motionless on the substrate or wedged under wood for hours, moving only to surface for air. Judge the fish on the surrounding signs instead: clear eyes, upright posture, fins held normally rather than clamped, calm gill movement, and a response when you approach. If those are fine, the fish is fine.

Snakehead gasping for air at the surface

Snakeheads must surface to breathe atmospheric air — typically every few minutes. Normal air-breathing is a deliberate rise, a gulp, and an immediate return to station on the bottom. Distress looks completely different: the fish stays at the surface, gill covers working rapidly, often tilted head-up, and repeats without settling. Normal breathing is not an emergency; staying at the surface is.

Snakehead hiding

Two to four weeks of hiding after import is standard. Dim the lights, add floating plants and caves, stop approaching the glass, and offer small food items every second day. A snakehead that has never come out after two months usually has too little cover, not too much — counter-intuitively, fish with more hiding places spend more time in the open.

Snakehead losing colour or turning black

Colour in Channa is partly a mood signal and can shift within hours. Darkening during territorial disputes, after a move, or under bright light is normal and reverses. Persistent dullness over weeks is different, and the first thing to test is nitrate — chronic nitrate above 40 ppm produces exactly that. Fixed black patches that appeared after a water quality problem are usually healing ammonia burn and fade over weeks to months.

Snakehead Fish Parasites

Most snakeheads in the trade are wild-caught and arrive carrying something. This is the single strongest argument for a 4–6 week quarantine in a bare-bottom tank before the fish goes anywhere near a display.

ParasiteSignsTreatment
Internal nematodes (roundworm)Thin fish with a good appetite, sunken belly, sometimes visible worms at the ventLevamisole or fenbendazole, repeated after 2–3 weeks
Cestodes (tapeworm)Weight loss, pale stringy waste, food spat outPraziquantel, repeated
Gill and skin flukesFlicking, rubbing on décor, rapid gill movement, staying at the surfacePraziquantel
Ich (white spot)Salt-grain white dots on body and fins, flickingRaise temperature gradually, aquarium salt, or a standard ich treatment
Anchor worm / fish liceVisible attached parasites, localised rednessManual removal plus an appropriate parasiticide
Air-breathing changes the maths on treatment. Snakeheads tolerate the low dissolved oxygen of heat treatment better than most fish, so raising the temperature for ich is comparatively safe. They also tolerate aquarium salt well. Be conservative with copper-based medications, and always dose to the actual water volume after subtracting substrate and décor.

Bacterial and Fungal Problems

Fin rot

Ragged, receding fin margins, sometimes with a white or reddened edge. In snakeheads it is almost always secondary — a bite from a tank mate or another snakehead, or a jumping injury, followed by poor water. Treat by fixing the water first (daily 30% changes, nitrate under 30 ppm) and only reaching for an antibacterial if the erosion continues after a week.

Ulcers and red sores

Aeromonas and similar bacteria entering through a wound. Common after a fish jumps and lands hard, or after a fight. Isolate, keep the water immaculate, and treat with a broad-spectrum antibacterial. Deep ulcers on a large fish may need a vet-prescribed antibiotic.

Cloudy eye

One cloudy eye usually means physical injury — a jump into the lid, or a strike at the glass. Both eyes cloudy usually means water quality. Clean water resolves most cases in one to two weeks.

Dropsy

A swollen belly with scales standing out like a pine cone is organ failure, usually kidney, and the prognosis is poor. A swollen belly with flat scales is almost always overfeeding or constipation — fast the fish for three to five days and feed a shelled, gut-loaded food such as earthworm afterwards.

Diet-Related Problems

  • Thiamine (B1) deficiency. Caused by a long-term diet of goldfish or rosy red minnows, which contain thiaminase. Signs are progressive and neurological: erratic swimming, loss of balance, swimming sideways, eventual seizures. Prevention is the only reliable treatment — see what to feed instead.
  • Fatty liver disease. From overfeeding, and from mammal or bird meat such as beef heart. Signs are a persistently swollen body, lethargy and reduced appetite. Reversible early by cutting to 2–3 feeds a week of lean whole foods.
  • Buoyancy problems. Usually overfeeding or gulping air with floating food. Feed sinking items and fast for a few days.
  • Constipation. A diet of soft foods with no shell or fibre. Shell-on shrimp and earthworms fix it.

Injuries: Jumping and Fighting

These are the injuries that actually kill captive snakeheads.

Jumping

A snakehead that hits the lid hard can suffer a bruised or cloudy eye, a scraped snout, jaw damage or internal injury; one that gets out will die within hours to a day. Every jump is a warning. Fix the cause the same day: a weighted, gap-free lid, all filter cutouts sealed, plus the underlying stressor — usually too little cover, too much light, strong flow or an aggressive tank mate. Full lid specification is on the tank size and setup page.

Fighting

Snakehead-on-snakehead damage is severe and fast — torn fins, jaw injuries, lost eyes. It happens when a pair bonds and turns on the rest of the group, when two unpaired adults share a tank, or during spawning. There is no medication for it. Separate the fish, treat the wounds as bacterial risk, and re-read tank mates.

Quarantine and Treatment Protocol

  1. Bare-bottom quarantine tank, 20 gallons or more, with its own sealed lid and a cycled sponge filter.
  2. Observe for a week before dosing anything. Let the fish settle; note breathing rate, waste appearance and whether it eats.
  3. Treat for internal parasites as a matter of course for a wild-caught import: praziquantel, then levamisole or fenbendazole, each repeated after 2–3 weeks to catch the next life stage.
  4. Treat externals only if seen — do not stack medications prophylactically.
  5. Hold 4–6 weeks total with clean water and good food before moving the fish to the display.

Remove carbon before dosing, dose to the real water volume, and keep the lid closed and weighted throughout — a medicated fish in an unfamiliar bare tank is the most likely of all to jump.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my snakehead fish not moving?

Almost always because it is behaving normally. Snakeheads are ambush predators that sit motionless on the bottom or under decor for hours, moving only to surface for air. Judge health by the surrounding signs instead - clear eyes, upright posture, unclamped fins, calm gill movement and a response when you approach. If those look right, the fish is fine.

Is my snakehead gasping for air or breathing normally?

Snakeheads are obligate air-breathers and must surface every few minutes, so a deliberate rise, a gulp and an immediate return to the bottom is normal. Distress looks different: the fish stays at the surface, often tilted head-up, with rapid gill movement, and repeats without settling. That pattern points to gill damage or gill flukes and needs immediate water testing.

Why is my snakehead fish turning black?

Colour in Channa is partly a mood signal, so darkening during territorial disputes, after a move or under bright light is normal and reverses within hours. Fixed black patches that appeared after a water quality problem are usually healing ammonia burn and fade over weeks to months. Persistent dullness over weeks usually means nitrate above 40 ppm.

Why does my snakehead fish have white spots?

White salt-grain dots on the body and fins, usually with flicking against decor, indicate ich (Ichthyophthirius). Because snakeheads breathe air they tolerate the low oxygen of heat treatment better than most fish, so raising the temperature gradually alongside aquarium salt or a standard ich medication works well. Remove carbon before dosing.

Why is my snakehead fish bloated?

Check the scales. A swollen belly with flat scales is nearly always overfeeding or constipation - fast the fish three to five days, then feed shell-on shrimp or earthworm. A swollen belly with scales standing out like a pine cone is dropsy, indicating organ failure, and the prognosis is poor.

Why does my snakehead fish keep jumping?

Jumping is normal escape behaviour and the leading cause of death in captive snakeheads. Fix the lid the same day - a weighted, gap-free solid cover with all filter cutouts sealed - and then the underlying stressor, which is usually too little cover, too much light, strong flow or an aggressive tank mate.
