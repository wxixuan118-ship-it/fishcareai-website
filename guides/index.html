Aquarium fish care in short

Cycle the tank before adding fish. Keep ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm and nitrate under 40 ppm. Change 20–25% of the water weekly with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. Feed once or twice a day, only what the fish finish in about two minutes. Match temperature, pH and hardness to the species you keep, and add new fish a few at a time.

Aquarium Fish Care Basics

These are the numbers and habits that sit underneath every species guide above. If a fish is unwell, check this list before you reach for medication — water quality explains most sudden problems.

The five things every aquarium needs

  1. Enough water volume. Size the tank to the adult fish, not the juvenile in the shop. A larger volume dilutes waste and resists temperature swings, which is why a 20-gallon tank is easier to keep alive than a 5-gallon one.
  2. A cycled filter. Beneficial bacteria convert ammonia to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate. Until that colony exists, every meal you feed becomes a toxin. Cycle before stocking, and never wash media in tap water.
  3. Stable water chemistry. Fish tolerate a wide range of values but not rapid change. A pH of 7.6 held steady beats a "perfect" 7.0 that swings half a point a day.
  4. Controlled feeding. Two small meals beat one large one. Uneaten food is the main source of nitrate, and nitrate is what feeds algae.
  5. Compatible stocking. Match temperature ranges, temperament and adult size. Most aggression problems are stocking problems that were decided at the shop.

A weekly and monthly care routine

Recommended daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly aquarium maintenance schedule
How oftenWhat to do
DailyFeed once or twice, only what is eaten in about two minutes. Count the fish. Check the heater reading. Look for gasping, clamped fins, hiding or white spots.
WeeklyTest ammonia, nitrite and nitrate. Change 20–25% of the water with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. Vacuum roughly a third of the substrate. Wipe the front glass.
MonthlyRinse filter media in old tank water — never tap. Trim plants. Check the impeller, tubing and airline. Top off evaporation with fresh dechlorinated or RO water, not saltwater.
QuarterlyReplace worn media (not all at once). Check test kit expiry dates. Deep-clean the substrate in sections across several weeks. Reassess stocking as fish grow.

Six mistakes that cause most beginner losses

  • Adding fish on day one. With no bacterial colony, ammonia climbs within 48 hours and burns gills. Cycle first, or stock very lightly and test daily.
  • Buying by shop size. A two-inch common pleco reaches 18 inches. A bala shark needs 125 gallons. Check adult size before you buy.
  • Overfeeding. The most common single cause of nitrate build-up, algae blooms and cloudy water in a new tank.
  • Cleaning too hard. Replacing all the water and scrubbing the filter in the same session removes the bacteria and restarts the cycle.
  • Medicating before testing. Most "sick fish" cases are ammonia, nitrite or temperature. Test first; medication on top of bad water makes things worse.
  • Mixing incompatible fish. Goldfish with tropicals, bettas with fin-nippers, or a single schooling fish kept alone all end badly. Use the compatibility checker before you stock.

Aquarium Fish Care FAQ

What is aquarium fish care?

Aquarium fish care is the routine of keeping a tank's water, filtration, temperature, feeding and stocking within the range your species tolerate. In practice it means a cycled filter, ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm, nitrate under 40 ppm, a stable temperature, a measured feeding schedule and tank mates that suit each other.

How often should I change aquarium water?

For most stocked freshwater community tanks, change 20–25% of the water once a week using dechlorinated water matched to tank temperature. Heavily stocked tanks, goldfish and large cichlids often need 30–50% weekly. Never change all the water at once, and never rinse filter media in tap water.

How much should I feed aquarium fish?

Feed once or twice a day and offer only what the fish finish in about two minutes. Uneaten food breaks down into ammonia and then nitrate, which is the most common cause of algae and poor water quality in new tanks. One fasting day a week is fine for most adult community fish.

What water parameters do aquarium fish need?

Ammonia 0 ppm, nitrite 0 ppm, nitrate under 40 ppm (under 20 ppm preferred), pH stable between roughly 6.5 and 7.8, KH 3–8 dKH so the pH does not crash, GH 4–12 dGH, and 74–80°F (23–27°C) for a tropical community tank. Stability matters more than hitting an exact number.

Do I need to cycle a new aquarium before adding fish?

Yes. A new filter has no bacteria to convert ammonia into nitrite and then nitrate, so fish added on day one are exposed to ammonia burn. A fishless cycle takes about four to six weeks, or a few days to two weeks if you seed the filter with mature media from an established tank. The cycle is finished when ammonia and nitrite both read 0 ppm within 24 hours of dosing ammonia.

What is the easiest fish to care for in an aquarium?

Guppies, platies, zebra danios, neon tetras and corydoras are the usual beginner picks: they tolerate a wide temperature range, accept prepared foods and stay small enough for a 10–20 gallon tank. Bettas are easy too, provided the tank is heated and filtered rather than an unheated bowl.

How do I know if my aquarium fish is sick?

Watch for clamped fins, gasping at the surface, hiding, loss of appetite, white spots, cotton-like growths, red streaks or rapid gill movement. Test the water before reaching for medication — ammonia, nitrite or a temperature swing explains most sudden illness, and correcting water quality resolves it without treatment.

What size aquarium should a beginner start with?

A 20-gallon tank is easier to keep stable than a 5 or 10 gallon, because a larger water volume dilutes ammonia spikes and temperature swings. Choose the tank around the adult size of the fish you want, not the size they are in the shop.
