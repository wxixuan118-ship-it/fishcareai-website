How to Lower Nitrates in an Aquarium: How Much Water to Change, How Often and When It's Fixed

💧 25–50% change a day until under 20 ppm  |  ⏱️ Drops the same hour  |  ✅ Solved after 3 good weekly tests

Updated November 21, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

To lower nitrates in an aquarium, change water first and fix the source second. Every water change removes nitrate in proportion to how much you change: a 50% change cuts the reading in half, as long as your tap water has little nitrate in it. From 80 ppm, two 50% changes a day apart get you to about 20 ppm. Then you find out why it climbed, usually too much food, too many fish or too few water changes, so it doesn't climb back.

The short answer

  1. Test the tank and your tap water. If the tap reads 20 ppm, water changes can't take the tank below 20 ppm.
  2. Change 25–50% of the water once a day until the tank reads under 20 ppm (under 40 ppm is the upper limit for most freshwater fish). Use no more than 50% a day if nitrate is above 100 ppm.
  3. Retest an hour after each change. The drop is immediate, so you can see whether the plan is working the same day.
  4. Find the source: cut feeding back, vacuum the gravel, rinse filter sponges in tank water and remove anything dead or rotting.
  5. Call it solved when your normal weekly water change holds nitrate under 20–40 ppm for 3 weekly tests in a row.

How High Is Too High? Nitrate Levels in ppm

Most hobby test kits, including the common liquid kits, report nitrate as NO3 in ppm (mg/L). These are the ranges we use for a freshwater tank:

Nitrate (ppm NO3)What it meansWhat to do
0–20Good. Normal for a tank with regular water changes.Keep your routine.
20–40Acceptable for most community fish, but the routine is not keeping up.Make the weekly change bigger, or feed less.
40–80High. Long exposure is linked to stress, poor growth and algae.Daily 25–50% changes until under 20, then fix the source.
80–160Very high. Usually months of missed maintenance.Daily changes, no more than 50% a day. Check pH first (see below).
Over 160Dangerous, especially with fish showing lethargy or gasping.Same plan, retest daily, and treat it as an emergency.

Sensitive fish and invertebrates want less. Shrimp, discus, wild-caught soft-water fish and fry do best under 10–20 ppm. In a review of nitrate toxicity, Camargo and colleagues recommended a ceiling of 2 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen (about 9 ppm on a hobby kit) to protect the most sensitive freshwater animals, and found that 10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen (44 ppm) can harm freshwater invertebrates over long exposures. Marine animals are more tolerant: the same review suggests about 20 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen (roughly 89 ppm) is generally acceptable for them. Reef corals are a different case, covered further down.

Check the units on your kit. Some kits and lab reports give nitrate as nitrate-nitrogen (NO3-N). Multiply NO3-N by 4.43 to get NO3. US tap water can legally contain up to 10 mg/L NO3-N, which is 44 ppm on a hobby kit, so "safe to drink" does not mean low enough for an aquarium.

Step-by-Step: How to Bring Nitrate Down

StepWhat to doHow long it takes
1 · TestTest the tank and a glass of tap water. Write both down. Also test pH and ammonia if nitrate is over 80 ppm.10 minutes
2 · First changeChange 25–50% of the water. Vacuum the gravel as you drain. Treat new water with dechlorinator and match the temperature within about 2°F (1°C).Immediate drop
3 · RetestTest again 1 hour later. The reading should have fallen by roughly the share of water you changed.Same day
4 · Repeat dailyRepeat once a day until the tank reads under your target. Use the calculator below to see how many changes you need.2–5 days for most tanks
5 · Fix the sourceFeed only what is eaten in about 2 minutes, skip one day a week, remove dead plants or fish, rinse sponges in old tank water and check stocking.Start on day 1
6 · Measure the weekly riseAfter the last change, test the same day and again 7 days later. The difference is how much nitrate your tank makes per week.1 week
7 · Set the routinePick a weekly change big enough to remove that weekly rise (calculator, second part). If it needs more than 50% a week, reduce the source.2–3 weeks to confirm
Very high nitrate in an old tank? Go slower. Tanks that have gone months without a water change often have a low pH as well. A huge change can swing pH up fast, and any ammonia in the water becomes more toxic as pH rises. If nitrate is over 100 ppm and pH is well below your tap water's, change 25% a day instead of 50% and watch the fish for an hour after each change.

The maths is simple. After each change, nitrate = old reading × (1 − share changed) + tap reading × share changed. In a steady routine, the highest reading just before each weekly change settles at tap nitrate + weekly rise ÷ share changed. A tank that gains 10 ppm a week with a 25% weekly change peaks at about 40 ppm above the tap level. Doubling the change to 50% halves that to 20 ppm.

Why Are My Nitrates High?

Nitrate is the end product of the nitrogen cycle. Fish waste and leftover food become ammonia, filter bacteria turn that into nitrite and then into nitrate, and nitrate stays in the water until you take it out. So high nitrate always means more waste is going in than water changes are taking out.

Something deadA sudden jump with no change in routine.Count the fish and snails. Check behind decor and inside the filter.

When Is the Nitrate Problem Solved?

A single low reading after a water change only shows that the water change worked. The problem is solved when all three are true:

  • Nitrate is under your target (20 ppm for most tanks, 10 ppm for sensitive species) right before your normal weekly change, three weeks in a row.
  • The weekly rise is small enough that a 25–50% weekly change removes it.
  • Ammonia and nitrite read 0, and the fish are eating and active.

If nitrate is back up within a week, the source has not been fixed. Go back to the causes table rather than doing ever-bigger water changes.

    Lowering Nitrates in a Saltwater or Reef Tank

    Water changes work the same way in a marine tank, but salt mix makes them more expensive, so marine keepers lean more on export: a protein skimmer to remove waste before it breaks down, macroalgae in a refugium that you harvest, and carbon dosing or biopellets in experienced hands. Fish-only tanks are usually fine under 40 ppm. Reef tanks are different: most reef keepers aim for roughly 2–10 ppm, because corals also need some nitrate, and stripping it to zero can cause coral bleaching and cyano. Lower a reef tank's nitrate gradually over weeks, not in one go.

    Lowering Nitrates FAQ

    What is a safe nitrate level in a fish tank?

    Under 20 ppm is the usual target for freshwater tanks and under 40 ppm the upper limit for most community fish. Shrimp, discus and fry do best under 10–20 ppm. Fish-only saltwater tanks tolerate up to about 40 ppm, and reef tanks usually run at 2–10 ppm.

    How fast can I lower nitrates?

    A water change lowers nitrate the same hour by roughly the share of water you change. From 80 ppm, two 50% changes on consecutive days bring it to about 20 ppm, as long as the tap water has little nitrate.

    Is a 50% water change safe?

    Usually, if the new water is dechlorinated and within about 2°F of the tank. In a neglected tank with very high nitrate and low pH, change 25% a day instead, because a sudden pH rise can make any ammonia more toxic.

    Why are my nitrates high after a water change?

    Either the tap water contains nitrate, or the change was too small for the reading. Test a glass of tap water. If it reads 20 ppm or more, mix in RO or distilled water.

    Do live plants lower nitrates?

    Yes, but slowly. Fast-growing and floating plants take up some nitrate over several weeks. In a well-stocked tank they reduce how often you need big water changes rather than replacing them.

    Do nitrate-removing products work?

    Resins and pads absorb nitrate until they are full, then stop. They can help a tank with nitrate in the tap water, but they don't fix overfeeding or overstocking, so water changes are still needed.

    Can high nitrates kill fish?

    Very high nitrate, usually well over 100 ppm, can stress and kill fish, especially when it rises suddenly or the fish are moved into a tank that has built up nitrate over months. Long exposure at lower levels is linked to poor growth and disease.
