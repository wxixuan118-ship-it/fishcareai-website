Betta Fish Tank Mates: Safe Species, Species to Avoid & Setup Rules

🐟 10 gal minimum  |  🚫 One male per tank  |  🌿 Heavy planting required

Betta compatibility is not a fixed list — it depends on the individual fish, the tank, and how you introduce the newcomers. This guide covers which species usually work, which never do, how to read your own betta's temperament before you spend money, and what to do when a pairing fails.

Quick answer: One male betta per tank, always. In a 10-gallon or larger planted tank, many bettas coexist with small, fast, drab-coloured species — ember tetras, chili rasboras, corydoras, otocinclus and snails. Avoid long-finned or brightly coloured fish, fin nippers, and other gouramis. Have a backup tank ready before you start.

Can Betta Fish Live With Other Fish?

The short answer: yes — but with important conditions. Male bettas are territorial and will aggressively attack other male bettas and any fish that resembles one (colourful, long-finned species). With the right tank mates, in a large enough tank with adequate cover, many bettas coexist peacefully with a wide variety of community fish.

The one absolute rule: no two male bettas together, ever. They will fight to the death. One male betta per tank, always.

It is worth being honest about where the hobby and the species literature disagree here. Seriously Fish states plainly that Betta splendens is "not recommended for the standard community aquarium" and is "best kept alone", noting that only a single individual can be kept per tank in most cases and that individual fish may sometimes tolerate other species. Community keeping is common and often successful, but it is the exception being managed, not the default — which is why every recommendation below assumes you have somewhere to move fish if it does not work.

The success of any betta community setup depends on three factors: the individual betta's temperament (some are aggressive toward everything; others are very relaxed), the tank size (10 gallons minimum, larger is better), and the tank mate species.

Fish to Avoid With Bettas

These species should never be housed with bettas, or require very careful monitoring:

  • Other male bettas: guaranteed fighting; do not attempt under any circumstances
  • Guppies: male guppies' colourful, long-flowing tails trigger betta aggression instinctively
  • Fin nippers: tiger barbs, serpae tetras and similar species will relentlessly nip betta fins; long fins make an especially tempting target
  • Gouramis: same family as bettas; males may trigger territorial aggression; generally not recommended
  • Cichlids: most are too aggressive and will bully or injure a betta
  • Goldfish: incompatible temperature requirements — goldfish prefer cooler water
  • Cherry shrimp and other small neocaridina: high risk of being eaten; larger Amano shrimp are safer

Judging Your Own Betta's Temperament

Individual variation is enormous. One betta may coexist peacefully with neon tetras for years; another will attack everything in the tank within minutes. You can get a useful read before buying anything:

  • The mirror test: hold a small mirror to the glass for 15–20 seconds. Brief flaring is normal and healthy. A fish that flares instantly, repeatedly, and stays inflamed for minutes after the mirror is gone is a poor community candidate.
  • Response to movement: a betta that charges the glass every time something passes the tank is running high on territorial drive.
  • History: bettas that have been housed alone for a long time, and older males, tend to be less tolerant of newcomers.

Do not repeat the mirror test often — sustained flaring is stressful. Once is enough to inform the decision.

How to Introduce Tank Mates

  1. Plant heavily first. Dense cover, driftwood and tall plants break lines of sight so the betta cannot patrol the whole tank at once.
  2. Add tank mates first, betta last where possible. A betta introduced into an occupied tank has no established territory to defend, which is far safer than the reverse.
  3. Rearrange the hardscape if the betta is already resident. Moving decor resets the territorial map and reduces the "this is mine" response.
  4. Add the whole group at once, not one fish at a time — a single newcomer absorbs all the attention.
  5. Watch for 48–72 hours. Some chasing on day one is normal; sustained pursuit, nipped fins or a fish hiding permanently is not.

Feed at multiple spots during this period — betta food at the surface, sinking food for bottom dwellers — so the betta cannot monopolise feeding time.

When It Goes Wrong

Have the backup tank running before you introduce anyone. A cycled 5-gallon with a heater is enough, and needing it is common rather than a sign you did something wrong.

Signs that a pairing has failed and needs separating within hours, not days: torn or split fins on either fish, a tank mate wedged permanently behind equipment, a fish that stops eating, or the betta patrolling one corner and refusing to let anything pass. Physical damage escalates quickly — torn fins are an entry point for bacterial infection, and a chased fish will exhaust itself well before it shows obvious injury.

Female Betta Sorority Tanks

Multiple female bettas can sometimes be kept together in a "sorority tank" — but this is an advanced technique that frequently fails, and it is not a beginner project. If attempting a sorority:

  • Use a 20-gallon minimum, heavily planted with dense sight breaks
  • Introduce all females simultaneously — never add a new female to an established group
  • Keep 5–6 females minimum, so aggression is spread across the group rather than focused on one victim
  • Monitor closely for a week; some hierarchical chasing is normal, but any fish being cornered or kept from food must be removed
  • Have a separate tank ready for any fish that cannot coexist

Frequently Asked Questions

Can betta fish live with other fish?

Often, yes — in a 10-gallon or larger planted tank, with small, fast, drab-coloured species, and never with a second male betta. Note that species references such as Seriously Fish consider Betta splendens best kept alone, so treat a community setup as something you manage and monitor rather than assume.

What fish can live with bettas?

Ember tetras, chili rasboras, harlequin rasboras, corydoras and pygmy corydoras, otocinclus, and nerite or mystery snails are the most consistently successful choices. All are small, short-finned, and occupy a different part of the tank than the betta.

Can bettas live with guppies?

Generally no, especially male guppies. Their long, brightly coloured tails read to a betta as a rival male, and the betta will usually attack them. Female guppies are a lower risk but still not a recommended pairing.

Can bettas live with shrimp?

Amano shrimp are usually large enough that most bettas ignore them. Small neocaridina such as cherry shrimp are frequently eaten, though a heavily planted tank with plenty of moss lets a colony breed faster than the betta can hunt.

How big a tank do I need for betta tank mates?

10 gallons is the practical floor, and 15–20 gallons makes almost every pairing easier by giving tank mates room to get out of the way. Snails are the exception and are fine from 5 gallons.

How do I know if my betta will accept tank mates?

Try a single brief mirror test. Short flaring is normal; a fish that flares instantly and stays inflamed for minutes afterwards has high territorial drive and is a poor community candidate. Temperament varies enormously between individuals, so always have a backup tank ready.
