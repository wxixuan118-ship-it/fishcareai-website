How to Set Up a Betta Fish Tank: Size, Filter, Heater & Cycling

📏 5 gal minimum  |  🌡️ 78–80°F  |  🔄 Gentle flow  |  ⏳ Cycle first

A betta needs more than a jar of water and a plastic plant. Seven decisions determine whether the tank stays stable for years or crashes in the first month — size, filtration, heat, substrate, planting, a lid, and cycling — in the order you should make them.

Quick answer: One adult betta needs a 5-gallon minimum (10 is easier to keep stable), a heater holding 78–80°F, a gentle sponge or baffled filter, a secure lid, and a fully cycled biofilter before the fish goes in — budget 4–6 weeks between buying the tank and the fish.

Minimum Tank Size for a Betta

A 5-gallon aquarium is the practical minimum for one adult betta. A 10-gallon tank is the better choice for most keepers: the larger volume dilutes waste more slowly, buffers temperature swings, and gives the fish more horizontal swimming space for little extra maintenance.

For context, Seriously Fish recommends an aquarium with a base footprint of at least 45 × 30 cm (18 × 12 in) for a single male — roughly the footprint of a standard 10-gallon tank. That is a footprint recommendation rather than a volume one — prefer a tank wider than it is tall, since bettas patrol horizontally and must reach the surface for every breath.

Why tank size matters: the cups and bowls bettas are sold in are temporary holding containers, not homes. In that little water, ammonia accumulates within hours, temperature tracks the room, and the fish cannot patrol a territory or explore cover.

Filtration: Gentle Flow Is Essential

Bettas are native to slow-moving rice paddies and shallow streams — they are not built for current. A filter that pushes the fish around the tank exhausts it, damages its fins, and causes chronic stress. The goal is biological filtration with minimal turbulence.

  • Sponge filter: the best choice for betta tanks — very gentle flow, provides biological filtration, safe for long fins; air pump required
  • Hang-on-back (HOB) filter: fine with a pre-filter sponge on the intake and the outflow baffled with a spray bar, plastic bottle, or filter floss
  • Internal power filter: usually too strong; reduce flow to minimum or baffle the outflow
  • No filter: not viable long-term without near-daily large water changes

Testing the flow: a betta should hold position anywhere in the tank without visibly working. If it parks in one low-flow corner, swims with its fins pinned back, or gets pushed sideways when it stops, baffle the outflow — a plastic bottle cut to fit over an HOB return spreads the water across the surface instead of driving it down. If a betta starts clamping its fins after a filter change, flow is the first thing to check.

Heating: Bettas Need Stable Warmth

Bettas are tropical fish and need a heater in almost every home — including rooms that feel warm to you. Air and water temperature are not the same thing, and an aquarium loses heat continuously through evaporation.

Cold water is the most common betta care mistake. A betta kept at 68–72°F will become lethargic, stop eating, develop a weakened immune system, and become vulnerable to every disease in the aquarium. Warmth is not optional.

Rule of thumb: about 5 watts per gallon in a room near 70°F, and 7–8 watts per gallon for a cold room or a tank on an exterior wall. Full heater sizing and failure recovery is covered in the betta temperature and water quality guide.

Substrate and Decor

Bettas spend most of their time in the middle and upper water column, so substrate is mostly a matter of looks and cleaning:

  • Fine sand: natural look; easy to vacuum; some bettas enjoy resting on it
  • Small rounded gravel: widely available; easy to vacuum between grains
  • Bare bottom: easiest to clean; practical for hospital tanks

All decorations must be smooth with no sharp edges. Run pantyhose over every surface before adding it — if the nylon snags, the decoration will shred fins, and torn fins are an open door for infection. Avoid rough ceramic, jagged plastic plants, sharp slate, and ornaments with holes the fish can enter but not turn around in.

Live vs Artificial Plants

Bettas feel exposed in bare tanks. Live plants absorb nitrate, break lines of sight, and give the fish leaves to rest on near the surface:

  • Java fern (Microsorum pteropus) — hardy, low-light, attach to driftwood
  • Anubias — very hardy, broad leaves that bettas love to rest on; attach to hardscape
  • Amazon sword — beautiful centrepiece plant for larger tanks
  • Water sprite — floating or rooted; excellent surface cover
  • Marimo moss balls — low maintenance; bettas often interact with them

If using artificial plants, choose soft silk only — never stiff plastic with pointed tips.

Lid: Bettas Jump

A secure lid is essential. Betta fish jump — often at night or when startled. Even a 2-inch gap around equipment cables is enough for a betta to escape. Most standard aquarium lids leave a gap at the back for filter and heater cables; cover this gap with cut pieces of plastic mesh or foam. Check under the lid every morning.

Bettas breathe atmospheric air through a labyrinth organ,; leave roughly an inch of warm air between the water and the lid so the fish is not breathing a cold draft.

Cycling the Aquarium

Cycling means colonising the filter with bacteria that convert toxic ammonia into nitrite and then into relatively safe nitrate. An uncycled tank can reach lethal ammonia within 24 hours of the fish going in.

The Merck Veterinary Manual notes a tropical biofilter can take up to 8 weeks to establish, and describes fishless cycling as dosing ammonia to 1–5 mg/L. That timeline is the single most common reason new betta tanks fail: the fish is bought the same day as the tank.

  • Fishless cycle: dose ammonia (drops, fish food, or raw shrimp) before the fish; wait 4–6 weeks until ammonia and nitrite both read 0 ppm
  • Seeded cycle: filter media, gravel or a sponge from a cycled tank speeds this up dramatically
  • Bottled bacteria: Seachem Stability or Fritz Zyme 7 can establish a cycle faster

What "cycled" actually looks like on a test kit

The tank is cycled when you can add ammonia and, 24 hours later, read 0 ppm ammonia and 0 ppm nitrite with measurable nitrate. Merck's reference ranges for freshwater ornamental aquaria list ammonia and nitrite at 0 mg/L, nitrate at <20 mg/L and chlorine at 0 mg/L. Repeat the test two days running — one low reading is not proof.

Test kit: a liquid master test kit — not strips — is the reliable way to monitor ammonia, nitrite and nitrate during and after cycling. Test weekly once the tank is established.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum tank size for a betta fish?

5 gallons is the practical minimum for one adult betta. A 10-gallon tank is easier to keep stable and is recommended for most keepers, and its footprint matches the 45 × 30 cm base that Seriously Fish suggests for a single male.

Do I need to cycle a betta tank before adding the fish?

Yes. Without an established biofilter, ammonia from the fish's own waste can reach harmful levels within a day. Merck notes a tropical biofilter can take up to 8 weeks to establish; plan 4–6 weeks for a fishless cycle, or days if you seed the filter from an established tank.

What filter is best for a betta tank?

A sponge filter is ideal for betta tanks — it provides biological filtration with very gentle flow. Bettas are native to slow-moving water and are stressed by strong currents. A baffled hang-on-back filter with a pre-filter sponge also works.

Do bettas need live plants?

Live plants are not required but are highly beneficial — they absorb nitrates, provide hiding spots, and give bettas surfaces to rest on near the surface. Java fern and Anubias are easy beginner choices; if you prefer artificial plants, use soft silk only.

Does a betta tank need a lid?

Yes. Bettas jump, particularly at night or when startled, and a gap of a couple of inches around cables is enough to escape through. Cover cable cut-outs with mesh or foam, and leave about an inch of warm air above the water for breathing.
