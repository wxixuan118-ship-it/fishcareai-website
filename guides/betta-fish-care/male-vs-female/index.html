Betta Fish Male vs Female: How to Tell Them Apart

♂ Larger beard  |  ♀ Egg spot  |  🔍 Reliable from ~10–12 weeks

Sexing a betta is easy once the fish is mature and long-finned, and genuinely difficult with a short-finned plakat or a young fish in a cup. This guide covers the three cues that actually hold up — fin length, the egg spot and the beard — which ones to ignore, and whether males and females need different care.

Quick answer: In an adult betta, check three things together: fin length, the egg spot (a small white dot between the ventral fins, on females only), and the beard under the gill covers (large and visible at rest on males, small on females). Fin length alone is unreliable — short-finned plakat males are routinely mislabelled as females in shops.

Male vs Female Betta at a Glance

No single trait sexes a betta with certainty. The table below ranks each cue by how much weight it deserves, which matters more than the list of differences itself — most of the traits people rely on are the weak ones.

Seriously Fish summarises the wild-type dimorphism simply: males are more colourful and develop more extended fins than females, and ornamental breeding has pushed that difference to extremes. The important word is extremes — decades of selective breeding have also produced short-finned males and vividly coloured females, which is exactly why the classic cues fail on modern shop fish.

The Beard: The Cue That Works on Every Betta

The "beard" is the opercular membrane, a flap of tissue tucked beneath the gill covers. Both sexes have one, but the male's is far larger. Look at the fish side-on at rest: on a male you can usually see a dark crescent protruding slightly past the edge of the gill plate, like a shirt collar sticking out. On a female the membrane is small and normally hidden until she flares.

This is the cue to reach for first, because it is the only reliable one that does not depend on fin length or on the fish being fully mature. If you want to confirm, trigger a brief flare — hold a finger near the glass or angle a phone screen at the tank for a few seconds. A flaring male fans out a conspicuous dark beard; a flaring female shows a much thinner rim of membrane.

Keep flaring short. A few seconds is enough to check. Leaving a mirror or a rival in view for minutes at a time is a stress response, not entertainment — see the daily routine section of our betta fish care guide.

The Egg Spot

The egg spot is the ovipositor — the external opening the female releases eggs through. It sits on the underside of the fish, between the base of the ventral fins and the front of the anal fin, and looks like a single small grain of white or cream. It typically becomes visible from around 9–12 weeks of age.

Three caveats stop this from being a standalone test:

  • Angle matters. The spot is on the belly, not the flank. You need the fish side-on or slightly below, with light on it — it disappears entirely from a head-on view.
  • Some males show a white speck. Young males occasionally have a small pale mark in roughly the same place. If a "spot" is present but the fish also has a heavy beard, trust the beard.
  • Dark and heavily scaled strains hide it. On black, dragon-scale and copper fish the spot can be genuinely invisible.

A visible egg spot combined with a small beard is close to conclusive. A missing egg spot on its own proves nothing.

Fins: Obvious, and Frequently Misleading

On the fish most people meet — a veiltail in a shop cup — fin length settles the question immediately. Males carry a long trailing caudal fin, a tall dorsal, an anal fin that sweeps back well past the body, and long thick ventral fins. Females have a short, roughly trapezoidal anal fin, a smaller dorsal, and short ventrals.

The trap is that fin length is a strain trait as much as a sex trait. A plakat male is short-finned by design, and a giant or wild-type female can carry more finnage than expected. Shops compound this by selling short-finned males from "assorted female" tanks. Whenever fin length says female, confirm with the beard before you act on it — particularly before adding the fish to a tank that already holds bettas.

Reading the anal fin

If you can only see the fish from one side, the anal fin is the most informative single fin. A male's tapers to a point or a long trailing tip; a female's ends bluntly, with a fairly straight lower edge. This holds up better on plakats than caudal fin shape does, though it is still weaker than the beard.

Body Shape, Size and Colour

Female bettas are usually shorter and deeper through the belly, with a rounder profile that becomes pronounced when they are carrying eggs. Males run longer and more slender, and look considerably bigger than they are because of the finnage. Wild Betta splendens reaches about 70 mm (2.8 in) standard length; ornamental strains sit broadly in that range, with giants going larger.

Colour is the cue to distrust most. It is true as a generalisation and false often enough to be useless in a shop: breeders now produce females in the same koi, dragon-scale and copper lines as males, and a well-conditioned female can easily outshine a stressed male in the next cup. Judge sex on structure — beard, egg spot, fins — and treat colour as background information.

Stripes: horizontal versus vertical

These get confused constantly, and they mean opposite things:

  • Vertical pale bars across the body of a female signal breeding readiness. They are a sex-linked display and a normal, healthy sign.
  • Horizontal dark stripes running the length of the body are stress stripes. Either sex can show them, and they point at water quality, temperature or a bullying tank mate — not at sex.

Behavioural Differences

Behaviour is supporting evidence rather than proof, but two patterns are worth knowing.

Bubble nests. Betta splendens is a bubble nester: the male builds a raft of saliva-coated bubbles at the surface and tends it, whether or not a female is present. A nest in the tank is strong evidence of a male. It is not absolute — females occasionally build small, scrappy nests — and the absence of a nest means nothing at all, since plenty of healthy males never build one in a bare or high-flow tank.

Aggression. Males will not tolerate other males and generally cannot be housed with females outside a supervised spawning. Females are also territorial with each other; the difference is that under specific conditions a group can be made to work, where a group of males cannot. Neither sex is reliably peaceful, so do not read a calm fish as female.

Plakats, Juveniles and Other Hard Cases

  • Plakats and halfmoon plakats. Short-finned by breeding, and the single most common source of mis-sexed bettas. Go straight to the beard, then the egg spot.
  • Juveniles under about 10–12 weeks. Fins have not differentiated and the ovipositor is not yet visible. Sexing is guesswork at this stage, which is why mixed juvenile tanks routinely contain both sexes.
  • Dumbo / elephant-ear bettas. The enlarged pectoral fins are a strain trait present in both sexes and tell you nothing about sex.
  • Giant and king bettas. The same rules apply on a larger fish; the beard is easier to see, if anything.
  • Wild species. In B. imbellis, B. smaragdina and the mouthbrooding species, differences are subtler — males show more iridescence and slightly longer fins, females are plainer and rounder. See our guide to betta types and wild species.

Do Males and Females Need Different Care?

Almost everything is identical. Both sexes are tropical carnivores with the same tank, temperature and diet requirements — the only real divergence is housing.

Seriously Fish notes that standard practice is to keep males and females apart except for breeding, and that applies to a home aquarium as much as a breeding facility. A "pair" is not a housing arrangement; it is a temporary, supervised spawning setup.

About sororities

A female group — a "sorority" — is an advanced setup, not a beginner shortcut to keeping several bettas. It needs 20 gallons or more, dense planting with broken sightlines, five or six females introduced at the same time, and a spare tank ready before you start. Groups fail suddenly and often, sometimes months in, and the usual outcome is shredded fins or a dead fish. Our betta tank mates guide covers the conditions in detail.

Which Should You Keep?

Neither sex is easier to keep alive — the difference is what you want from the fish.

  • Choose a male for the display finnage and the simplest possible housing decision: one fish, one tank, no compatibility question to get wrong.
  • Choose a female for a faster, more active fish with far less fin damage risk, and slightly better odds in a well-planned community tank.
  • Choose a plakat male if you want a male without the fin-management problems — short finnage means less drag, less tearing and less fin biting.

What should not drive the decision is the idea that females are lower-maintenance because they are cheaper and plainer. A female in an unheated bowl dies exactly as fast as a male in one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can you tell if a betta fish is male or female?

Check three traits together: the beard under the gill covers (large and visible at rest on males), the egg spot between the ventral fins (present only on females), and fin length (long on males, short on females). The beard is the most reliable because it works on short-finned plakats, where fin length gives the wrong answer.

What is the egg spot on a betta fish?

It is the ovipositor — the opening the female releases eggs through. It appears as a small white dot on the underside, between the ventral fins and the anal fin, and is usually visible from around 9–12 weeks of age. Dark or dragon-scale strains can hide it completely, so a missing egg spot does not prove a fish is male.

Can female betta fish build bubble nests?

Occasionally, yes. Nest building is overwhelmingly male behaviour and a well-built nest is strong evidence of a male, but females do sometimes produce small ones. Treat a nest as supporting evidence rather than proof, and confirm with the beard.

Can male and female bettas live together?

No, not as a permanent arrangement. Standard practice is to house males and females separately except during a supervised spawning, after which the female is removed. A male and female left together will usually result in the female being harassed or killed.

Are female bettas less colourful than males?

As a wild-type generalisation yes, but it is unreliable for shop fish. Selective breeding has produced females in the same koi, dragon-scale and copper lines as males, and colour intensity also varies with stress, temperature and conditioning. Use structural cues instead.

At what age can you sex a betta fish?

Reliably from about 10–12 weeks, once fins have differentiated and the ovipositor is visible. Younger juveniles cannot be sexed with confidence, which is why mixed grow-out tanks and cheap "female" cups often contain both sexes.
