Discus Fish Tank Size Guide

By FishCare AI Editorial TeamEditorial reviewUpdated 2026-08-13

How Much Space Does a Discus Fish Need?

Each adult discus fish needs approximately 10–15 gallons of well-maintained water. Because discus must be kept in groups of at least 6 for stable social dynamics, the minimum tank size is driven by the group requirement — not by any single fish.

Adult discus reach 5–7 inches (13–18 cm) in both height and length, and they are nearly as tall as they are long. This body shape requires both horizontal swimming space and vertical clearance. Tank length, depth, and filtration capacity all matter as much as total gallon count.

Why 55 Gallons Is a Minimum, Not a Comfort Zone

A 55-gallon tank for six discus works — but only with diligent, experienced management. The water volume is constrained relative to the bioload of six large, protein-fed fish at 84°F. Temperature fluctuations have a bigger impact in smaller volumes, ammonia spikes are faster, and the margin for maintenance delays shrinks.

Experienced discus keepers consistently report that the jump from 55 to 75 gallons makes tank management noticeably more stable and forgiving. If you can afford the space and cost, start with 75 gallons or more. The difference in daily maintenance effort is significant.

Don't plan around juvenile size. A 2-inch juvenile discus is sold from a tank that looks oversized for it, but that fish will be 6 inches wide and 6 inches tall in 12–18 months. Plan for adult dimensions from the start.

Tank Dimensions That Matter

Two tanks with the same gallon rating can have very different utility for discus. Key dimensions:

  • Length: More length gives more horizontal swimming room and allows fish to spread out
  • Height: Discus are tall-bodied; a tank at least 18–20 inches tall is preferable
  • Front-to-back depth: More depth creates territory and visual breaks for subordinate fish

Filtration Sizing

Discus produce a high protein waste load on their typical diet of bloodworms, brine shrimp, and beef heart paste. Filter your discus tank at two to three times the tank volume per hour at a minimum. A 75-gallon tank should have a filter rated for 150–225 gallons per hour (GPH) flow.

A canister filter provides excellent mechanical and biological filtration with low flow resistance. Adding a sponge filter or secondary filter gives you backup biological capacity and useful surface movement — important in warm, oxygen-depleted water.

Frequently Asked Questions

A 55-gallon tank is the constrained minimum for a carefully managed small group of adult discus. A 75-gallon is the practical recommended starting point for about six fish. Do not size the tank around juveniles — plan for their adult size of 5–7 inches.
A 40-gallon breeder may work as a temporary grow-out tank for juvenile discus, but it is not an appropriate permanent home for adults. Adults produce significant waste and need stable water chemistry that a smaller tank cannot maintain under normal feeding schedules.
Six to eight adult discus is a reasonable number for a 75-gallon tank with strong filtration and consistent water changes. Do not push beyond eight without a well-established system and a realistic upgrade plan.
Discus benefit from tank height because they are a tall, laterally compressed species. A standard 55-gallon (48×13×21 inches) gives better usable swimming height than a long, shallow 55-gallon. Breeder-style tanks with extra depth front-to-back are also well-suited.
