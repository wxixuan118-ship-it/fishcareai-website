What Do Discus Fish Eat? Complete Feeding Guide

By FishCare AI Editorial TeamEditorial reviewUpdated 2026-08-13

Discus Diet in the Wild

Wild discus in the Amazon basin are opportunistic carnivores. They feed on small invertebrates, insect larvae, crustaceans, worms, and other small organisms that fall into or live in slow-moving blackwater rivers. Their diet is high in protein with minimal plant material.

This wild diet is the model for captive feeding: high protein, variety, and frequent small meals. Aquarium foods that closely replicate the protein profile and nutrient density of the natural diet produce the best coloration, growth, and health in captive discus.

Best Foods for Discus Fish

Frozen Bloodworms

Frozen bloodworms (larvae of Chironomus midges) are a staple discus food and one of the most eagerly accepted foods. They provide excellent protein and are available from any aquarium shop. Feed from a feeding cone or thaw first and add with a feeding ring to keep them concentrated. Do not overfeed — bloodworms can cause constipation if they are the only food provided long-term.

Frozen Brine Shrimp

Frozen adult brine shrimp are highly nutritious and accepted readily by almost all discus. They provide a good amino acid profile and are easy to digest. Frozen brine shrimp nauplii (baby brine shrimp) are useful for smaller or more finicky individuals. Use as part of a varied diet alongside bloodworms.

Beef Heart Paste

Beef heart paste is a classic discus food with a long track record among breeders. Prepared correctly, it provides exceptional protein density and supports rapid growth and vivid coloration. Commercial beef heart paste is available ready-made, or experienced keepers blend raw beef heart with vitamins, garlic, spirulina, and other supplements to create a custom mix. It is messy and requires immediate removal of uneaten portions to protect water quality.

High-Quality Discus Pellets and Granules

Commercial discus pellets formulated with high protein content (45%+ crude protein) provide a convenient, consistent daily food. Brands specifically formulated for discus are preferable to generic cichlid pellets. Pellets reduce the waste load compared to wet foods and are easier to portion control. Transition new fish to pellets gradually; mix with bloodworms initially if they are reluctant.

Other Foods

  • Frozen mysis shrimp: Highly nutritious; excellent alternative to brine shrimp
  • Frozen daphnia: Good for variety; acts as a mild digestive aid
  • Freeze-dried foods: Acceptable as occasional treats but inferior to frozen; more likely to cause bloating
  • Live black worms: Excellent for conditioning discus before breeding; high risk of introducing disease — source carefully

Feeding Frequency and Portions

Feed discus three to five small meals per day. This is significantly more frequent than most other aquarium fish. The reason is that discus have relatively small stomachs and are active throughout the day in their natural environment — small, frequent meals match their physiology better than two large feedings.

Practical feeding schedule:
Morning: bloodworms or brine shrimp (small portion)
Midday: pellets (if you can be home)
Afternoon: beef heart paste or frozen food
Evening: pellets or brine shrimp
Night: skip — do not feed just before lights-out in a warm tank

Give only what can be consumed in 3–5 minutes. Remove all uneaten food promptly. Discus waste is already high on a protein diet; rotting food in a 84°F tank will spike ammonia within hours.

Common Feeding Mistakes

Feeding too little, too rarely

Discus fed once or twice a day in small portions often appear thin, have subdued coloration, and grow slowly. Three to five feedings is not excessive — it matches their natural feeding pattern.

Not removing uneaten food

A 5-minute rule is standard for all discus feeding: if food is not eaten in 5 minutes, remove it. Use a turkey baster or gravel vac to pull out leftovers. This single habit prevents more water quality problems than any filter upgrade.

Only feeding one food type

Discus fed exclusively bloodworms often develop nutritional imbalances and can become conditioned to refuse other foods. Rotate through at least three different food types to ensure complete nutrition and dietary variety.

Feeding immediately after large water changes

Discus often stop eating for 30–60 minutes after a large water change as they adjust. This is normal — wait for them to settle before offering food. Uneaten food from an immediate post-change feeding is especially wasteful.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frozen bloodworms and frozen brine shrimp are excellent staple foods for discus. Beef heart paste is a traditional and highly effective food for growth and color. High-quality discus-specific pellets or granules round out the diet and are convenient for daily feeding.
Three to five small meals daily is the standard recommendation. Discus have small stomachs relative to their size and benefit from frequent small feedings rather than one or two large ones. Feed only what they can consume in 3–5 minutes and remove uneaten food immediately.
Discus can eat flake food but it should not be the staple. Most flakes do not meet discus protein requirements and can cause digestive issues. Use high-quality discus pellets or granules as the prepared food base, supplemented with frozen or live foods.
Discus are primarily carnivorous and do not need plant material in their diet. Occasional blanched vegetables are fine as variety, but should not replace protein-rich foods. Focus on high-quality protein sources for best coloration, growth, and health.
