New Tank Syndrome: What to Do Today, Which Readings Are Dangerous and How Long It Lasts

🚨 Act at 0.5 ppm ammonia or nitrite  |  ⏳ 2–6 weeks to recover  |  ✅ Over after 7 days at 0 and 0

Updated November 21, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

New tank syndrome is ammonia and nitrite poisoning in a tank whose filter hasn't grown enough bacteria yet. It happens when fish go into a new tank too soon or too many at once, and it can also hit an old tank after the filter media is replaced or a medicine wipes out the bacteria. The fix is to keep the fish alive while the filter catches up: test every day, and change enough water to keep ammonia and nitrite at or under 0.5 ppm combined. Expect it to take 2 to 6 weeks. It is over when ammonia and nitrite read 0 for 7 days in a row with normal feeding.

Do this today

  1. Test ammonia, nitrite and pH. Write them down.
  2. Change 50% of the water if ammonia or nitrite is 0.5 ppm or more. Dechlorinate and match the temperature. Over 2 ppm? Do a second 50% change a few hours later.
  3. Stop feeding for a day, then feed a small amount every other day.
  4. Leave the filter alone. Don't rinse, replace or switch it off.
  5. Add an air stone and, if you have it, mature filter media from a healthy tank or a bottled nitrifying bacteria product.
  6. Don't add more fish until it is over.

New Tank Syndrome Symptoms

  • Fish gasping at the surface or hanging near the filter outlet
  • Fast gill movement, red or inflamed gills
  • Lethargy, sitting on the bottom, clamped fins
  • Loss of appetite
  • Darkened or blotchy color, red streaks in the fins
  • Fish dying one or two at a time in the first few weeks of a new tank
  • Often, cloudy white water (a bacterial bloom) at the same time

These signs overlap with many diseases, so the test kit makes the diagnosis. Strips are fine in an emergency, but a liquid kit is more accurate at the low readings that matter here.

How Much Ammonia and Nitrite Is Dangerous?

ReadingAmmonia (total)NitriteAction
Safe0 ppm0 ppmTest every 2–3 days while the tank is new.
Watch0.25 ppm0.25 ppmFeed less, retest tomorrow.
Act0.5–1 ppm0.5–1 ppm50% water change today.
Emergency2 ppm or more2 ppm or moreTwo 50% changes a few hours apart, then daily.

UF/IFAS reports nitrite to be toxic to fish at levels as low as 0.10 mg/L, so any measurable nitrite is worth acting on. Ammonia is more complicated: test kits measure total ammonia, but the harmful part is the un-ionized form, NH3, which UF/IFAS describes as about 100 times more toxic than the ionized form. How much of the reading is NH3 depends on pH and temperature:

pH (at 77°F / 25°C)Share of total ammonia that is toxic NH3What 1 ppm on the kit means
6.5about 0.2%Low risk in the short term
7.0about 0.6%Moderate
7.5about 1.8%Harmful within days
8.0about 5.4%Dangerous
8.5about 15%Very dangerous

Two practical points follow. First, a tank with hard, alkaline water (pH 8 and up, common for African cichlids and livebearers) needs faster action at the same reading. Second, don't raise pH while ammonia is present: low pH is protecting the fish. Keep it stable and let water changes do the work.

WhenWhat to doWhat to expect
Day 1Test. Water change(s) to get ammonia + nitrite under 0.5 ppm. No food today. Add air stone, seeded media or bottled bacteria.Fish breathe more easily within hours.
Days 2–7Test every morning. Change 25–50% whenever ammonia or nitrite reaches 0.5 ppm. Feed a little every other day.Ammonia keeps appearing. Many tanks need a change every 1–2 days.
Day 7 · checkIs ammonia rising more slowly between tests? Is nitrite showing up?Nitrite appearing is good news: the first bacteria are working.
Weeks 2–3Keep testing daily. Nitrite now needs the water changes. Optional: salt for nitrite (below).Ammonia falls to 0. Nitrite peaks, then falls. Nitrate starts to rise.
Day 14 and 21 · checkAre you changing water less often to stay under 0.5 ppm?Most tanks need fewer changes each week from here.

How much water to change: a change removes the same share of ammonia and nitrite as the share of water you replace. To get from 2 ppm to 0.5 ppm you need to remove three quarters, which is two 50% changes a few hours apart (2 → 1 → 0.5). A single 75% change works too in a new tank if the new water matches temperature and is dechlorinated.

    Water changesEssentialThe only thing that removes ammonia and nitrite immediately.

    Salt (chloride) for nitriteHelps with nitriteChloride blocks nitrite uptake through the gills. See below.
    Cleaning or replacing filter mediaHarmfulThrows away the bacteria you are waiting for.
    MedicationUsually harmfulMany medicines, especially antibiotics, set the bacteria back. Fix the water first.

    Using salt against nitrite

    Fish take nitrite in through the gills by mistake for chloride, so extra chloride in the water protects them. The SRAC fact sheet on nitrite recommends at least 10 parts chloride for every part nitrite in catfish ponds. For a tropical tank with salt-tolerant fish, about 1 gram of aquarium salt (sodium chloride, no additives) per litre, roughly 3.8 g per US gallon, gives a wide margin over any nitrite reading you are likely to see. Dissolve it first and add it over a few hours. Replace only the salt you remove in water changes. Skip it for corydoras, loaches, many tetras, live plants and shrimp, which tolerate salt poorly.

    New Tank Syndrome in an Established Tank

    The same thing can happen in a tank that has been running for years if the bacteria are lost. Common triggers: replacing all the filter media at once, rinsing it in chlorinated tap water, a power cut that leaves the filter off for several hours, an antibiotic treatment, or adding a big group of fish at once. Treat it the same way. To prevent it, rinse media only in old tank water, replace no more than half of it at a time, and add new fish in small groups.

    How to Prevent It Next Time

    Cycle the tank before fish go in. A fishless cycle with ammonia takes about 4–8 weeks, or 1–2 weeks with seeded media. See how to cycle a fish tank for the method and doses, and use the cycle tracker to check whether a tank is ready. Then add about a third of your planned fish at a time, a week or two apart, testing between each group.

    New Tank Syndrome FAQ

    How long does new tank syndrome last?

    Usually 2 to 6 weeks, until the filter grows enough bacteria to process the fish's waste. Mature filter media from a healthy tank can shorten it to days. It is over when ammonia and nitrite read 0 for 7 days in a row.

    Can fish survive new tank syndrome?

    Yes, if ammonia and nitrite are kept low with water changes while the filter matures. Most losses happen when nobody is testing and readings stay high for days.

    How much water should I change for new tank syndrome?

    Enough to keep ammonia and nitrite at or under 0.5 ppm combined. A 50% change halves the reading. From 2 ppm, two 50% changes a few hours apart bring it to 0.5 ppm.

    Will water changes slow down the cycle?

    Not meaningfully. The bacteria live on the filter media and surfaces, not in the water. Keeping ammonia under 0.5 ppm still leaves them plenty to feed on.

    Does bottled bacteria fix new tank syndrome?

    It can help the filter establish sooner, but results vary by product. Keep testing and changing water until the readings stay at 0.

    Can new tank syndrome happen in an old tank?

    Yes. Replacing all the filter media, rinsing it in tap water, a long power cut or an antibiotic treatment can wipe out the bacteria and cause the same ammonia and nitrite spike.

    Should I feed my fish during new tank syndrome?

    Feed lightly: skip the first day, then a small amount every other day. Less food means less ammonia between water changes.
