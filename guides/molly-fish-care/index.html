Molly Fish Care Guide: Varieties, Tank Setup and Breeding

By FishCare AI Editorial TeamEditorial reviewUpdated 2026-07-2310 min read

Mollies need a 20-gallon or larger cycled aquarium, 72–82°F water, a pH around 7.5–8.5, and measurable mineral hardness. Keep a small social group with two to three females per male, feed a plant-rich omnivore diet, and plan for fry before mixing sexes. Black, dalmatian, balloon, lyretail, and sailfin mollies share these fundamentals, although sailfins need more swimming room and balloon mollies can be less robust.

Quick care note: Test water before guessing. Ammonia and nitrite should always be 0 ppm, and oxygen problems need fast action. Soft or acidic water is the most common cause of molly health problems.

Molly Fish Varieties

There are dozens of molly varieties available in the aquarium hobby. Each shares the same basic care requirements, but size and fin shape differ between types.

Black Molly: The most common variety. Solid jet-black coloration, short fins, and a calm temperament. Hardy and well-suited to beginners. Black molly fish care is identical to other mollies — stable hard water, varied diet, and group housing.

Dalmatian Molly: White body with irregular black spots, resembling a dalmatian dog. Short-finned and active. Can be slightly more sensitive to water quality than black mollies.

Sailfin Molly (Poecilia latipinna): Males develop an impressive large dorsal fin. Sailfin mollies need a longer tank (at least 30 gallons) to accommodate their fin and swimming behavior. They are also more sensitive to water quality than short-finned varieties.

Balloon Molly: A selectively bred variety with a rounded, compressed body. Visually distinctive but physically more delicate. Balloon mollies can be more prone to swim bladder issues and are not recommended as a first molly for new fishkeepers.

Lyretail Molly: Features a forked caudal fin. Available in black, gold, and dalmatian colorations. Care is the same as standard mollies.

Gold Dust Molly and Creamsicle Molly: Bright color morphs with yellow, orange, and white patterning. Good for adding color to a community tank with other hard-water species.

Tank Size and Setup

A 20-gallon tank is the minimum for a small group of short-finned mollies, but 30 gallons is more comfortable and reduces territorial tension between males. Sailfin mollies need a 30-gallon minimum because of their size and active swimming.

Mollies do best in a planted tank with open swimming space in the middle. Floating plants like hornwort or water sprite give cover to pregnant females and fry without blocking surface oxygen exchange. A secure lid is important because mollies are surface-active and can jump.

Use a hang-on-back or sponge filter rated for the tank size. Mollies tolerate moderate to strong flow, but avoid creating constant strong currents that exhaust the fish. Substrate can be plain gravel or sand — mollies are not substrate-sensitive, though they will occasionally sift sand.

Water Conditions

Water quality is the single most important factor in molly fish care. Mollies thrive in hard, alkaline water with a pH between 7.5 and 8.5 and hardness between 15 and 30 dGH. Many fishkeepers fail with mollies simply because their tap water is too soft.

If your tap water is soft (below 10 dGH), confirm both GH and KH before changing it. Crushed coral or a measured freshwater remineralizer can raise mineral content gradually. Aquarium salt changes salinity but does not replace the calcium and magnesium measured by GH, so it should not be treated as the default fix for soft water. Avoid salt when tank mates, plants, or invertebrates are salt-sensitive.

Keep ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm at all times. Nitrate should stay under 20 ppm — achievable with 25–30% weekly water changes. Temperature should remain stable between 72°F and 82°F. Mollies tolerate a wide range but suffer when temperature fluctuates more than 2–3°F in a short period.

Feeding Molly Fish

Mollies are omnivores that naturally graze on algae, plant matter, and small invertebrates. In a home aquarium, offer a varied diet to keep them healthy and colorful.

Good staple foods include high-quality tropical flakes or micro pellets with a plant-based ingredient list. Supplement with blanched vegetables such as zucchini, spinach, or cucumber two or three times per week. Mollies eagerly eat spirulina-based flakes, which support their plant-grazing instincts and enhance color.

Protein treats like frozen or live brine shrimp or daphnia can be offered one or two times per week. Feed small amounts two or three times daily rather than one large feeding. Remove uneaten food after three to five minutes to protect water quality.

Breeding Molly Fish

Mollies are livebearers — females carry fully developed fry and give birth to live young rather than laying eggs. A female can give birth every four to six weeks, producing 20–100 fry per batch depending on her size and age.

To encourage breeding, maintain a ratio of at least two to three females per male. Too many males stress females through constant chasing and harassment. Males are identified by their gonopodium — a modified anal fin used for internal fertilization.

A pregnant female develops a dark gravid spot near the anal fin and becomes noticeably rounder over the course of a few weeks. When birth is close, she may become reclusive and hover near the bottom or in plants. Move her to a breeder box or a separate fry tank a few days before birth, or provide heavy plant cover so fry can hide from adult fish.

Fry are born fully formed and immediately free-swimming. Feed them crushed flakes or commercially prepared fry food four to five times daily. They grow quickly and can be introduced to the main tank once they are large enough not to fit in the mouths of adult tankmates — typically at six to eight weeks.

Tank Mates for Molly Fish

Mollies usually settle well into calm community aquariums when every species shares similar water requirements. Platies and swordtails are natural companions because they also prefer harder, alkaline water. Bristlenose plecos and larger peaceful tetras, including Congo or emperor tetras, may also work in a suitably sized tank. Some corydoras catfish can live alongside mollies, but check the needs of the exact species first because many corydoras prefer softer water than mollies.

Avoid tiger barbs and other persistent fin nippers, as well as tiny fish that may be pushed away from food by active mollies. Male mollies—especially sailfin varieties—can chase one another or repeatedly pursue females. Keeping one male with two or three females, plus plants and visual barriers, spreads that attention and gives less assertive fish room to retreat.

Common Health Problems

Shimmy Disease: Mollies that rock or shimmy in place without making forward progress are showing a classic stress response, almost always linked to poor water quality — especially low hardness, cold water, or elevated ammonia. Correct the water first before treating for any disease.

Velvet (Oodinium): A parasitic infection producing a golden or rust-colored dust on the body. Treat with copper-based medication and raise water temperature to 82°F. Reduce light intensity during treatment.

Fin Rot: Ragged or eroding fin edges indicate bacterial infection, usually caused by poor water quality. Improve water conditions first. In severe cases, treat with an antibiotic such as kanamycin or a broad-spectrum bacterial treatment.

Ich (White Spot): White granules resembling salt on the body and fins. Treat with ich medication, gradually raise temperature to 86°F, and increase aeration since warmer water holds less oxygen.

Daily Molly Care Routine

Watch the group during feeding to make sure dominant males are not bullying weaker fish. Healthy mollies are alert, active, and quick to eat, but constant chasing, clamped fins, shimmying, or hiding can point to stress or water quality problems. Check temperature and surface movement daily because livebearers do poorly when oxygen and mineral balance are unstable.

Perform 25–30% water changes every week. Gravel-vacuum the substrate to remove waste, and rinse filter media in old tank water (never tap water) when flow slows. Replace filter media only when it falls apart — beneficial bacteria live in the media, and replacing it all at once can crash a cycled tank.

Natural Habitat

Wild mollies inhabit coastal lagoons, estuaries, mangrove swamps, and slow-moving freshwater streams from Mexico through Central America to Colombia. Some populations encounter brackish water, while others live in freshwater. They are surface-oriented fish that graze on algae and biofilm on plant surfaces and rocks.

Do Molly Fish Need Aquarium Salt?

Not routinely. Mollies can tolerate—and some populations naturally inhabit—brackish water, but a properly cycled freshwater aquarium with suitable GH, KH, pH, and stable temperature is appropriate for most captive mollies. Salt may be used for a deliberately planned brackish setup or a specific short-term treatment, but it should be measured rather than added automatically. It does not correct low GH, and it can harm salt-sensitive tank mates, plants, and invertebrates. When mollies shimmy or decline, test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, temperature, GH, KH, and pH before reaching for salt.

Fish Care FAQ

Are molly fish easy to care for?

Yes, for most beginners — but only if water is hard and alkaline. Mollies kept in soft, acidic water will shimmy, lose color, and become disease-prone despite good care. Check your tap water hardness before buying mollies.

Do mollies breed easily?

Yes. Mollies are prolific livebearers and can produce fry every four to six weeks when males and females are kept together. Plan ahead for what to do with fry — overcrowding degrades water quality quickly.

What is black molly fish care?

Black molly fish care is identical to other molly varieties: stable hard water between pH 7.5 and 8.5, group housing with more females than males, a varied plant-based diet, and weekly water changes.

How many mollies should I keep together?

Keep a minimum of four to six, with at least two females for every male. A group of six in a 30-gallon tank is a comfortable starting point. Mollies are social and show stress when kept alone or in very small groups.

Can mollies live with guppies?

Yes. Guppies and mollies share similar water requirements and are both peaceful livebearers. They make excellent tank mates, though you should maintain appropriate ratios of females to males for each species separately.

Why is my molly shimmying?

Shimmy in mollies almost always indicates a water quality issue — most often low water hardness, cold temperature, or elevated ammonia or nitrite. Test the water immediately and raise hardness if it is below 10 dGH.
