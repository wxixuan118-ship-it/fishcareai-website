How to Cycle a Fish Tank: The Fishless Method Step by Step

🧪 Dose ammonia to 2 ppm  |  ⏳ 4–8 weeks, 1–2 if seeded  |  ✅ Done: 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours after dosing

Updated November 21, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

To cycle a fish tank, you grow the bacteria that turn fish waste into less harmful nitrate before any fish go in. The most reliable way is a fishless cycle: run the filter and heater, add pure ammonia to 2 ppm, and keep feeding it until the tank turns 2 ppm of ammonia into zero ammonia and zero nitrite within 24 hours. That takes about 4 to 8 weeks from scratch, or 1 to 2 weeks if you can put mature filter media from another tank into the filter. This page is the method. If your tank is already going and you want to know which stage it is at, enter your readings in the aquarium cycle tracker.

The short answer

  1. Set up the tank, filter and heater. Dechlorinate the water and set 77–82°F (25–28°C).
  2. Dose pure ammonia to 2 ppm (calculator below). Test 1 hour later and adjust.
  3. Test ammonia and nitrite every 2–3 days. When ammonia falls below about 0.5 ppm, top it back up to 2 ppm.
  4. Around week 2–3, nitrite rises and ammonia starts vanishing within a day or two. Keep dosing.
  5. Done when 2 ppm ammonia reads 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later, two days running.
  6. Change 50–75% of the water to bring nitrate down, then add fish within 1–2 days.

UF/IFAS notes that a new biofilter needs six to eight weeks to build up enough bacteria to control ammonia and nitrite, which is why an unseeded cycle should not be rushed.

Ammonia Dose Calculator for a Fishless Cycle

Actual water, not tank size — usually 10–15% less.

Pure ammonia only: no scent, soap or surfactants.

Household ammonia labels are not always precise, so treat the result as a starting point: add three quarters of it, wait an hour for it to mix, test, then top up. To check a bottle has no surfactants, shake it. Foam that lingers means it has soap in it and should not go in a tank. Bottled ammonium chloride sold for fishless cycling avoids the guesswork, and its label gives drops per gallon.

Why 2 ppm and not 4–5? Older guides dose 4–5 ppm. That builds so much nitrite that it can stall the bacteria that are supposed to remove it, and the cycle drags on for weeks. A 2 ppm dose grows plenty of bacteria for a normally stocked tank.

How to Cycle a Fish Tank Faster

  • Seed it. A handful of mature filter sponge, ceramic rings or gravel from a healthy tank is by far the biggest speed-up. Squeezing an old sponge into the new filter also helps.
  • Keep it warm. Nitrifying bacteria grow faster at 77–86°F (25–30°C) than at room temperature. Bring it back to your fish's range before they go in.
  • Keep pH above 7 and KH above about 3 dKH. The cycle itself uses up carbonate hardness. If pH drops below about 6.5, the bacteria slow down and can stop. A small water change, or a little baking soda dissolved in water and added over a day, puts KH back.
  • Give them surface area and oxygen. Run the filter 24 hours a day and keep the surface moving.
  • Don't sterilize. No dechlorinator overdoses, no antibiotics, no UV sterilizer during the cycle.

After the Cycle: Adding Fish

A cycled filter has enough bacteria for the ammonia you were dosing, not for any number of fish. Add a few hardy fish first, roughly a third of your planned stock, then test ammonia and nitrite every 2–3 days for a week before adding the next group. Never let the tank go more than a few days without an ammonia source between finishing the cycle and adding fish, or the bacteria start to die back. Size your stocking with the stocking calculator, and set up a routine with the water change schedule.

Cycling a Fish Tank FAQ

How long does it take to cycle a fish tank?

Usually 4–8 weeks for a fishless cycle started from scratch. UF/IFAS gives six to eight weeks for a new biofilter. Seeding the filter with mature media from a healthy tank can cut it to 1–2 weeks.

How much ammonia do I add to cycle a tank?

Enough to read 2 ppm on your test kit, then top back up to 2 ppm whenever it falls below about 0.5 ppm. For 10% household ammonia that is roughly 1.5 ml per 20 US gallons of water, but test and adjust.

How do I know when my tank is cycled?

Dose 2 ppm of ammonia and test 24 hours later. If ammonia and nitrite both read 0, two days in a row, the tank is cycled. Nitrate should be present.

Can I cycle a tank with fish in it?

It is possible but hard on the fish, because they are exposed to ammonia and nitrite for weeks. If fish are already in the tank, follow a new tank syndrome plan: test daily and change water to keep ammonia and nitrite under 0.5 ppm.

Does bottled bacteria really work?

Some products speed the cycle up, but results vary by product and storage. Use it with an ammonia source and keep testing. Mature filter media from an established tank is more reliable.

Should I do water changes while cycling?

Not on a fishless cycle, unless nitrite goes over about 5 ppm or pH drops below 6.5. Do one large change at the end to bring nitrate down before fish go in.

Why is my cycle stuck at the nitrite stage?

Usually too much ammonia dosed, low pH or low KH. Change about 50% of the water, check pH and KH, and drop the dose to 1–2 ppm.
