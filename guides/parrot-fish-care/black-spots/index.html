Parrot Fish Black Spots: Causes and Treatment

🧪 Test water first  |  ⚫ Usually healing burn  |  📅 Fades over weeks

Black spots on a blood parrot fish look alarming and are usually not an infection. In the large majority of cases they are melanin marks left by healing chemical burns from ammonia or nitrite, or ordinary stress marbling. This page works through the causes in order of likelihood, what each one looks like, and what to do.

Start here. Before treating anything, test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate. If ammonia or nitrite is above 0 ppm, or nitrate is above 40 ppm, you have almost certainly found your answer — and adding medication to bad water makes things worse.

Ammonia Burn: The Usual Answer

Ammonia and nitrite chemically burn skin and gill tissue. As the tissue heals, the fish deposits melanin over it — so the black marks appear as the fish gets better, not while it is getting worse. That timing confuses a lot of owners into thinking a new disease has started.

Typical pattern: a new tank, a filter change, a dead fish left in the tank, or a skipped run of water changes; then clamped fins and hiding; then, a week or two later, black patches spreading across the head and fins.

What to do:

  1. Test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate with a liquid kit.
  2. Do a 25–30% water change immediately, then daily changes until ammonia and nitrite both read 0.
  3. Use a dechlorinator that detoxifies ammonia while the biofilter catches up.
  4. Stop feeding for a day or two — less food means less ammonia.
  5. Do not change or rinse the filter media in tap water; the bacteria you need are living in it.
  6. Keep temperature stable at 80°F and leave the fish alone.

The marks fade over weeks to a few months once the water is clean. Some fish keep a faint permanent shadow, which is cosmetic. Full parameter targets are on the water parameters page.

Stress Marbling and Genetics

Blood parrots also darken from plain stress — bullying, a new tank, a light that is too bright, a tank in a high-traffic room. Stress marbling is smudgier than burn marks and often changes within hours. Fix the stressor and the colour returns: add caves, dim the lighting, and check the tank mate list for an aggressor.

Genetic black markings are different again: they are stable, present from a young age, and do not spread. Calico and marbled blood parrots are a recognised natural pattern, not a health problem.

Black Spot Disease (Rare)

True black spot disease is caused by encysted digenean fluke larvae, and it needs snails plus fish-eating birds to complete its life cycle — which is why it turns up in ponds and wild-caught fish and almost never in a closed indoor aquarium.

Tell it apart by the shape: black spot disease produces distinct, evenly sized, slightly raised dots like ground pepper, scattered uniformly. Ammonia burn produces irregular patches of varying size. If you genuinely have flukes, treat with praziquantel and remove snails to break the cycle.

Quick Diagnostic Walkthrough

  1. Are ammonia or nitrite above 0? → Chemical burn. Water changes, not medication.
  2. Is nitrate above 40 ppm? → Chronic stress and burn. Increase water change volume and frequency.
  3. Are the marks irregular patches? → Burn or stress marbling.
  4. Are they uniform raised dots? → Possible flukes; treat with praziquantel.
  5. Have they been there since the fish was small? → Genetics; no action needed.
  6. Is the fish eating and swimming normally? → Cosmetic. Fix the water and wait.
  7. Is it also gasping, clamped, or refusing food? → Treat that first; the spots are secondary. See blood parrot diseases.

Preventing Black Spots

  • Cycle the tank fully before adding fish, and never fish-in cycle a blood parrot if you can avoid it.
  • Change 25–30% of the water weekly and keep nitrate under 30 ppm.
  • Do not overfeed; uneaten food is the most common ammonia source in a stocked tank.
  • Rinse filter media in old tank water only.
  • Keep at least one cave per fish so a stressed parrot has somewhere to go.
  • Quarantine new fish for 2–4 weeks before they meet the display tank.
  • Test monthly even when everything looks fine — the point is to catch the problem before the fish shows it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my blood parrot fish have black spots?

The most common cause is melanin left behind as ammonia or nitrite burns heal, which is why the marks often appear after a water quality problem has already passed. Stress marbling and natural calico genetics account for most of the rest.

Will black spots on my parrot fish go away?

Ammonia burn marks usually fade over several weeks to a few months once ammonia and nitrite read 0 ppm and nitrate stays under 30 ppm. Some fish keep a faint permanent shadow, which is cosmetic only.

Are black spots on parrot fish contagious?

Burn marks, stress marbling and genetic markings are not contagious. True black spot disease from digenean flukes is contagious but very rare in closed aquariums, because it needs snails and fish-eating birds to complete its life cycle.

How do I treat black spots on a blood parrot?

Test the water first. If ammonia or nitrite is above 0, do daily 25-30% water changes until they clear rather than medicating. Only uniform, raised, pepper-like dots suggest flukes, which are treated with praziquantel.

Is black colour on a blood parrot normal?

It can be. Calico and marbled blood parrots naturally carry black patches, and these are stable from a young age and do not spread. New black marks that appear on an adult fish point to water quality or stress instead.
