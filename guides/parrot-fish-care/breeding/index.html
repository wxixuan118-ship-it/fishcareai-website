Blood Parrot Fish Breeding and Baby Parrot Fish

🥚 200–400 eggs  |  ♂️ Males usually sterile  |  🌡️ 82–84°F to spawn

Blood parrot cichlids pair up, dig pits, lay eggs and guard them like textbook cichlid parents — and then almost nothing hatches. The reason is hybrid sterility, and understanding it saves a lot of disappointment. This page covers blood parrot fish breeding, why the eggs usually fail, what a viable cross looks like, and how to raise baby blood parrot fish if you do get fry.

Why Blood Parrot Breeding Usually Fails

Almost all male blood parrots are sterile. They are hybrids, and hybrid males in this cross generally have non-functional or absent milt-producing tissue. Females are usually fertile and lay perfectly good eggs; there is simply nothing to fertilise them.

What you see in a home tank is therefore very common: a pair forms, cleans a flat rock or digs a pit, the female lays 200–400 eggs, both fish fan and guard them — and within 48–72 hours the eggs turn opaque white with fungus and the parents eat them. That is unfertilised eggs going off, not bad parenting.

Infertile spawning is still a good sign. Fish only spawn when they are healthy, well fed and settled in stable water. If your blood parrots are laying eggs, your husbandry is working — even though nothing will hatch.

When Fry Are Actually Possible

Viable blood parrot fry come from one of three routes, and commercial breeders use all of them:

  • Female blood parrot × male of a parent-type species — a midas, redhead or convict cichlid male. This is the common backcross and it does produce fry, though the young often look less "parrot" than the mother.
  • A rare fertile male blood parrot. A small percentage exist. If a spawn in an all-parrot tank produces fry, you have one.
  • Commercial hormone or hand-stripping methods, used in production hatcheries and not practical or advisable at home.

Fry from a backcross are legitimate fish, but be clear with anyone you give them to about what they are — hybrids of hybrids are exactly how the trade produced fish with mouths that do not close.

Sexing Blood Parrot Fish

Sexing is genuinely difficult and no single feature is reliable. Working indicators:

FeatureMaleFemale
SizeUsually slightly largerUsually slightly smaller
Fin shapeDorsal and anal fins more pointedFins more rounded
Venting (at spawning)Narrow, pointed papillaWider, blunter ovipositor
BehaviourMore display and territory patrollingCleans and inspects the spawning site

The dependable method is behavioural: whichever fish lays the eggs is female. Venting is only readable in the days around a spawn.

Setting Up for a Spawn

  1. Let a pair form naturally. Grow out 5–6 juveniles in a 75–100 gallon tank and let two pick each other — forced pairings usually end in one fish being beaten.
  2. Move the pair to their own tank of 55 gallons or more if you can. Spawning blood parrots become genuinely territorial and will harass tank mates.
  3. Provide spawning sites: flat slate, a large smooth rock, or a terracotta pot on its side.
  4. Raise the temperature to 82–84°F and hold pH near 7.0.
  5. Condition with protein — frozen bloodworms, mysis and brine shrimp for two to three weeks. See the feeding guide.
  6. Trigger with a water change — a 30% change with slightly cooler water often prompts spawning within days.

Eggs and Timeline

SpawningDay 0200–400 amber eggs on a cleaned flat surface
Fertile eggsDay 1–3Stay amber/translucent; parents fan constantly

First colourMonth 5–6Grey-brown juveniles begin turning orange

Blood parrots are good parents when they have something to parent — they fan the eggs, move wrigglers between pits, and herd free-swimming fry. Leaving the fry with the parents is usually fine, and safer than moving eggs to a bare tank.

Raising Baby Blood Parrot Fish

  • First foods: newly hatched brine shrimp, microworms or a quality powdered fry food, 3–4 times a day.
  • Water: small daily changes of 10–15% with a sponge filter only — fry get pulled into hang-on and canister intakes.
  • Temperature: hold 82°F for the first few weeks.
  • Grading: separate fast growers from slow ones by week four; blood parrot fry cannibalise size differences.
  • Move to crushed pellets around week six, then small sinking pellets.
  • Expect the colour change at 5–6 months. Grey-brown fry are normal, not failed fish.
  • Cull expectations: a share of blood parrot fry carry deformities incompatible with life. Decide in advance how you will handle it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can blood parrot fish breed?

Females are fertile and lay 200-400 eggs, but almost all male blood parrots are sterile because they are hybrids. Pairs spawn and guard the eggs normally, then the unfertilised eggs turn white and fungus over within two to three days.

Why are my blood parrot eggs turning white?

White, opaque eggs are unfertilised. This is the normal outcome in an all-blood-parrot tank because the males are sterile. Fertile eggs stay amber and translucent and hatch in three to five days.

How do you get baby blood parrot fish?

The reliable route is crossing a female blood parrot with a male of a parent-type species such as a midas, redhead or convict cichlid. A small percentage of male blood parrots are fertile, so an all-parrot spawn occasionally produces fry.

How can you tell if a blood parrot fish is male or female?

There is no reliable external test. Males tend to be slightly larger with more pointed dorsal and anal fins; females are rounder-finned and show a wider ovipositor at spawning time. In practice, the fish that lays the eggs is the female.

What do baby blood parrot fish look like?

Fry and juveniles are grey-brown with no orange at all. The colour change happens at around five to six months, so a drab juvenile is normal rather than a sign of poor health.

How many eggs do blood parrot fish lay?

A spawn is typically 200-400 eggs laid on a cleaned flat rock, slate or the floor of a pit the pair digs. Both parents fan and guard them.
