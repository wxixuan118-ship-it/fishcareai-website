Oscar Fish Hole in the Head (HITH and HLLE)

🕳️ Pits above the eyes  |  💧 Nitrate is the main cause  |  💊 Metronidazole if wasting

Hole-in-the-head is the disease oscars are known for. It starts as small pale pits above the eyes, often mistaken for damage from digging, and if the cause is not corrected it develops into open craters along the head and lateral line. It is treatable, and it is very largely preventable.

The short version. Pits above the eyes on an oscar mean the tank's long-term water quality or the fish's diet is wrong. Fix nitrate and diet first, and treat with metronidazole if the fish is also losing weight or passing white stringy faeces.

What Hole-in-the-Head Is

Two overlapping conditions get the same name:

  • HITH (hole-in-the-head) — pitting concentrated on the head, above and around the eyes, frequently associated with the flagellate parasite Hexamita (also classified as Spironucleus).
  • HLLE (head and lateral line erosion) — the same kind of erosion extending backward along the lateral line, usually with no parasite involvement at all.

In practice the two run together in oscars and respond to the same corrections. Hexamita is a normal resident of a healthy cichlid's intestine; it only becomes a problem when a fish is immunosuppressed — which is why treating the parasite without fixing the tank produces a fish that relapses within months.

Symptoms in Order of Appearance

  1. Small pale or white pits above the eyes, 1–2 mm across. Easy to miss.
  2. Pits deepen and widen; the surrounding skin loses colour.
  3. Erosion extends backward along the lateral line.
  4. White or cream-coloured mucus threads from the openings.
  5. Appetite drops; the fish becomes lethargic.
  6. White stringy faeces and visible weight loss — this is the sign that Hexamita is systemically involved.
  7. Secondary bacterial and fungal infection of the open lesions.
Do not confuse pits with sensory pores. Every oscar has a row of normal sensory pores across the head — small, evenly spaced, symmetrical and the same colour as the surrounding skin. HITH lesions are irregular, uneven, pale-rimmed and get bigger over weeks.

Treatment, Step by Step

Stage 1 — fix the environment (always, whatever else you do).

  • Test nitrate. If it is over 40 ppm, bring it down with 30% changes daily for several days rather than one huge change.
  • Move to 50% weekly water changes permanently, or twice weekly if the tank is heavily stocked.
  • Confirm ammonia and nitrite read 0 and the filter is adequate for the tank.
  • Check the tank size and any aggression from tank mates — chronic stress is a real contributor.

Stage 2 — fix the diet.

  • Replace dry food opened more than six months ago. Vitamin content degrades.
  • Switch to a quality pellet with added vitamin C, and add frozen foods — krill, mysis — two or three times a week.
  • Add vegetable content: blanched peas, spirulina.
  • Vitamin-soaking food with a commercial fish vitamin supplement is worthwhile during recovery.

Stage 3 — medicate, if indicated.

  • Treat with metronidazole if the fish shows weight loss, white stringy faeces or a poor appetite alongside the pits.
  • Medicated food is far more effective than water dosing, because the target is in the gut. If the fish is still eating, soak pellets in a metronidazole solution.
  • If the fish is not eating, dose the water in a hospital tank — typically around 250 mg per 10 gallons every 24 hours for three days, with a 25–50% water change before each dose. Follow the product's instructions.
  • Add an antibacterial only if the lesions are visibly infected, red-rimmed or fungused.

What Recovery Looks Like

The pits do not close overnight. Expect this sequence:

TimeframeWhat to expect
Week 1–2Appetite improves; lesions stop spreading; mucus threads stop
Week 3–6Pit edges become less inflamed; new tissue starts filling shallow pits
Month 2–4Shallow pits close; deeper craters visibly shrink
Month 4–12Deep lesions fill in; faint scarring may remain permanently

Deep craters often leave a permanent mark. That is cosmetic, and a fish with scarred pits that have stopped progressing is a recovered fish.

Prevention

  • Nitrate under 20 ppm, tested weekly. This alone prevents most cases.
  • 40–50% water changes every week without fail.
  • A varied diet with genuine vitamin content, and dry food replaced twice a year.
  • A tank large enough that the fish is not chronically stressed — see oscar tank size.
  • Quarantine new fish for four weeks so you are not importing parasites.
  • Check the head above the eyes every week while you feed. Early pits are trivially easy to reverse; deep craters take a year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes hole in the head in oscar fish?

The most consistent factors are chronic high nitrate from infrequent water changes and a poor or vitamin-deficient diet, which suppress the fish's immunity. The parasite Hexamita, a normal gut resident, then overgrows and spreads. Chronic stress from an undersized tank contributes.

How do I treat hole in the head in an oscar?

Fix the environment first: bring nitrate under 20 ppm with successive water changes and move to 50% weekly changes. Then fix the diet with fresh vitamin-rich food. Add metronidazole - ideally in food - if the fish is also losing weight, passing white stringy faeces, or off its food.

Are the holes on my oscar's head normal pores?

Every oscar has a row of normal sensory pores across its head: small, evenly spaced, symmetrical and the same colour as the surrounding skin. Hole-in-the-head lesions are irregular, uneven, pale-rimmed, and grow larger over weeks.

Will hole in the head heal on its own?

Shallow early pits often close once nitrate and diet are corrected, without medication. Established lesions with weight loss or white stringy faeces need metronidazole as well. Untreated, the erosion continues and secondary bacterial infection becomes likely.

How long does it take an oscar to recover from hole in the head?

Lesions stop spreading within one to two weeks of correcting water and diet. Shallow pits close over two to four months, and deep craters can take four months to a year to fill in, often leaving faint permanent scarring that is cosmetic only.

Is hole in the head contagious to other fish?

Not in the way ich is. Hexamita is present in most healthy cichlids already and only causes disease in fish whose immunity is compromised. If several fish in one tank develop pitting, the shared cause is the tank conditions, not fish-to-fish transmission.
