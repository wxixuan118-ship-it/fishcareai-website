Oscar Fish Care Guide: Size, Lifespan, Price & Tank Mates

By James ChenReviewed by Dr. Sarah MitchellUpdated 2026-06-2310 min read

Oscar fish care is for keepers ready for a large, intelligent, messy cichlid. Oscars need large tanks, strong filtration, secure lids, and long-term commitment.

Quick care note: Test water before guessing. Ammonia and nitrite should always be 0 ppm, and oxygen problems need fast action.
🖨️ Oscar Fish Care Sheet (save or print this box)
  • Tank size: 75 gallons minimum for one adult; 6-foot-long tank for a pair
  • Temperature: 74–80°F (23–27°C); pH 6–8
  • Ammonia / nitrite: always 0 ppm; nitrate under 40 ppm
  • Adult size: 10–14 inches (some to 16+ in a large tank)
  • Lifespan: 10–15 years typically, up to 20 with excellent care
  • Diet: quality cichlid pellets as staple; avoid feeder fish
  • Price: $10–$30 typical juvenile; $50–$200+ for specialty color varieties
  • Tank mates: large, robust, similarly sized fish only — nothing that fits in an Oscar's mouth

Tank Size

Oscars grow large and should not be kept in small aquariums. Plan around adult size, not the juvenile size seen in stores.

Oscar Fish Size & Growth by Age

The single biggest mistake new Oscar owners make is judging tank needs by the 2–3 inch juvenile at the store rather than the fish it becomes. Oscars are fast growers, typically adding about an inch of length per month for their first six months before growth slows.

AgeTypical Size
At purchase (2–3 months)2–3 in (5–8 cm)
6 months7–8 in (18–20 cm)

18–24 months (adult)10–14 in (25–36 cm), some to 16+ in

Growth rate slows considerably after the first year, but fish continue to fill out and bulk up in body mass for another year or two after reaching typical adult length. A cramped tank can stunt growth — another reason to size the tank for the adult, not the juvenile.

Oscar Fish Lifespan

With good care, Oscars typically live 10–15 years, and well-maintained fish in spacious, stable tanks have been reported to reach 20 years or more — genuinely a multi-decade commitment, closer to a dog's lifespan than most aquarium fish. Water quality, tank size, and diet are the biggest factors separating a 10-year Oscar from a 20-year Oscar.

Filtration

Oscars are messy eaters and heavy waste producers. Oversized filtration and regular maintenance are essential.

Feeding

Feed quality cichlid pellets with appropriate treats. Avoid feeder fish because they can introduce disease and poor nutrition.

How Much Do Oscar Fish Cost?

Oscars are inexpensive compared to most large aquarium fish. A typical juvenile costs $10–$30 at a local pet store, with baby Oscars (1–3 inches) often at the lower end of that range. Specialty color varieties — albino, long-fin, short-body, or rare patterns — commonly run $30–$100 and can exceed $200 for particularly striking or hard-to-find individuals. Established breeding pairs cost more than buying fish individually and aren't always readily available.

The fish itself is the smaller expense. The real cost of Oscar ownership is the setup: a 75-gallon tank runs roughly $300–$600, plus $75–$150 for a canister filter capable of handling the bioload, plus a heater and ongoing food and electricity costs. Budget for the tank and equipment as the primary expense, not the fish.

When looking at Oscar fish for sale, local fish stores are typically the cheapest option for common color varieties. Online retailers and specialty breeders often charge more but may offer higher-grade or rarer color morphs, with shipping adding $25–$80 to the total. Whichever source you choose, ask about the fish's current size and how long it's been in the seller's tank — a recently arrived fish benefits from a few days to settle before a stressful move to a new home.

Water Quality

Ammonia and nitrite should always be 0 ppm. Large water changes may be needed to control nitrate in oscar tanks.

Tank Mates

Tank mates must be chosen carefully. Oscars have a large mouth that can swallow anything small enough to fit, so nano fish, small tetras, and dwarf shrimp are off the table entirely — regardless of temperament, they're at risk simply from size.

Oscars generally do best with other large, robust South American fish that can hold their own: bigger plecos, silver dollars, other similarly sized cichlids like Jack Dempseys and Firemouths, and Texas or Salvini cichlids in a large enough tank. Oscars aren't constantly aggressive the way some cichlids are, but they become territorial and unpredictable during spawning, and a stressed or bullied Oscar can also end up on the losing end against equally large but more aggressive tankmates — so "big enough to not be eaten" isn't the same as "guaranteed compatible." Many experienced keepers ultimately keep a single Oscar alone, which sidesteps compatibility risk entirely and is a perfectly reasonable choice given how interactive a lone Oscar is with its owner.

Enrichment and Behavior

Oscars are intelligent and interactive. Provide sturdy decor, secure equipment, and a lid because they may jump or move objects.

Daily Oscar Care Routine

Oscars are interactive fish, so daily observation is easy and important. Watch appetite, breathing, color, waste, and whether the fish is rubbing, sulking, or damaging decor. A sudden loss of appetite often points to stress, water quality, or temperature problems.

Large Tank Maintenance

Oscar fish produce heavy waste, so strong filtration and water changes are not optional. Test nitrate regularly and keep the substrate free of trapped food. Secure heaters, lids, and decorations because large oscars can move equipment and injure themselves.

Feeding and Enrichment

Use quality cichlid pellets as the staple diet and add appropriate frozen or fresh foods for variety. Avoid feeder fish because they can introduce disease and poor nutrition. Enrichment can include rearranged decor, safe hiding areas, and a stable routine that reduces stress.

Oscar Warning Signs

Watch for heavy breathing, cloudy eyes, head pits, torn skin, loss of appetite, or repeated rubbing against objects. Oscars are tough fish, but poor water quality and cramped tanks can still cause long-term disease. Because they are messy eaters, remove leftover food and keep water changes consistent.

Taxonomy

Oscars belong to the family Cichlidae and genus Astronotus. The single commonly kept species is A. ocellatus. Common color forms include Tiger Oscar (red-orange pattern on dark body), Red Oscar (vivid red-orange), Albino, Lemon, and Lutino varieties produced through selective breeding.

Breeding

Oscars form monogamous pairs that can take years to establish naturally from a group. Breeding pairs clean a flat surface (often the aquarium floor or a large flat stone), spawn 1,000–3,000 adhesive eggs, and aggressively defend the clutch. Eggs hatch in 48–72 hours; both parents tend to the larvae. Fry become free-swimming in about a week and can be fed baby brine shrimp.

Fish Care FAQ

Are oscars good beginner fish?

Not ideal as a true first fish. Oscars are hardy and forgiving of water-parameter swings compared to many cichlids, which counts in their favor, but the 75-gallon-plus tank, heavy waste production, and years-long size commitment trip up new keepers who buy a 2-inch juvenile without planning for the adult it becomes. A better fit for a committed beginner who has already run a smaller tank successfully and understands what they're signing up for.

How big do oscar fish get?

Most adults reach 10–14 inches, with some individuals exceeding 16 inches in very large, well-maintained tanks. They reach most of that length within 18–24 months, growing roughly an inch a month for the first six months.

What is the oscar fish lifespan?

10–15 years typically, with well-cared-for fish in spacious, stable tanks living 20 years or more.

How much does an oscar fish cost?

A typical juvenile costs $10–$30. Specialty color varieties like albino, long-fin, or short-body Oscars run $30–$100 or more. The tank and filtration setup costs considerably more than the fish itself.

What do oscar fish eat?

A quality cichlid pellet should be the staple, with occasional appropriate frozen or prepared foods.

Can oscars live with small fish?

No. Small fish may be eaten or stressed by an oscar.

What are the best tank mates for an oscar?

Other large, robust South American fish such as big plecos, silver dollars, and similarly sized cichlids like Jack Dempseys or Firemouths. Many keepers house a single Oscar alone rather than risk an incompatible pairing.
