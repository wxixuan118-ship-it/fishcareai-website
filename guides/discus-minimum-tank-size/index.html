Discus Minimum Tank Size

Compare 55, 75, 90 and 100-gallon aquariums for adult discus groups, juvenile grow-out tanks and established pairs.

What Is the Minimum Tank Size for Discus?

A standard 55-gallon aquarium is a constrained lower limit, not the ideal default. For a social group of about six adult discus, 75 gallons or more is the more practical starting point. A 90–100-gallon tank provides additional water volume, front-to-back depth and room for lower-ranking fish to avoid dominant individuals.

Planning answer: use 55 gallons only as a carefully managed minimum; choose 75+ gallons for a normal adult group; consider 90–100 gallons when space, aquascaping, tank mates or a larger group reduce the usable swimming area.

Discus Minimum Tank Size Depends on the Whole Group

Discus are tall, social cichlids that produce substantial waste and establish a hierarchy. Tank size therefore cannot be decided from the length of one fish or a simple gallons-per-fish rule. The aquarium must hold the intended adult group, provide usable depth and height, dilute waste between water changes and leave enough territory for a subordinate fish to retreat.

The Reddit discussion supplied for this page shows the range of real keeper opinions: several commenters describe 55 gallons as a minimum, others prefer 75 gallons for adults, and one recommends about 100 gallons because the same fish become much more labor-intensive in a smaller volume. Those comments support a conservative conclusion: a setup can be technically possible at the lower boundary without being the easiest or safest long-term choice.

Can Discus Live in a 55-Gallon Tank?

A 55-gallon aquarium can function for a carefully managed small group, especially while captive-bred fish are still growing, but it leaves little margin. The common narrow footprint limits turning space and aquascaping, and adults occupy more visual territory than juveniles suggest. Warm water also holds less dissolved oxygen, so filtration, surface exchange and maintenance must be consistent.

Do not treat “55 gallons” as permission to add a large group, active tank mates and bulky decor. Measure usable water after substrate, wood, equipment and the actual water line. If aggression persists, nitrate rises rapidly or the fish cannot maintain good body condition, reduce competing livestock or upgrade rather than compensating indefinitely with more products.

Is a 75-Gallon Tank Better for Discus?

Yes. A standard 75-gallon tank usually adds valuable front-to-back depth while retaining a useful four-foot length. That extra footprint creates broader feeding areas, more flexible visual barriers and a better chance for lower-ranking fish to move away without being pinned against the glass. The added volume also slows changes in temperature and waste concentration.

For approximately six adult discus, 75 gallons is a sensible planning baseline rather than a guarantee. Heavy feeding, warm water, tank mates and dense hardscape can still demand stronger filtration and more frequent maintenance. A 90- or 100-gallon aquarium becomes attractive when the keeper wants more open water, a planted display or a larger social group.

How Many Discus Should Be Kept Together?

A group of roughly five or six is commonly recommended because discus form a social hierarchy. In an undersized group, one lower-ranking fish may receive concentrated aggression and lose access to food. A single discus is not the normal long-term plan. An established compatible pair can be kept separately for breeding, but a random pair is not automatically bonded or peaceful.

Do not solve aggression with arithmetic alone. Watch feeding access, body color, hiding, fin damage and weight. Have a divider, quarantine tank or rehoming plan if one fish is repeatedly excluded.

Best Discus Tank Dimensions and Footprint

Gallons describe volume, but adult discus also need height and front-to-back depth for their tall, laterally compressed bodies. Prefer a long rectangular aquarium with open central swimming space over a narrow decorative tank of similar capacity. Leave enough height above the substrate and enough depth around wood or plants for adults to turn comfortably.

Juvenile Discus Grow-Out Tank Size

Small juveniles can temporarily occupy less space, but they need frequent feeding and therefore create a high waste load. A bare-bottom grow-out tank may be easier to clean and monitor, yet it is a production stage—not evidence that the same aquarium will suit the fish as adults. Build the upgrade into the purchase plan before buying the group.

Track body growth, hierarchy and water quality rather than waiting until the fish visibly look crowded. Moving a mature group after chronic aggression or declining water quality is harder than beginning with the adult tank.

Filtration, Oxygen and Maintenance Change the Answer

Discus are commonly maintained in warm water, which carries less dissolved oxygen than cooler water. Use mature biological filtration, reliable mechanical filtration and gentle but effective circulation with surface exchange. Oversized filtration can process more waste, but it does not create swimming room or eliminate the need for water changes.

Measure ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm and track nitrate trends. Feed so every fish gets access without leaving food to decay. Smaller tanks usually require more frequent observation and maintenance; that workload is why experienced keepers in the Reddit discussion often recommend moving beyond the absolute minimum.

Discus Tank Size FAQ

Is 10 gallons per discus enough?

It can be a rough comparison inside an already adequate group aquarium, but it should not determine the tank by itself. Five fish do not automatically make a 50-gallon tank suitable; footprint, group dynamics, filtration and usable volume still matter.

Can I keep two discus in a 40-gallon tank?

An established compatible breeding pair may use a dedicated tank near that size, but two unpaired discus can direct aggression at each other. A 40-gallon tank is not a general substitute for a properly sized social group aquarium.

Are discus suitable for a first aquarium?

They are usually a challenging first fish because warm, clean, stable water, social management and frequent observation are essential. Read the complete Discus Fish Care Guide before purchasing livestock.
