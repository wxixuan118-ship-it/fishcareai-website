Aquarium Hardscape: Choosing Safe Rocks, Driftwood and Substrate

🧪 Test every rock  |  🪵 Soak wood 1–2 weeks  |  📐 Substrate calculator below

Updated November 22, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

Aquarium hardscape is everything in an aquascape that isn't alive: rocks, driftwood and the substrate they sit in. It is the skeleton of the layout, and it is also part of your fish's water chemistry. Some stones raise pH and hardness, some woods stain the water and lower pH, and a badly stacked rock can crack the glass. This guide covers what is safe, how to test and prepare it, and how much substrate to buy. For styles and the full setup order, see the aquascaping guide.

Aquarium hardscape in short

  1. Choose inert rock (dragon stone, lava rock, slate, quartz) for soft-water fish. Seiryu and limestone raise pH and hardness.
  2. Test unknown stones with a few drops of vinegar, then confirm with a KH test after a week in a bucket.
  3. Soak driftwood for 1–2 weeks so it sinks and releases most of its tannins. White fuzz in the first weeks is harmless.
  4. Build rocks on the glass, not on the substrate, so fish can't undermine them, and slope the substrate from 1–2 inches at the front to 3–5 at the back.

Avoid rocks with metallic veins or rusty stains (they can leach metals), painted or sealed decor stones, and anything collected from polluted ground or near roads. Keep sharp lava and dragon stone away from bettas and other long-finned fish, which tear fins easily.

How to Test Hardscape Rocks Before They Go In

  1. Vinegar test. Put a few drops of white vinegar (or a weak acid) on a clean, dry spot. Fizzing means carbonate, so the rock will raise pH and hardness. No fizz is a good sign but not proof: some stones react too slowly to see.
  2. Bucket test. Soak the scrubbed rock for a week in a bucket of tap water, with a second bucket of plain tap water as a control. Test KH and pH in both. If the rock's bucket reads 1–2 dKH or more above the control, it is buffering.
  3. Clean it. Scrub with a stiff brush and hot water. No soap or bleach. Boil small collected stones if you can; never boil or heat rocks that might hold water inside, because they can crack.

  • Soak it. Weigh the wood down in a bucket or tub for 1–2 weeks, changing the water every few days. Boiling small pieces for an hour speeds this up and helps it sink.
  • Tannins turn the water tea-coloured and lower pH a little. They are harmless and many soft-water fish like them. Activated carbon and water changes clear them if you want clear water.
  • White fuzz (a harmless fungus or biofilm) often appears on new wood in the first 2–4 weeks. Snails, otocinclus and shrimp eat it, and it disappears on its own.
  • Collected wood: only use fully dead, dry hardwood with no bark or rot, and avoid conifers, cedar and pine (resins), treated or painted wood, and wood from polluted areas. If in doubt, buy aquarium driftwood.

A common layout is aqua soil in the planted areas and a strip of sand in the front or along a "path", separated by stones so they don't mix.

How Much Substrate for a Sloped Hardscape?

Use the same number as the front for a flat bed.

Stones and wood take the place of some substrate.

The sum is the floor area times the average depth. A 30 × 12 inch tank sloped from 1.5 to 4 inches averages 2.75 inches deep, so it needs about 16 litres of substrate before you subtract the space under the rocks. Building a Stable Aquarium Hardscape

  • Rocks go on the glass first. Set large stones on the bare bottom (on a thin sheet of egg crate or foam to spread the load), then pour the substrate around them. Fish that dig can undermine stones that sit on top of the substrate.
  • Lock pieces together. Wedge small stones under larger ones, and glue stacks with cyanoacrylate gel (superglue gel) or aquarium-safe epoxy. Cyanoacrylate is commonly used once it has cured; let glued pieces set out of water first.
  • Build the slope with structure, not just substrate. Stones or mesh bags of lava rock under the back substrate hold a steep slope without packing 6+ inches of soil.
  • Mind the weight. Rock adds a lot: a large iwagumi can put 30–60 lb of stone on the glass of a 20–40 gallon tank. Check your stand against the filled weight on our tank size pages, such as the 40 gallon fish tank.
  • Leave room to clean. Keep a gap between the hardscape and the glass, and make sure you can reach every corner with a scraper and a gravel vacuum.
  • Leave room to swim. Active fish need open water along the front or top; plan caves for shy fish and bottom dwellers.

Hardscape by Aquascaping Style

  • Iwagumi: stones only, one rock type, odd number, no wood.
  • Nature: wood as the main structure with stones at its base; branches give the forest look.
  • Dutch: little or no visible hardscape; a few stones may hold terraces.
  • Biotope: whatever the habitat has, such as roots and leaf litter for an Amazon blackwater tank; see the discus aquascape.

Hardscape Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding untested collected rocks to a soft-water tank.
  • Putting floating wood in without soaking it, then tying it down with fishing line that shows.
  • Stacking rocks on top of the substrate, where digging fish can topple them.
  • Hardscape so tight against the glass that you can't clean behind it.
  • Adding fish straight onto new aqua soil before the ammonia spike has passed.

Aquarium Hardscape FAQ

What rocks are safe for an aquarium?

Inert rocks such as dragon (ohko) stone, lava rock, slate, quartz and granite are safe for most fish. Seiryu stone, limestone and Texas holey rock raise pH and hardness, so use them only where harder water suits the fish.

How do I know if a rock will raise pH?

Put a few drops of vinegar on it: fizzing means carbonate, so it will raise pH and hardness. For certainty, soak the rock in a bucket for a week and compare KH and pH with a bucket of plain tap water.

How long should I soak driftwood?

Usually 1–2 weeks, changing the water every few days, until it sinks and the water stays fairly clear. Boiling small pieces for an hour speeds it up.

Is the white fuzz on my driftwood harmful?

No. It is a harmless fungus or bacterial film that is common on new wood in the first few weeks. Snails, shrimp and otocinclus eat it, and it fades on its own.

Does driftwood lower pH?

A little. Tannins released by the wood slightly lower pH and soften the water, and they tint it brown. The effect is mild in most tap water.

How deep should aquarium substrate be?

About 1–2 inches at the front and 3–5 inches at the back for a planted tank. Sand for fish-only tanks can be 1–2 inches throughout.

Can I use rocks and wood from outside?

Sometimes. Test rocks for carbonate, scrub them and avoid metallic or polluted stones. Only use dead, dry hardwood without bark, and avoid conifers and treated wood. If unsure, buy aquarium hardscape.
