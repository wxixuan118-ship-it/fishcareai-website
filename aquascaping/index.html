Aquascaping: What It Is, the Main Styles and How to Build Your First Layout

🪨 Hardscape first, plants second  |  ⏱️ 6–12 weeks to grow in  |  🐟 Fish go in after the cycle

Updated November 22, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

Aquascaping is the craft of arranging rocks, wood, substrate and live plants in an aquarium so the tank looks like a landscape: a mountain range, a forest floor, a riverbank or a lush garden. It is part design and part fishkeeping. A layout only stays beautiful if the water underneath it is healthy, so this guide covers both: the styles and steps, and what keeps the plants and fish thriving once the scape is planted.

The short answer

  1. Pick a style first. Nature (wood, stones and mixed plants) is the most forgiving. Iwagumi (stones only) looks simple but is the least forgiving. Dutch (plants only, no hardscape) needs the most trimming.
  2. Build the hardscape dry, in the empty tank, until it looks right from the front. Substrate goes in sloped, about 1–2 inches at the front and 3–5 inches at the back.
  3. Plant densely on day one, fill slowly and run the light 6 hours a day at first, rising to 7–8 hours once the plants are growing.
  4. Cycle before adding fish. Expect 4–8 weeks, plus an algae phase in the first month that big water changes keep under control.

What Aquascaping Means

Every aquascape is built from three layers. The hardscape is the structure: stones, driftwood and the shape of the substrate. The plants soften the structure and give it depth. The livestock, usually one school of small fish plus shrimp or snails, gives the scene movement and scale.

The hobby has two main roots. Dutch hobbyists were building plant-only "underwater gardens" from the 1930s onward. In the 1990s the Japanese photographer and designer Takashi Amano popularised the nature aquarium, which copies real landscapes and made stone-only iwagumi layouts famous. Contests such as the International Aquatic Plants Layout Contest (IAPLC) now judge thousands of entries a year. You don't need contest-grade equipment to aquascape, though; a 10–20 gallon tank with a few stones, one piece of wood and easy plants is a complete aquascape.

How to choose: if this is your first scape, start with a nature or jungle layout using low-light plants. They tolerate imperfect lighting and dosing, and the wood hides mistakes. Choose iwagumi when you are ready for CO₂ and a carpet, and Dutch when you enjoy trimming every week. If one fish matters more than the look, a biotope or a style built around that fish works better; our discus aquascape guide is an example.

How to Aquascape a Tank: Step by Step

  1. Choose the tank and the style. A 20 gallon long or a tank about 24 inches wide is the easiest size to scape: big enough for depth, small enough to light well. Nano tanks (5–10 gallons) look great but show every mistake. See our 20 gallon fish tank page for dimensions.
  2. Sketch the layout. Decide where the focal point goes. Put it about one third of the way in from the left or right, not in the centre. Decide on a shape: a triangle (high on one side), a concave "U" (high on both sides, open middle) or a convex island (high in the middle).
  3. Build the hardscape dry. Arrange stones and wood in the empty tank, step back and look from the front, and adjust. This takes longer than anything else and is worth it. Our hardscape guide covers which rocks and wood are safe and how much substrate you need.
  4. Add the substrate in a slope, low at the front and high at the back, which makes a small tank look deeper. Tuck substrate under and around the stones so they sit firmly.
  5. Plant before you fill. Mist the substrate, then plant with tweezers, carpet plants in small pinches, stems in groups of 3–5. Plant more than you think you need: dense planting from day one is the best protection against algae.
  6. Fill slowly. Lay a plastic bag or plate on the substrate and pour onto it so the slope and plants stay in place. Add dechlorinator.
  7. Cycle the tank, then add fish gradually. Follow how to cycle a fish tank and stock lightly at first; the stocking calculator gives a sensible ceiling.

Low-Tech vs High-Tech Aquascapes

The biggest decision after the style is whether to inject CO₂. It changes almost everything else.

Low-techHigh-tech
CO₂None (or liquid carbon)Pressurised CO₂, about 20–30 ppm in the water
LightLow to medium, 6–8 hoursMedium to high, 7–8 hours
FertiliserRoot tabs, light weekly doseNutrient-rich soil plus dosing several times a week
Good plantsAnubias, java fern, cryptocoryne, bucephalandra, java moss, vallisneriaCarpets (Monte Carlo, HC Cuba, hairgrass), red stem plants, Rotala
TrimmingEvery few weeksWeekly
Main riskSlow growth, so algae on leavesUnbalanced light, CO₂ and nutrients, so algae fast

Pressurised CO₂ can also suffocate fish if it is overdosed, especially at night or when surface movement is low. If you add it, use a drop checker (green is the target, yellow means too much), run it on a timer that switches off an hour before the lights, and watch for fish gasping at the surface. For lighting, fertiliser and CO₂ details, see aquarium plant care.

Choosing Plants by Zone

  • Foreground (short, front 3–4 inches): Monte Carlo, dwarf hairgrass, dwarf sagittaria, small cryptocoryne such as C. parva. Dwarf sagittaria and crypts are the low-tech options; true carpets mostly need CO₂.
  • Midground (around the hardscape): anubias nana and java fern tied to stones or wood, bucephalandra, cryptocoryne wendtii, mosses.
  • Background (tall, fast growth): vallisneria, Rotala rotundifolia, Ludwigia, hygrophila, Amazon sword in larger tanks. Fast stem plants also take up nitrate and starve algae of nutrients.

Rhizome plants (anubias, java fern, bucephalandra) must be tied or glued to hardscape, not buried; a buried rhizome rots.

Fish for an Aquascape

Small schooling fish suit a scape best because they make the plants look large. Good choices are harlequin rasboras, ember tetras, chili rasboras (nano tanks), neon tetras and, in unheated tanks, white cloud mountain minnows. Add otocinclus, Amano shrimp or nerite snails as an algae crew.

Fish that wreck planted tanks: goldfish (eat and uproot plants), oscars and most large cichlids (dig and rearrange everything), silver dollars and Buenos Aires tetras (eat soft plants), and adult common plecos (too big, bulldoze layouts). If you keep these fish, use a hardscape-only layout with tough plants such as anubias and java fern attached to wood.

Keeping an Aquascape Healthy After Setup

Most aquascapes fail in the first two months, and almost always for the same reason: the plants have not grown in yet, so algae gets the light and nutrients first. A few habits carry a new scape through that phase:

  • Weeks 1–4: change 30–50% of the water two or three times a week, especially with nutrient-rich aqua soil, which leaches ammonia at first. Keep the light at 6 hours.
  • Weeks 4–8: drop to one 30–50% change a week. Raise the light by 30 minutes a week up to 7–8 hours if the plants are pearling or putting out new leaves and algae is under control.
  • After that: weekly 25–30% changes, trim stem plants before they shade the carpet, vacuum the open sand areas and clean the glass.

Brown diatoms in the first weeks are normal and fade on their own. Green hair algae or black beard algae later on usually mean light is ahead of CO₂ or nutrients; see how to get rid of algae. Plants melting, yellowing or turning transparent are covered under plant problems in aquarium plant care. And if nitrate creeps up, how to lower nitrates has a water-change calculator.

Common Aquascaping Mistakes

  • Too little hardscape. Stones and wood look bigger in the shop than in the tank. Buy more than you think you need and use the best pieces.
  • Everything centred and the same height. Move the focal point off-centre and make one side or one stone clearly dominant.
  • Too few plants at the start. Sparse planting is an open invitation to algae.
  • Lights on 10–12 hours from day one. Start at 6 hours.
  • Adding fish before the tank is cycled, often because the scape "looks finished". Ammonia from new soil plus fish waste is a common cause of new tank syndrome.
  • Untested rocks. Some stones raise pH and hardness. Test them first (see the hardscape guide).

Aquascaping FAQ

What is aquascaping?

Aquascaping is arranging rocks, driftwood, substrate and live plants in an aquarium so it looks like a landscape. Popular styles are Nature, Iwagumi, Dutch, Jungle and Biotope.

What is the easiest aquascaping style for beginners?

A nature or jungle layout with low-light plants such as anubias, java fern, cryptocoryne and vallisneria. It doesn't need CO₂, tolerates mistakes, and the wood and plants hide imperfections. Iwagumi looks simple but is one of the hardest styles.

Do I need CO₂ for an aquascape?

No. Low-tech aquascapes with slow-growing plants work well without it. You do need CO₂ for most carpet plants, for Dutch layouts and for dense red stem plants.

How long does an aquascape take to grow in?

Usually 6–12 weeks for stem plants and carpets to fill in, and several months for slow plants such as anubias and java fern. Expect an algae phase in the first month.

Can you aquascape a small tank?

Yes. 5–10 gallon nano aquascapes are popular, but small tanks change temperature and water quality quickly, so they need more frequent maintenance. A 20 gallon long is easier to keep stable.

Is aquascaping bad for fish?

Not if the tank is cycled before fish go in, the hardscape is safe, there is open swimming space and CO₂ is not overdosed. Live plants generally improve water quality.

How much does it cost to start aquascaping?

A simple low-tech 10–20 gallon aquascape with a basic light, inert substrate, a few stones, one piece of wood and easy plants costs far less than a high-tech setup with pressurised CO₂, aqua soil and a strong LED. Hardscape is often the most expensive part.
