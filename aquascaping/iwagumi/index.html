Iwagumi Aquascape: How to Place the Stones, Choose the Carpet and Keep It Clean

🪨 Odd number of stones, one dominant  |  🌱 One carpet plant  |  🫧 CO₂ for the carpet

Updated November 22, 2026 · FishCare AI editorial team

An iwagumi aquascape is a stone-only layout: a few rocks set in a low carpet of plants, with a lot of open space. The word is Japanese for "rock formation", and the style was made famous by Takashi Amano's nature aquariums. It uses fewer materials than any other style, yet it is one of the hardest to keep looking right, because there is nothing to hide algae, a patchy carpet or a badly placed stone. This page explains the placement rules, the stones and plants that work, and the upkeep that keeps it clean. For the basics of aquascaping, start with the aquascaping guide.

The short answer

  1. Use an odd number of stones (3, 5 or 7) of one rock type, with one clearly largest stone.
  2. Put the main stone off-centre, about one third of the way across the tank, and tilt every stone the same way with the grain lined up.
  3. Carpet with one plant, such as Monte Carlo or dwarf hairgrass, on 2–4 inches of aqua soil sloping up toward the back.
  4. Run pressurised CO₂ and 6–8 hours of light, change water often in the first month, and keep one school of small fish.

The Stones and Their Roles

Classic iwagumi gives each stone a name and a job. You don't need the names, but the hierarchy they describe is what makes a layout look calm instead of cluttered.

StoneRoleSize and position
Oyaishi (main stone)The focal pointThe largest and most striking stone, near the one-third line, usually the tallest
Fukuishi (secondary)Balances the main stoneClearly smaller, beside or behind the main stone, leaning the same way
Soeishi (third)Adds depth and leads the eyeSmaller again, often lower and further forward or on the opposite side
Suteishi (sacrificial)Fills gaps, may be half buriedSmall stones around the base, only in 5+ stone layouts

Placement Rules That Make It Work

  • Off-centre focal point. Place the main stone at roughly one third (or the golden ratio, about 38%) of the tank's width from one side. Dead centre looks static.
  • One clear leader. The main stone should be noticeably bigger than the rest. Three stones of equal size read as a wall, not a landscape.
  • Same direction. Tilt all the stones the same way, as if wind or water had shaped them together, and line up the grain or strata on each stone.
  • Different distances. Set the stones at different depths, front to back, so the view has layers.
  • Leave negative space. Open carpet or sand is part of the design. Resist filling it.
  • Bury the base. Set each stone a third or so into the substrate so it looks rooted, not placed on top.

These are starting points, not rules. A single dramatic stone can carry a nano tank, and some large layouts use dozens of small stones in a "mountain range" (sansui) arrangement.

Best Rocks for Iwagumi

  • Seiryu stone: grey-blue with white veins and sharp ridges; the classic choice. It contains calcium carbonate and raises KH, GH and pH over time, which matters for soft-water fish and shrimp.
  • Ohko (dragon stone): clay-coloured with deep holes; inert, so it doesn't change water chemistry. Rinse it well, because the pockets hold dried clay.
  • Lava rock: light, porous and inert; easy for large tanks because it weighs little.
  • Unzan or Manten stone: rounded, softer-looking; inert or nearly so.

Use one rock type only. Mixing types is one of the quickest ways to make an iwagumi look like a pile of rocks. Test any stone you collect yourself first; the hardscape guide explains how.

Traditional iwagumi uses one carpet species, sometimes two. For a low-tech iwagumi, use dwarf sagittaria or a sand foreground with moss on the stones; it won't look like a contest tank, but it will stay healthy without CO₂.

Dry start method: plant the carpet into damp soil, cover the tank with cling film and run the light for 4–6 weeks before flooding. The carpet roots in without algae competition. Mist daily and open the cover for a few minutes to stop mould.

Substrate, CO₂ and Light

  • Substrate: nutrient-rich aqua soil, about 2 inches at the front rising to 4 inches at the back. Carpets need the nutrients in the soil, and the slope adds depth.
  • CO₂: with no stem plants to soak up nutrients, an iwagumi relies on a healthy carpet to compete with algae, and carpets grow well only with CO₂. Aim for a drop checker that turns lime green by the time the lights come on, and switch CO₂ off an hour before lights-out.
  • Light: medium to high. Start at 6 hours a day and add 30 minutes a week up to 7–8 hours once the carpet is spreading.
  • Flow: enough to move the surface and the carpet gently everywhere; dead spots grow algae first.

Fish and Shrimp for an Iwagumi

One school of a single small species keeps the minimalist look and makes the stones look larger. Good picks are harlequin rasboras, ember tetras, chili rasboras in nano tanks, neon tetras and white cloud minnows. Amano shrimp and otocinclus help with algae on the stones and carpet.

Avoid fish that dig or graze carpets (goldfish, most cichlids, large plecos), and keep in mind that seiryu stone hardens the water, which suits rasboras and white clouds but not soft-water shrimp such as crystal reds.

Keeping an Iwagumi Clean

Algae is the main enemy, because there is no hardscape or plant mass to hide it. The first two months decide whether the layout succeeds:

  • Weeks 1–4: 50% water changes two or three times a week; new aqua soil leaches ammonia, which feeds algae and harms fish. Fish go in only after the tank has cycled (see how to cycle a fish tank).
  • Weekly after that: one 30–50% change, a scrub of the glass and a light vacuum over open sand.
  • Trim the carpet once it is thick, cutting it back to about half its height with curved scissors, so the lower layer doesn't die off and lift.
  • Watch the stones. Green spot or hair algae on the stones usually means light is ahead of CO₂. Cut the photoperiod and check the drop checker before reaching for chemicals. See how to get rid of algae.

Iwagumi Mistakes to Avoid

  • Stones of similar size, or an even number of them.
  • The main stone in the centre.
  • Mixing rock types, or adding wood.
  • Planting the carpet too sparsely, then waiting months for it to fill.
  • Long photoperiods before the carpet is established.
  • A mixed community of fish: it breaks the calm look and adds waste.

Iwagumi FAQ

What is an iwagumi aquascape?

An iwagumi is a Japanese aquascaping style that uses only stones, usually an odd number of one rock type, set in a carpet of short plants with plenty of open space. The name means "rock formation".

How many stones should an iwagumi have?

An odd number, usually 3 in tanks up to 10 gallons, 3–5 in 20 gallons and 5–7 or more in larger tanks, with one stone clearly larger than the rest.

Can you do an iwagumi without CO₂?

You can, but the classic carpets (HC Cuba, Glossostigma, dense Monte Carlo) grow poorly without CO₂ and algae tends to win. A low-tech version with dwarf sagittaria or a sand foreground and moss works better.

What is the best rock for iwagumi?

Seiryu stone is the classic choice for its ridges and veins, but it raises water hardness and pH. Ohko (dragon) stone and lava rock are inert alternatives.

Does seiryu stone raise pH?

Yes. Seiryu contains calcium carbonate and slowly raises KH, GH and pH. Test your water after adding it, and avoid it with soft-water shrimp or fish that need low pH.

What fish are best for an iwagumi tank?

One school of a single small species, such as harlequin rasboras, ember tetras, chili rasboras or white cloud mountain minnows, plus Amano shrimp or otocinclus for algae.

Why is my iwagumi getting algae?

Usually because the carpet hasn't filled in yet, the light runs too long, CO₂ is low, or new aqua soil is still leaching ammonia. Shorten the photoperiod, change water more often and check CO₂.
