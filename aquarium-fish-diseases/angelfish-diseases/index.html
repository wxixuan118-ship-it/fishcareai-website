Angelfish Health Problems: Symptoms, Diseases & Treatment

Match what you see on your freshwater angelfish (Pterophyllum) to the likely cause, then use the 30 symptom guides to treat it.

← Back to All Fish Diseases

Freshwater angelfish (Pterophyllum scalare) are hardier than discus but they are still Amazon cichlids, and most angelfish health problems come from the same three sources: water that has drifted cold, hard or high in nitrate; long fins that get torn and then infected; and parasites carried in by new fish. Tank-bred angelfish also inherit a few problems of their own, from Hexamita to the so-called angelfish virus.

This hub is the index for angelfish health problems on FishCare AI. Use the symptom finder to jump to the guide that matches what you see, read the profiles of the diseases angelfish actually get, and run the checklist before you medicate. The angelfish care guide covers the routine that prevents most of them, and angelfish health problems on the symptom checker is the interactive version of this page. Marine angelfish (emperor, flame, French, Koran, bicolor) have their own hubs listed at the bottom.

Treat as urgent: an angelfish lying on its side, gasping at the surface with clamped fins, or with scales standing out from the body (dropsy). These progress within a day or two and need a hospital tank immediately.

Most Common Angelfish Health Problems and Diseases

These are the conditions behind the symptoms above, in roughly the order a angelfish keeper is likely to meet them.

Ich (white spot) in Angelfish

The most common angelfish disease. Salt-grain spots on the fins first, then the body; the fish flashes against décor and clamps its fins. It usually follows a temperature drop or a new fish. Raise the temperature to 84–86°F, add aeration, and treat the whole tank for at least ten days so the free-swimming stage is killed.

Symptom guides: Angelfish white spots → Angelfish spots on fins → Angelfish rubbing / flashing → Angelfish clamped fins →

Fin rot and torn fins in Angelfish

Angelfish fins are long and easily damaged by nipping tank mates, sharp décor or a net. Once torn, Aeromonas or Flavobacterium turn the edge milky, then ragged and red. Clean water alone heals torn fins; fin rot with a white or red edge needs an antibacterial in a hospital tank.

Symptom guides: Angelfish fin rot → Angelfish torn fins → Angelfish red streaks →

Hexamita and hole-in-the-head in Angelfish

Like discus, angelfish carry Hexamita at low levels and break down with it under stress: white stringy faeces, refusing food, a dark body and, later, pits on the head. Metronidazole in food or a hospital tank, with nitrate pulled below 20 ppm, resolves early cases.

Symptom guides: Angelfish not eating → Angelfish sunken belly → Angelfish losing color → Angelfish hiding →

Internal parasites (Capillaria, tapeworm) in Angelfish

An angelfish that eats but stays thin, or passes clear or white faeces, usually has nematodes or tapeworms. Wild-caught and farm-raised fish both carry them. Levamisole (nematodes) or praziquantel (tapeworms) chosen after looking at the faeces; repeat in two weeks.

Symptom guides: Angelfish sunken belly → Angelfish lethargic → Angelfish pale color →

Swim bladder and bloat in Angelfish

Floating head-up, sinking to the bottom or tilting sideways in angelfish is most often the swim bladder squeezed by a swollen gut: overfeeding dry food, constipation, or an internal infection. Fast for two days and feed a blanched pea or daphnia. If the belly swells and scales lift (dropsy), it is systemic and needs antibiotics plus Epsom salt.

Symptom guides: Angelfish floating → Angelfish sinking → Angelfish swimming sideways → Angelfish bloated →

Velvet (Oodinium) and gill flukes in Angelfish

Rapid breathing, coughing and a fine gold-to-grey dust on the body point to velvet; rapid breathing with rubbing and no dust points to gill flukes. Velvet is treated like ich with the lights off; flukes need praziquantel.

Symptom guides: Angelfish rapid breathing → Angelfish coughing → Angelfish gasping → Angelfish mucus coating →

Bacterial ulcers, pop-eye and cloudy eyes in Angelfish

Missing scales with a red or white crater, one or both eyes bulging, or a milky eye all point to bacteria taking hold on a stressed fish. Cloudy eyes after a water-quality dip clear with water changes; ulcers and pop-eye need a hospital tank and a broad-spectrum antibiotic.

Symptom guides: Angelfish missing scales → Angelfish bulging eyes → Angelfish cloudy eyes → Angelfish fuzzy growth →

Angelfish virus and aggression injuries in Angelfish

"Angelfish virus" or angelfish plague is a fast-moving infection that arrives with new fish: every angelfish goes dark, slimy and clamped and hangs at the surface. There is no direct cure, only support and quarantine. Separately, angelfish pair up and defend territory, so many torn fins, black spots and missing scales are simply fight damage.

Symptom guides: Angelfish aggressive → Angelfish darting → Angelfish black spots → Angelfish torn fins →

Why Angelfish Get Sick: The Conditions Behind Most Angelfish Health Problems

Very few angelfish health problems start as a disease. They start as a husbandry gap that a parasite or bacterium then exploits. Close these and most of the list above never appears.

  1. Temperature swings — Angelfish want a steady 76–82°F (24–28°C). Ich outbreaks almost always follow a drop of a few degrees from a failed heater, a cold water change or a winter room. See angelfish temperature.
  2. Nitrate and stale water — Angelfish tolerate nitrate up to about 40 ppm but Hexamita blooms and fins start to fray long before that. A weekly 25–30% change on a lightly stocked tank keeps nitrate under 20 ppm.
  3. Tank too small or too short — An adult angelfish is 6 inches tall with fins; a tank under 18 inches high or under 29 gallons for a pair means constant fin damage and fighting. Use the angelfish tank size calculator.
  4. Fin-nipping tank mates — Tiger barbs, serpae tetras and some danios shred angelfish fins, and every tear is a route for fin rot. The angelfish tank mates guide and compatibility ranking list the safe ones.
  5. Pairs and aggression — Two angelfish that pair off will bully the rest of the group, and a lone bullied fish stops eating and hides. Keep six or more, or a single pair; see are angelfish aggressive.
  6. Diet — Flake-only diets lead to constipation and thin fish. Rotate quality pellet with frozen bloodworm, brine shrimp and daphnia; the angelfish diet guide has a schedule.

Before You Treat Angelfish Health Problems: Checklist

Rule out water quality first. Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and temperature explain the majority of angelfish symptoms. Medicating a fish whose real problem is the water wastes money and adds stress.
  1. Test water: Ammonia 0 ppm, nitrite 0 ppm, nitrate under 20 ppm, pH 6.5–7.5, temperature 76–82°F. Angelfish usually show cloudy eyes or clamped fins within a day of an ammonia spike.
  2. Check the fins: Tears with clean edges are injury and heal on their own; milky or red edges are fin rot; white dots on the fins are ich.
  3. Look at the faeces: White, clear or stringy faeces mean internal parasites and change the treatment completely.
  4. Watch the group: One fish bullied into a corner is a social problem, not a disease; every fish gasping is oxygen or water; spots spreading fish to fish is ich or velvet.
  5. Set up a hospital tank: A bare 10–20 gallon tank at 82°F with an air stone and a seeded sponge filter is where antibiotics, metronidazole and wormers go, never the display tank.
  6. One treatment at a time: Identify the problem from the symptom guide, treat it, and reassess after five days before adding a second medication.

Angelfish Health Problems: Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What are the most common angelfish health problems?

A: Ich, fin rot on torn fins, Hexamita (white stringy faeces, then hole-in-the-head), internal worms that keep a fish thin, and swim-bladder trouble from overfeeding cover most angelfish health problems in home tanks. Nearly all of them follow a temperature drop, rising nitrate, fin damage from tank mates, or a new fish that was not quarantined.

Q: Why is my angelfish lying on its side or floating?

A: A swollen gut pressing on the swim bladder is the usual cause: too much dry food, constipation, or an internal infection. Fast the fish for two days, then feed a blanched pea or daphnia. If the belly is swollen and the scales stand out, it is dropsy and needs antibiotics in a hospital tank. See the angelfish swimming sideways guide.

Q: Why are my angelfish's fins ragged or clamped?

A: Ragged fins with clean tears are injury from tank mates or décor and regrow in clean water. A milky or red edge that keeps receding is fin rot and needs an antibacterial. Clamped fins on their own are stress, usually from a water-quality problem or the first stage of ich.

Q: What does hole-in-the-head look like on an angelfish?

A: Small pits above the eyes and along the lateral line, often with a dark body, refusal to eat and white stringy faeces. It is caused by Hexamita and made worse by nitrate. Treat with metronidazole and daily water changes; early pits heal within a few weeks.

Q: What temperature prevents angelfish diseases?

A: A steady 76–82°F (24–28°C). Angelfish tolerate the range, but a sudden drop of a few degrees is the most common trigger for ich. 84–86°F is the treatment temperature for ich and velvet; keep it there for the full course, not just until spots vanish.

Q: Can I treat angelfish in the main tank?

A: Ich, velvet and water-quality problems are treated in the display tank, because the parasite is in the whole tank. Antibiotics, metronidazole and wormers go in a hospital tank so they do not harm the filter or the other fish. Move the sick fish, not the medication.

Q: Why is my angelfish turning black or losing its stripes?

A: Angelfish change contrast with mood: bars fade when the fish is relaxed or asleep and darken when it is stressed or dominant. A fish that stays very dark, clamps its fins and hides is stressed by water quality, bullying or the start of Hexamita. Faded, washed-out colour with fast breathing points to velvet or poor water.

Q: Is the angelfish virus real and can it be cured?

A: Yes. Angelfish virus (angelfish plague) is a fast-spreading infection, thought to be viral, that turns every angelfish in a tank dark, slimy and listless within days of a new fish arriving. There is no cure; keep water perfect, raise the temperature slightly, treat secondary bacterial infections, and quarantine all new angelfish for four weeks so it never gets in.
