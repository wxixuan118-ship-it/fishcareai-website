Quick answer

What diseases affect maroon clownfish?

Most maroon clownfish diseases come from stress and a handful of marine parasites. Maroon clownfish are the largest and most aggressive clownfish, so torn fins, bite marks and stress from constant fighting cause many of the problems owners see. They are also a documented host of Brooklynella, the slime-producing parasite, and are just as vulnerable to velvet and marine ich as any reef fish.[1]

Act the same day if your maroon clownfish breathes fast and shows dust, slime or peeling skin. Velvet and Brooklynella can kill within days; move the fish to a quarantine tank and start treatment.

For any other symptom, test ammonia, nitrite, salinity and temperature first — water problems cause more illness than any single parasite.

Species risk profile

Why maroon clownfish get sick

Maroons (Premnas biaculeatus) have traditionally been placed in their own genus, Premnas, and are recognizable by the spines on their cheeks. Females can reach about 17 cm and will fight other clownfish, their mate and even the owner's hand.

Blasiola lists Premnas biaculeatus as one of the anemonefish hosts of Brooklynella.

Because injuries are common, check a sick maroon for bite wounds before assuming a parasite — but never ignore fast breathing or slime.[1]

Symptoms

Maroon Clownfish symptoms and what they mean

Most maroon clownfish diseases first show up as one of eight symptom groups, covering the 30 signs owners describe most often. Find the closest match, check the urgency label, then confirm the likely disease in the table below.

Breathing problemsEmergency

Covers: gasping, rapid breathing, coughing

Fast gill movement, gasping at the surface or "coughing" (sudden flaring of the gills) is the most important sign on this page. Gill parasites — velvet, Brooklynella and flukes — attack the gills first, often before anything shows on the skin, and low oxygen or ammonia produce the same picture.[1][2]

Maroon note: Maroons are big, high-oxygen fish. Rapid breathing in a crowded or warm tank may be oxygen, not disease.

Spots, slime and skin lesionsUrgent

Covers: white spots, black spots, spots on fins, fuzzy growth, mucus coating, missing scales, red streaks

Distinct salt-grain dots point to marine ich; a fine gold-grey dust to velvet; thick, peeling slime to Brooklynella. Red streaks and open sores are usually bacterial or Uronema infections taking hold after an injury. Firm, white, wart-like lumps on the body or fins fit lymphocystis, a virus documented in captive clownfish.[1][2][3][4]

Maroon note: Bite wounds and torn skin heal well in clean water; red, spreading edges mean infection or Uronema has taken hold.

Not eating, bloating or a sunken bellyUrgent

Covers: not eating, sunken belly, bloated

Refusing food for a day or two after a move is normal. Refusing food in an established fish is often the first sign of a parasite. A swollen belly points to overfeeding, constipation or internal infection; a pinched, sunken belly to long-term underfeeding or internal parasites.

Maroon note: A maroon recovering from a fight may sulk and skip food for several days. Persistent refusal with other symptoms is a disease sign.

Swimming sideways, sinking or floatingUrgent

Covers: swimming sideways, floating, sinking, curled body, darting, jumping

Losing balance — floating, sinking, swimming sideways — usually means a swim bladder problem or an advanced infection. Darting and jumping are signs of irritation (parasites) or of being chased. A curved spine that develops over weeks can be nutritional or degenerative rather than infectious.[5]

Maroon note: Swimming sideways after a fight can be injury or exhaustion. If it lasts more than a day, or the body starts to curve, isolate the fish.

Cloudy or bulging eyesUrgent

Covers: bulging eyes, cloudy eyes

One cloudy or swollen eye is usually an injury. Both eyes together suggest infection, flukes or poor water quality. Corneal cloudiness was one of the first signs of skin flukes in imported reef fish.[6]

Maroon note: Maroons often damage an eye on rockwork while fighting; one cloudy eye is usually trauma.

Torn, clamped or rotting finsUrgent

Covers: fin rot, torn fins, clamped fins

Clean tears are fight damage; ragged, whitish edges that keep receding are fin rot. Fins held tight to the body (clamped) are a general stress sign — check water quality before anything else.

Maroon note: Fin damage is expected in maroon pairs. Watch that it heals within two weeks.

Hiding, lethargy, scratching or aggressionWatch

Covers: hiding, lethargic, aggressive, rubbing

Scratching on rock ("flashing") is an early parasite sign and deserves a close look under a flashlight. Hiding and lethargy are vague on their own; combine them with breathing and skin checks.[3]

Maroon note: Aggression is normal. A maroon that suddenly stops defending its territory is unwell.

Losing color or turning paleWatch

Covers: losing color, pale color

Fading is a stress response — a new tank, bullying, unstable temperature or salinity. Pale color together with excess slime and fast breathing is the classic Brooklynella picture and is an emergency.[1]

Maroon note: Maroons darken with age and mood. Sudden pale patches with slime are the warning sign.

Treating a maroon clownfish

Use the largest quarantine tank you can — 30 gallons for an adult female maroon — and keep it alone. Maroons are strong and fast; plan how you will catch the fish before you start daily baths.

Copper is effective against ich and velvet only while free copper stays at 0.15–0.20 mg/L, so measure it with a test kit at least daily; it is lethal to most invertebrates and must never go into a reef tank.[7] Leave the display without fish while you treat: marine ich can complete its life cycle in anything from 6 days to 11 weeks, and its guidance calls for 3–6 weeks of quarantine, sometimes 7–11.[3]

Prevention

How to keep maroon clownfish healthy

Temp74–80°F
Salinity1.020–1.025
pH8.0–8.4
Ammonia / NO₂0 ppm
Nitrate< 20 ppm

Most maroon clownfish diseases arrive with a new fish, so quarantine every new fish for at least four weeks before it joins your maroon clownfish, buy captive-bred where you can, and keep temperature and salinity steady. Feed a varied marine diet in small portions; a fish in good condition fights off parasites that would overwhelm a stressed one.

Maroon Clownfish diseases FAQ

Why is my maroon clownfish swimming sideways?

After a fight it can be injury or exhaustion and usually passes within a day. If it continues, or the fish also breathes fast or stops eating, isolate it and look for infection or parasites.

Are maroon clownfish more prone to disease?

Not inherently, but their aggression means more injuries, and injuries let infections in. They are also a documented Brooklynella host.

Can I treat my maroon clownfish in the display tank?

Not for parasites. Copper and formalin harm invertebrates and corals, and parasites survive in a display that still has fish. Use a hospital tank.
