Quick answer

What diseases affect tomato clownfish?

Most tomato clownfish diseases come from stress and a handful of marine parasites. Tomato clownfish are tough but aggressive, so many of their health problems start as injuries — torn fins, missing scales and bite wounds that then become infected. They also get the classic marine parasites: Brooklynella was recorded in tomato clownfish during an importer outbreak described by Blasiola, and velvet and marine ich are just as dangerous for them as for smaller clowns.[1]

Act the same day if your tomato clownfish breathes fast and shows dust, slime or peeling skin. Velvet and Brooklynella can kill within days; move the fish to a quarantine tank and start treatment.

For any other symptom, test ammonia, nitrite, salinity and temperature first — water problems cause more illness than any single parasite.

Species risk profile

Why tomato clownfish get sick

Tomato clownfish (Amphiprion frenatus) grow larger than ocellaris and females become strongly territorial. Fights with tank mates or a mismatched partner are the most common source of wounds.

Wounds are the entry point for bacteria and for Uronema, a ciliate that eats into damaged skin. Treating the injury early prevents the infection.

Blasiola describes a 1979 Brooklynella outbreak in tomato clownfish at an importer — a reminder that wild-caught tomatoes need a full quarantine.[1][2]

Symptoms

Tomato Clownfish symptoms and what they mean

Most tomato clownfish diseases first show up as one of eight symptom groups, covering the 30 signs owners describe most often. Find the closest match, check the urgency label, then confirm the likely disease in the table below.

Breathing problemsEmergency

Covers: gasping, rapid breathing, coughing

Fast gill movement, gasping at the surface or "coughing" (sudden flaring of the gills) is the most important sign on this page. Gill parasites — velvet, Brooklynella and flukes — attack the gills first, often before anything shows on the skin, and low oxygen or ammonia produce the same picture.[1][3]

Tomato note: Tomatoes gasping at the surface together is more often an oxygen or temperature problem than disease — check the heater and aeration first.

Spots, slime and skin lesionsUrgent

Covers: white spots, black spots, spots on fins, fuzzy growth, mucus coating, missing scales, red streaks

Distinct salt-grain dots point to marine ich; a fine gold-grey dust to velvet; thick, peeling slime to Brooklynella. Red streaks and open sores are usually bacterial or Uronema infections taking hold after an injury. Firm, white, wart-like lumps on the body or fins fit lymphocystis, a virus documented in captive clownfish.[1][3][4][5]

Tomato note: Missing scales and a white, fuzzy-looking edge around a wound mean infection is starting in an injury. Move the fish away from the aggressor and treat in quarantine.

Not eating, bloating or a sunken bellyUrgent

Covers: not eating, sunken belly, bloated

Refusing food for a day or two after a move is normal. Refusing food in an established fish is often the first sign of a parasite. A swollen belly points to overfeeding, constipation or internal infection; a pinched, sunken belly to long-term underfeeding or internal parasites.

Tomato note: A dominant tomato may block a subordinate from food. Watch feeding time before assuming disease.

Swimming sideways, sinking or floatingUrgent

Covers: swimming sideways, floating, sinking, curled body, darting, jumping

Losing balance — floating, sinking, swimming sideways — usually means a swim bladder problem or an advanced infection. Darting and jumping are signs of irritation (parasites) or of being chased. A curved spine that develops over weeks can be nutritional or degenerative rather than infectious.[6]

Tomato note: Darting and jumping are common in tomatoes being chased by a female. A lid prevents jumping deaths.

Cloudy or bulging eyesUrgent

Covers: bulging eyes, cloudy eyes

One cloudy or swollen eye is usually an injury. Both eyes together suggest infection, flukes or poor water quality. Corneal cloudiness was one of the first signs of skin flukes in imported reef fish.[7]

Tomato note: Single cloudy eyes in tomatoes are usually bite or scrape injuries from rockwork during fights.

Torn, clamped or rotting finsUrgent

Covers: fin rot, torn fins, clamped fins

Clean tears are fight damage; ragged, whitish edges that keep receding are fin rot. Fins held tight to the body (clamped) are a general stress sign — check water quality before anything else.

Tomato note: Expect some fin nipping in a pair; ragged, receding edges that spread are fin rot.

Hiding, lethargy, scratching or aggressionWatch

Covers: hiding, lethargic, aggressive, rubbing

Scratching on rock ("flashing") is an early parasite sign and deserves a close look under a flashlight. Hiding and lethargy are vague on their own; combine them with breathing and skin checks.[4]

Tomato note: Aggression is normal for a tomato. Constant hiding by a subordinate is not, and stress lowers resistance to parasites.

Losing color or turning paleWatch

Covers: losing color, pale color

Fading is a stress response — a new tank, bullying, unstable temperature or salinity. Pale color together with excess slime and fast breathing is the classic Brooklynella picture and is an emergency.[1]

Tomato note: Juvenile tomatoes can show two or three white bars that fade to one head bar as they mature — that is normal color change, not disease.

Treating a tomato clownfish

Give tomatoes a 20 gallon or larger quarantine tank with several hiding pipes; they stress and bite in cramped tanks. Never quarantine two unpaired tomatoes together.

Copper is effective against ich and velvet only while free copper stays at 0.15–0.20 mg/L, so measure it with a test kit at least daily; it is lethal to most invertebrates and must never go into a reef tank.[8] Leave the display without fish while you treat: marine ich can complete its life cycle in anything from 6 days to 11 weeks, and its guidance calls for 3–6 weeks of quarantine, sometimes 7–11.[4]

Prevention

How to keep tomato clownfish healthy

Temp74–80°F
Salinity1.020–1.025
pH8.0–8.4
Ammonia / NO₂0 ppm
Nitrate< 20 ppm

Most tomato clownfish diseases arrive with a new fish, so quarantine every new fish for at least four weeks before it joins your tomato clownfish, buy captive-bred where you can, and keep temperature and salinity steady. Feed a varied marine diet in small portions; a fish in good condition fights off parasites that would overwhelm a stressed one.

Tomato Clownfish diseases FAQ

Why is my tomato clownfish gasping?

Check oxygen and temperature first: tomatoes gasping together usually means low oxygen. A single fish gasping with spots, dust or slime points to gill parasites such as velvet or Brooklynella.

What is the fuzzy growth on my tomato clownfish?

A white, fuzzy edge around a wound is usually a bacterial infection starting in an injury. Separate the fish from the aggressor and treat in a hospital tank.

Are tomato clownfish hardy?

Yes, but they are aggressive. Most of their health problems begin as fight injuries that become infected.
