Quick answer

What diseases affect ocellaris clownfish?

Most ocellaris clownfish diseases come from stress and a handful of marine parasites. Captive-bred ocellaris clownfish are among the hardiest marine fish, so most problems trace back to stress: a new tank, an uncycled quarantine, unstable salinity or a bullying tank mate. The diseases that do appear are the same marine parasites that affect other reef fish — marine ich, velvet and Brooklynella — plus lymphocystis, a virus that has been confirmed in ocellaris clownfish in breeding facilities.[1][2]

Act the same day if your ocellaris clownfish breathes fast and shows dust, slime or peeling skin. Velvet and Brooklynella can kill within days; move the fish to a quarantine tank and start treatment.

For any other symptom, test ammonia, nitrite, salinity and temperature first — water problems cause more illness than any single parasite.

Species risk profile

Why ocellaris clownfish get sick

Ocellaris are the clownfish most people buy first, so they show up most often in beginners' tanks — where an uncycled system, overfeeding and skipped quarantine cause most illness.

Being farmed does not make them immune. A hatchery report from ICAR-CMFRI found Amyloodinium (velvet) in three of eight captive ocellaris, and a 2022 study in Taiwan confirmed lymphocystis virus in ocellaris kept in a breeding room.

The good news: a healthy, captive-bred ocellaris that eats well and lives in a stable tank rarely gets sick. Most cases follow a new arrival that was not quarantined.[1][2]

Symptoms

Ocellaris Clownfish symptoms and what they mean

Most ocellaris clownfish diseases first show up as one of eight symptom groups, covering the 30 signs owners describe most often. Find the closest match, check the urgency label, then confirm the likely disease in the table below.

Breathing problemsEmergency

Covers: gasping, rapid breathing, coughing

Fast gill movement, gasping at the surface or "coughing" (sudden flaring of the gills) is the most important sign on this page. Gill parasites — velvet, Brooklynella and flukes — attack the gills first, often before anything shows on the skin, and low oxygen or ammonia produce the same picture.[3][4]

Ocellaris note: In ocellaris, rapid breathing in the first two weeks after purchase is the main warning sign of velvet carried in from the shop.

Spots, slime and skin lesionsUrgent

Covers: white spots, black spots, spots on fins, fuzzy growth, mucus coating, missing scales, red streaks

Distinct salt-grain dots point to marine ich; a fine gold-grey dust to velvet; thick, peeling slime to Brooklynella. Red streaks and open sores are usually bacterial or Uronema infections taking hold after an injury. Firm, white, wart-like lumps on the body or fins fit lymphocystis, a virus documented in captive clownfish.[2][3][4][5]

Ocellaris note: Raised white nodules that stay put for weeks fit lymphocystis, which Cheng et al. described in ocellaris as sparse, white, papilloma-like nodules on the body, gills and fins. It is unsightly but usually resolves with good water quality.

Not eating, bloating or a sunken bellyUrgent

Covers: not eating, sunken belly, bloated

Refusing food for a day or two after a move is normal. Refusing food in an established fish is often the first sign of a parasite. A swollen belly points to overfeeding, constipation or internal infection; a pinched, sunken belly to long-term underfeeding or internal parasites.

Ocellaris note: New ocellaris often hide and skip meals for 2–3 days. Offer small portions of frozen mysis or quality pellets twice a day and remove leftovers.

Swimming sideways, sinking or floatingUrgent

Covers: swimming sideways, floating, sinking, curled body, darting, jumping

Losing balance — floating, sinking, swimming sideways — usually means a swim bladder problem or an advanced infection. Darting and jumping are signs of irritation (parasites) or of being chased. A curved spine that develops over weeks can be nutritional or degenerative rather than infectious.[6]

Ocellaris note: Young, fast-growing ocellaris fed large meals are prone to temporary buoyancy trouble. Fast 24 hours, then feed smaller portions.

Cloudy or bulging eyesUrgent

Covers: bulging eyes, cloudy eyes

One cloudy or swollen eye is usually an injury. Both eyes together suggest infection, flukes or poor water quality. Corneal cloudiness was one of the first signs of skin flukes in imported reef fish.[7]

Ocellaris note: Ocellaris pairs rarely fight hard enough to damage eyes, so cloudy eyes in both eyes deserve a closer look for flukes.

Torn, clamped or rotting finsUrgent

Covers: fin rot, torn fins, clamped fins

Clean tears are fight damage; ragged, whitish edges that keep receding are fin rot. Fins held tight to the body (clamped) are a general stress sign — check water quality before anything else.

Ocellaris note: Clamped fins in a single ocellaris are usually stress from a dominant tank mate — often the larger female of the pair.

Hiding, lethargy, scratching or aggressionWatch

Covers: hiding, lethargic, aggressive, rubbing

Scratching on rock ("flashing") is an early parasite sign and deserves a close look under a flashlight. Hiding and lethargy are vague on their own; combine them with breathing and skin checks.[5]

Ocellaris note: The side-to-side "wiggle" near a favorite spot is normal ocellaris behavior, not a symptom.

Losing color or turning paleWatch

Covers: losing color, pale color

Fading is a stress response — a new tank, bullying, unstable temperature or salinity. Pale color together with excess slime and fast breathing is the classic Brooklynella picture and is an emergency.[4]

Ocellaris note: Captive-bred ocellaris may look slightly duller than wild fish; that is genetics, not illness. Sudden fading is the warning sign.

Treating a ocellaris clownfish

Ocellaris tolerate a bare 10–20 gallon quarantine tank well. Their small size makes them easy to net for formalin baths, but they stress quickly when chased — use a cup or a second net. Keep a PVC pipe in the tank so they can hide.

Copper is effective against ich and velvet only while free copper stays at 0.15–0.20 mg/L, so measure it with a test kit at least daily; it is lethal to most invertebrates and must never go into a reef tank.[8] Leave the display without fish while you treat: marine ich can complete its life cycle in anything from 6 days to 11 weeks, and its guidance calls for 3–6 weeks of quarantine, sometimes 7–11.[5]

Most ocellaris clownfish diseases arrive with a new fish, so quarantine every new fish for at least four weeks before it joins your ocellaris clownfish, buy captive-bred where you can, and keep temperature and salinity steady. Feed a varied marine diet in small portions; a fish in good condition fights off parasites that would overwhelm a stressed one.

Ocellaris Clownfish diseases FAQ

Are ocellaris clownfish prone to disease?

No — captive-bred ocellaris are hardy. Most illness follows stress or a new, unquarantined fish. They can still catch marine ich, velvet and Brooklynella, and hatchery stock has been documented with velvet and lymphocystis.

What are the white bumps on my ocellaris clownfish?

Firm white lumps that do not move or spread quickly may be lymphocystis, a virus reported in ocellaris clownfish. Tiny salt-grain dots that come and go are more likely marine ich.

Why is my new ocellaris clownfish not eating?

Most new ocellaris start eating within 2–3 days. If it still refuses food after that and also breathes fast or shows spots, treat it as a parasite problem.
