Disease guide

Aquarium Fish Diseases, Symptoms and Treatment

Identify what's wrong with your fish, compare symptoms across common aquarium diseases, and find the right treatment before it spreads.

How to diagnose a sick fish

Step 1 — Check water quality first

Most fish disease outbreaks start with poor water conditions. Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, and temperature before treating. A reading out of range is usually the cause, not a secondary symptom.

Step 2 — Observe the fish closely

Look at the body surface (spots, lesions, discoloration, texture), fins (fraying, clamping, holes), eyes (cloudiness, bulging), and behavior (gasping, flashing, hiding, listing, not eating).

Step 3 — Match to a disease by symptoms

Use the symptom table and disease profiles below to narrow down the cause. Many diseases look similar — frequency, spread rate, and water conditions help distinguish them.

Step 4 — Treat in a quarantine tank

Move the affected fish to a separate tank before treating. This protects other tank residents from both the disease and the medication, and lets you observe progress without disrupting the main tank.

Common aquarium fish diseases

Ich (White Spot Disease)

Urgent

Symptoms: Small white salt-like spots covering body and fins, flashing against objects, clamped fins, lethargy.

See betta ich guide →

Fin Rot

Monitor

Symptoms: Frayed or ragged fin edges, milky or reddened fin tissue, progressive fin loss from tips inward.

See betta fin rot guide →

Velvet (Oodinium)

Urgent

Symptoms: Gold or rust-coloured dust on body (visible in torchlight), rapid gill movement, rubbing, extreme lethargy.

See guppy velvet guide →

Swim Bladder Disease

Monitor

Symptoms: Fish floating on side or upside down, sinking to the bottom, difficulty maintaining position in the water column.

See betta swim bladder guide →

Dropsy

Emergency

Symptoms: Swollen abdomen, raised scales (pinecone appearance), protruding eyes, lethargy. Often an internal bacterial infection.

See betta dropsy guide →

Popeye (Exophthalmia)

Monitor

Symptoms: One or both eyes bulging outward, cloudiness around the eye, may be accompanied by swelling of the orbital socket.

See betta popeye guide →

Columnaris (Cotton Mouth)

Urgent

Symptoms: White or grey cottony patches on mouth, body sores with a saddle-like shape across the back, rapid deterioration.

See guppy columnaris guide →

Hole-in-Head Disease (HITH)

Monitor

Symptoms: Small pits or holes on the head and lateral line, most common in cichlids; linked to nutritional deficiencies and activated carbon use.

See oscar HITH guide →

Anchor Worm

Monitor

Symptoms: Visible thread-like parasites anchored in the skin, redness and inflammation at attachment sites, affected fish flash against objects.

See goldfish anchor worm guide →

Fungal Infection

Monitor

Symptoms: White or grey fluffy cotton-like growth on body, fins, or wounds; often secondary to physical injury or bacterial infection.

See betta fungal infection guide →

Gill Flukes

Urgent

Symptoms: Rapid or laboured breathing, gasping at surface, clamped fins, scratching gills against objects. Flukes are microscopic parasites.

See goldfish gill flukes guide →

Bacterial Septicaemia

Emergency

Symptoms: Red streaks or haemorrhaging under skin, bloody fins, severe lethargy, loss of appetite. Internal systemic bacterial infection.

See goldfish bacterial infection guide →

Preventing aquarium fish diseases

Quarantine new fish

Place every new fish in a separate quarantine tank for at least two weeks before adding to the display tank. Most diseases are introduced by new arrivals.

Maintain stable water parameters

Ammonia and nitrite must be zero. Test weekly, do regular partial water changes, and make sure your filter is cycled before adding fish.

Do not overcrowd

Overstocked tanks accumulate waste faster, stress fish (lowering immunity), and give diseases a larger population to spread through. Use the compatibility checker when planning your stock.

Varied, quality diet

Malnourished fish are far more susceptible to disease. Rotate between high-quality flakes or pellets, frozen foods, and live foods matched to the species.

Frequently asked questions

The earliest signs are behavioural: hiding more than usual, not eating, gasping at the surface, or swimming abnormally. Physical signs come next — clamped fins, pale colour, white spots, sores, or a swollen abdomen. Check water quality first, then observe the fish closely for 24 hours before deciding on treatment.
Ich (Ichthyophthirius multifiliis) is the most common aquarium fish disease worldwide. It appears as small white dots, similar to salt crystals, across the body and fins. It is highly contagious but treatable if caught early by raising the temperature and using a dedicated ich medication.
Most aquarium fish diseases are species-specific and pose no risk to humans. The one exception to be aware of is Mycobacterium marinum (fish tank granuloma), a skin infection that can occur through open wounds after contact with aquarium water. Wearing gloves when working in a tank with fish showing symptoms is a simple precaution.
For contagious diseases like ich and velvet, treat the entire tank — the parasite spends most of its lifecycle in the water and substrate, not on the fish. For bacterial infections and injuries, quarantine the affected fish and treat in a separate tank to protect healthy fish from medication and cross-infection.
Treatment times vary by disease. Ich typically clears in 7–14 days at elevated temperature with medication. Fin rot may take 1–3 weeks. Bacterial infections often respond within 5–10 days if caught early. Dropsy and severe septicaemia are harder to cure; early diagnosis greatly improves outcomes.
