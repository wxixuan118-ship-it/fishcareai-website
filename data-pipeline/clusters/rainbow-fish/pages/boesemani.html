Boesemani rainbowfish care is straightforward if you give the fish the three things it cannot do without: a school of six or more, a tank 3–4 feet long (40–55 gallons), and hard, alkaline water at 75–79°F. Do that and Melanotaenia boesemani is the hardiest, most peaceful and longest-lived of the popular rainbowfish, reaching 4 inches and living ten years — and after a year, the silver juvenile you bought turns into the only common freshwater fish that is blue at the front and orange at the back.

About the Boesemani Rainbowfish

Melanotaenia boesemani comes from just three lakes — the Ayamaru Lakes in the Vogelkop peninsula of West Papua — where it is listed as endangered because of over-collection and habitat change; the species entry has the details. Every boesemani in the trade today is captive-bred, mostly in Southeast Asia and Europe, which is part of why it is so tough in an aquarium. Males reach 4–4.5 inches and become almost as deep as they are long; females stay slimmer at 3.5 inches and show only a hint of the colour. The split colour — blue-purple head and front half, yellow-orange rear half and fins — is unique in the family, and a dominant male in display adds a pale bar down the forehead.

Boesemani Rainbowfish Tank Size and Water

Six boesemani need a 40-gallon breeder (36 inches) at minimum; a 55 or 75 gallon at 4 feet is where the school looks its best and leaves room for a second species. They are sprinters, so length beats volume, and a dark substrate with a black background, plants along the back, and an open lane along the front glass is the layout that shows the colour. Water should be hard and alkaline: pH 7.0–8.0, 8–15 dGH, 75–79°F, nitrate under 20 ppm, with moderate flow they can play in. In soft water the colour fades and they stop spawning; crushed coral in the filter fixes soft tap water. A tight lid is not optional — boesemani jump during their dawn chases. Getting the Boesemani Colour

The question behind most boesemani rainbowfish care searches is “why is mine grey?”. The answers, in order of likelihood:

  1. Age. Juveniles are silver until 8–12 months and do not reach full colour until 18. The 1.5-inch shop fish is a year from the photo.
  2. Group size. A pair or trio never colours. Six is the minimum; at eight or more the males compete and the tank lights up every morning.
  3. Water. Soft or acidic water washes the colour out. Check GH and pH before anything else.
  4. Background and substrate. Over white sand under a bare wall, even a good male looks pale. Go dark.
  5. Food. Colour-enhancing flake, krill and spirulina three times a week deepen the orange and blue within six weeks.
  6. Time of day. Best colour is the first two hours after lights-on. A drab school at dinner time is normal.
Sexing for colour. A school of eight with three or four males gives constant sparring and the strongest colour. Males are deeper-bodied with a longer first dorsal fin; in an all-female group nobody colours up.

Boesemani Rainbowfish Food

Boesemani eat anything small enough to swallow and beg constantly. The staple is crushed flake or 1 mm micro pellets twice a day, as much as the school clears in two minutes; frozen daphnia, bloodworm and brine shrimp three or four times a week; spirulina flake or blanched spinach twice a week; and live daphnia or brine shrimp to bring them into breeding condition. They spit out large pellets, so size down. The full schedule and feeding for colour are on the rainbow fish food page.

Boesemani Rainbowfish Tank Mates

Peaceful with everything they cannot swallow. In a 55 or larger, boesemani go well with tetras, rasboras, danios, barbs, corydoras, bristlenose plecos, loaches, platies, mollies, swordtails, gouramis, rams and angelfish, and with a second rainbowfish species — the turquoise rainbowfish is the classic partner. Avoid bettas (too slow and easily stressed by the activity), discus (wrong water and too slow to feed), goldfish (too cold) and anything that nips fins. Boesemani will eat shrimplets but usually leave adult cherry shrimp alone. Do not keep them with other Melanotaenia in groups under six each, because the species hybridise. Breeding Boesemani Rainbowfish

Boesemani are among the easiest rainbowfish to spawn and among the slowest to raise. A conditioned trio (one male, two females) in a 20-gallon breeding tank at 77°F with a spawning mop lays 10–30 adhesive eggs every morning; the eggs hatch in 7–10 days, and the fry need infusoria or liquid fry food for a week before they can take baby brine shrimp, then grow slowly to an inch at three months and colour at a year. In a planted community tank a few fry occasionally survive on their own.
