The right rainbow fish water parameters are the opposite of what most community-fish advice gives you: hard, alkaline and steady. Target 74–79°F, pH 7.0–8.0, 8–15 dGH and 5–12 dKH, ammonia and nitrite at zero, nitrate under 20 ppm and moderate flow. Melanotaenia rainbowfish come from limestone rivers and lakes in Australia and New Guinea, and in the soft, acidic water that tetra and discus keepers chase, their colour fades, spawning stops and they slowly decline. Most tap water suits them as it comes out of the pipe.

Rainbow Fish Temperature

Keep most rainbowfish at 74–79°F; 76°F is a good all-round setting for a boesemani, turquoise or Australian school. Two groups run warmer: the dwarf neon (M. praecox) and the red rainbowfish (Glossolepis incisus) come from lowland New Guinea and do best at 75–82°F, so a community of dwarf neons and boesemani meets at 78°F. Rainbowfish are not fussy about the exact number but they are about swings: a heater on a controller, and a room that does not cool 8°F overnight, matter more than the last degree. Cooler water (70–72°F) slows them and dulls the colour; above 84°F the oxygen drops and they hang at the surface.

pH, GH and KH: Why Rainbow Fish Want Hard Water

This is the parameter people get wrong. Rainbowfish evolved in mineral-rich water, and in an aquarium at pH 7.0–8.0 and 8–15 dGH they hold their colour, spawn readily and resist disease. Below pH 6.8 and 5 dGH the same fish look washed out and stop breeding, and in very soft water they become prone to bacterial infections like columnaris. If your tap water is soft:

  • Put a bag of crushed coral or aragonite in the filter; it dissolves slowly and raises both GH and KH.
  • Or add a remineralising salt (a GH/KH booster) to water-change water to reach 8 dGH.
  • Skip driftwood, peat and almond leaves, which push the pH down.

KH is what holds the pH still between water changes; at 5 dKH or more the pH will not crash overnight. Test both once a month. Rainbowfish do not need salt in the water and should not be kept brackish; a few Australian species tolerate estuaries in the wild, but aquarium stock is freshwater.

Ammonia, Nitrite and Nitrate for Rainbow Fish

Ammonia and nitrite must read zero. Rainbowfish show ammonia stress fast — gasping at the surface, red gill edges, fading colour — and a school of eight added to a new tank is a heavy load, which is why they belong in a mature, cycled aquarium and are best added in two batches a week apart. Nitrate under 20 ppm is the target; rainbowfish tolerate 40 ppm without obvious harm, but their colour is the early warning that the water is going stale, and chronic high nitrate goes with the bloat and fin problems that shorten their lives. Weekly 25% water changes with matched water, a filter rated above the tank volume, and not overfeeding a school that begs constantly keep nitrate where it belongs. Flow: Do Rainbow Fish Like Current?

Yes. Rainbowfish come from flowing rivers and streams and will spend hours playing in the outflow of a filter or powerhead, swimming into it, drifting back and doing it again. Aim for 6–10 times the tank volume per hour of total flow from a canister or hang-on filter, with a small powerhead across the length of tanks over 4 feet. What they do not like are dead spots where waste settles, or a current so strong across the whole tank that there is nowhere calm to rest at night; angle the outflow along the front so the back half of the tank is quieter. Good flow also keeps oxygen up, which matters for a fast fish at 78°F.

Water Changes and Keeping Rainbow Fish Water Parameters Stable

  1. 25% weekly, siphoning the substrate, with new water matched to the tank's temperature and hardness.
  2. Heater on a controller set to 76–78°F.
  3. Crushed coral in the filter if GH is under 8, replaced when it dissolves.
  4. Test pH, GH and KH monthly; ammonia, nitrite and nitrate weekly in a new tank.
  5. Watch the colour. A school that fades mid-week is telling you the nitrate or the pH has moved before a test kit will.

Rainbowfish that go pale, hide or flash on the decor after a water change have usually met a temperature or hardness mismatch; the signals are decoded on the rainbow fish behavior page, and the infections that follow chronic bad water are on rainbow fish diseases.
