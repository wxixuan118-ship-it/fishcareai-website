Six grouper fish diseases account for almost every sick grouper in an aquarium, and only two of them arrive with the fish: marine ich and velvet, and flukes, which most wild-caught groupers carry. The rest — HLLE, fatty liver and bloat, cloudy eye and popeye — are made in the tank by diet and water. Groupers are otherwise among the hardiest marine fish there are, which makes this a short list, but a 12-inch grouper is a difficult patient: it cannot be treated in a reef, it is hard to catch, and it needs a hospital tank of 75 gallons or more. This page tells the six apart, gives the treatments that work for large predators, and lays out the four-week quarantine that prevents most of it.

Grouper Fish Diseases That Arrive With the Fish: Ich and Velvet

Ich shows as discrete white spots the size of salt grains that appear, vanish for a few days and return heavier, with flashing against rock. Groupers get it later and milder than tangs but still get it, usually within two weeks of arrival or after a temperature swing. Velvet is a finer gold or grey dust, seen with a torch from the side, with rapid breathing and the fish hanging in the flow; it kills in one to three days.

Both are treated in a bare hospital tank — for a large grouper, 75 gallons or more with a PVC pipe to hide in — with chelated copper at the therapeutic level for 30 days, tested daily; groupers tolerate copper well. For ich alone, hyposalinity at 1.009 specific gravity for four weeks is the gentler option and groupers, being euryhaline, take it easily. Neither parasite can be cured in a display tank with invertebrates, and reef-safe remedies do not clear them. The parasite life cycle is on the Cryptocaryon entry.

Flukes and Cloudy Eye

Almost every wild-caught grouper carries skin and gill flukes (Neobenedenia, Benedenia) — flat, transparent worms a few millimetres long that are invisible on the fish but show as scratching on rock, clamped fins, cloudy eyes and rapid breathing. A 5-minute freshwater dip (RO water matched to tank pH and temperature) makes them drop off as visible white specks, which confirms the diagnosis; praziquantel in the quarantine tank, two courses a week apart, clears them. Flukes are the reason the quarantine below includes praziquantel by default.

Cloudy eye on one side is usually a fluke on the cornea or a scrape against rock; on both sides it is nitrate, a bacterial infection or both. Popeye (a bulging eye) follows an injury or gas behind the eye; both clear with a large water change and, if not, a broad-spectrum antibiotic in the hospital tank.

Grouper Fish Diseases Made in the Tank: HLLE

Head and lateral line erosion — pale pits and channels around the eyes that spread along the flank — is the commonest of the chronic grouper fish diseases in fish kept for years, and it is made in the tank. The links are a frozen-only diet without vitamin supplements (silversides alone lack vitamins A, C and the fatty acids the fish needs), activated carbon dust, stray voltage from a failing heater or pump, and the chronic stress of a tank that is too small. It is not infectious. Early pits fill in over two or three months with vitamin-soaked food, a carnivore pellet in the rotation, carbon in a bag or removed, a grounding probe and, if the fish is cramped, a bigger tank. Deep erosion scars but stops spreading. Feeding to prevent it is on the grouper fish food page.

Bloat and Fatty Liver Disease

A grouper with a permanently swollen belly, growing lethargic and pale, is almost always suffering from fatty liver disease: the result of live freshwater feeder fish (goldfish, rosy reds) and of feeding an adult every day because it begs. The liver fills with fat, the fish loses condition and dies at half its lifespan. There is no medication; the treatment is a week's fast, then lean marine food (silversides, squid, shrimp, clam) with a vitamin soak two or three times a week, and no feeders ever again. A belly that swells suddenly after a meal and flattens in two days is normal digestion. A swollen belly with raised scales is dropsy, a sign of internal infection, treated with Epsom salt and a broad antibiotic in the hospital tank and often fatal. Feeder goldfish also carry thiaminase, which destroys vitamin B1 and shows as loss of appetite and twitching — reversible early with vitamin-soaked marine food.

The Grouper Quarantine Plan

  1. Set up a bare tank of 40–75 gallons (larger for a panther grouper), heater, a sponge or hang-on filter seeded from the display, PVC pipe or a ceramic cave, no substrate.
  2. Acclimate slowly, then lights off for the first day. Offer a live ghost shrimp on a stick on day two.
  3. Day 3: freshwater dip for 5 minutes to confirm and knock down flukes.
  4. Week 1: praziquantel, repeated after seven days.
  5. Weeks 2–4: copper ramped over three days and held for 14–30 days (or hyposalinity for the full four weeks), feeding well throughout.
  6. Introduce only a fish that has eaten for two weeks with no spots, and add it to a display with tank mates it cannot swallow.

Four weeks in a bare tank is tedious for a fish that looked healthy in the shop; treating a 2-foot grouper in a 240-gallon reef with corals and a moray eel is far worse.
