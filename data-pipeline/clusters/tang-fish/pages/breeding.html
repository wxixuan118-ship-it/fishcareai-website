Tang fish breeding in a home aquarium stops at the first step: you cannot tell a male from a female, and if a pair does spawn, the eggs float away and hatch into larvae that drift in open water for two to three months eating plankton no hobbyist can supply. Every captive-bred tang on sale today came from a research hatchery, and the first ones only appeared in 2015. This page covers what can be done — sexing where it is possible, recognising a spawn, and understanding why the fry are the problem — and what to make of a tang that looks pregnant.

Male vs Female Tang Fish

Tangs are sexually monomorphic: males and females have the same colour, fins and shape, and there is no external difference you can rely on in a shop. The exceptions and the hints:

In a group, the male is the one that circles, flares its fins and chases at dusk; a lone tang gives you nothing to go on. Buying “a pair” of tangs from a shop is buying two tangs of unknown sex that will probably fight (see tang fish tank mates).

How Tang Fish Spawn

Tangs are broadcast (pelagic) spawners. On the reef, groups gather at dusk near a reef edge or channel where the current runs out to sea; pairs or small groups dash upward, release eggs and sperm at the top of the rush, and return to the bottom. Yellow tangs spawn in pairs or groups around the full moon; blue tangs and naso tangs spawn in large aggregations. There is no nest, no guarding and no parental care — the eggs are carried away on the current within seconds. The reproductive biology is summarised on the Acanthuridae page.

In a large aquarium (180 gallons and up) with a settled group of yellow or convict tangs, dusk spawning rushes do occasionally happen, usually noticed as two fish shooting up the water column together with the lights dimming. The skimmer collects the eggs by morning.

Tang Fish Eggs and Larvae

  • Eggs: about 0.7 mm, clear, buoyant, with an oil droplet. A female releases tens of thousands per spawn. They hatch in 24–30 hours.
  • Larvae: hatch at 1.5–2 mm with no mouth or fins, living on the yolk for two days, then need live prey far smaller than rotifers — copepod nauplii of specific species.
  • Pelagic stage: 60–80 days drifting in open water, growing into the transparent, kite-shaped “acronurus” larva, before settling onto a reef and taking adult colour within days.

That two-month pelagic stage on a diet of cultured copepods is why tang fish breeding was considered impossible for decades and why a home tank cannot do it: the larvae need a dedicated larval tank, live copepod cultures scaled up daily, and no filtration that would remove the prey.

Can You Breed Tang Fish at Home?

Practically, no. The parts that are possible — keeping a compatible group in a very large tank and occasionally seeing a spawn — are described above. The parts that are not are the same for every aquarist without a hatchery: collecting floating eggs before the overflow takes them, and raising larvae that need 60–80 days of live copepods. The realistic route to “breeding tangs” for a hobbyist is to buy captive-bred fish and support the hatcheries that do the work. The husbandry that keeps an adult healthy is the same whichever way it was born; see the tang fish care guide.

Captive-bred tangs matter for two reasons: they take pressure off wild reefs (Hawaiian collection has been closed since 2021), and they survive the first year in a home tank far better than wild fish, because they arrive with no wild parasites and already eating prepared food. Is My Tang Fish Pregnant?

No tang is ever pregnant; they do not carry young. A tang with a swollen belly is one of three things: well fed (a full, rounded belly after nori is healthy), bloated from too much frozen or dry food and too little fibre (two days of seaweed only, and it clears), or in rare cases carrying eggs just before a spawn, which passes within a day. A belly that stays swollen for a week with raised scales or a fish that stops eating is a health problem, not a breeding one — see tang fish diseases. What a healthy adult should look like, size by size, is on the tang fish size page; how it should be fed is under tang fish food.
