What Do Axolotls Eat?

Ambystoma mexicanum · Updated November 20, 2026

What do axolotls eat? They are carnivores. For most captive adults, appropriately sized earthworms are a useful staple; quality sinking carnivore pellets can add variety. Match each portion to the animal's size and appetite rather than following a fixed count of worms.

Quick reference. These are husbandry starting points. Test your own water and consult an amphibian veterinarian for illness or an individual animal's needs.

How to feed an axolotl cleanly

How often axolotls eat and how much

Growing juveniles usually eat more often than adults. Offer juveniles small, suitable meals daily; many adults do well with meals every two or three days. Adjust to body condition, water temperature and veterinary advice. The abdomen should not become markedly wider than the head, and unexplained weight loss needs investigation.

How to feed cleanly

Offer food with clean blunt tongs or in a feeding dish. Watch the animal swallow, then remove uneaten pieces after feeding. Leftovers raise ammonia in a cool tank. Never force-feed a weak animal without advice from an amphibian veterinarian.

If an axolotl refuses food

Check temperature and test ammonia and nitrite first. Recent transport, a new environment and oversized food can also suppress feeding. If refusal persists, or is paired with bloating, abnormal floating or skin changes, seek an amphibian vet. Read the axolotl water parameters guide to interpret the tests, and the axolotl diseases guide if appetite loss comes with other signs.

What axolotls eat as a staple

Axolotls are aquatic carnivores that seize and swallow prey. In the wild they take a variety of small animals, but wild diet lists are not a shopping list for a home aquarium. Captive food must be available consistently, safe from chemical contamination, appropriately sized, and nutritionally useful. Earthworms meet many of these needs for older juveniles and adults. A formulated sinking carnivore pellet can provide another reliable option, especially when a keeper cannot source worms year-round. Choose meals the axolotl swallows easily and portions that do not leave food decaying in the tank.

Food names alone do not establish quality. An earthworm collected from a lawn treated with pesticides is a poor choice even though earthworms are generally useful. A pellet designed for tropical community fish may differ from one intended for carnivorous aquatic animals. Read the product label, keep food stored as directed, and discard spoiled or expired food. Use foods that sink or can be delivered near the animal rather than expecting it to chase floating flakes. Consider the animal's age and mouth size before offering any item.

Bloodworms may be eagerly accepted, but they should remain an occasional food for adults. Bloodworms are convenient and can be suitable for small juveniles or occasional variety, but an adult eating only bloodworms may not receive an adequate long-term diet. Feeder fish add risks of parasites, gill nipping, injury, and uncertain nutrition. Raw meat from land animals does not resemble normal prey and is not a good staple. When in doubt, return to a suitable worm or formulated carnivore pellet and discuss special needs with an amphibian veterinarian.

Earthworms: selecting, preparing and offering

Use worms from a trusted culture or an area known to be free of lawn chemicals, fertilizers, and road runoff. Rinse soil from the worm with clean dechlorinated water before feeding. For a small axolotl, cut a large worm into short pieces that can be swallowed comfortably. Watch the animal eat each piece. A worm left loose can hide under decor and decay later, creating a water-quality problem. If the animal rejects a particular species of worm, try a smaller piece or a different clean source before concluding that all worms are unsuitable.

A rough portion guide is the animal's condition and interest, not a universal number of worms. Worms vary enormously in length and thickness. Offer a manageable amount and pause to let the axolotl swallow. Stop when it loses interest, and remove what remains. A healthy adult may take one or several pieces depending on size. Keep notes for several weeks to learn that individual's normal intake. Keep portions moderate even when the axolotl readily accepts food; oversized meals add waste and can change body condition.

If using home-cultured worms, keep the culture away from pesticides and household cleaners. Feed the culture appropriately and avoid using worms from a bin that has developed a chemical smell or obvious contamination. Do not use bait worms that may have been dyed or kept in unsuitable additives unless their source is clearly safe. There is no nutritional advantage in offering prey so large that the axolotl struggles to swallow it. A rinsed, bite-sized piece is easier to feed and easier to remove if refused.

Pellets: a practical second staple

Choose sinking pellets formulated for aquatic carnivores or specifically recommended for axolotls. Compare ingredient lists and avoid assuming all pellets are interchangeable. A pellet should soften and be easy to swallow, especially for smaller animals. Offer only a few at a time in a dish or with tongs, then count what is left. Pellets hidden in substrate can break apart and foul water. A feeding dish makes it easier to inspect the animal's intake and keeps food out of a bare-bottom tank's corners.

Introduce pellets gradually if an axolotl is accustomed to live worms. Some individuals respond strongly to movement, so a stationary pellet may be ignored at first. Place it near the mouth or use blunt tongs to create gentle movement without poking the animal. Never withhold food for an extended period simply to force a food switch. If the animal is losing weight or refusing all food, review water and temperature and seek veterinary advice. A mixed routine can work well when both foods are accepted.

Store dry pellets in a sealed container according to the manufacturer's instructions, away from humidity and heat. Buy a quantity that will be used while fresh. Avoid leaving the container open near the aquarium where condensation can cause spoilage. If a pellet crumbles immediately or smells stale, replace it. The best product is one the animal can consume reliably and that supports good condition, not one with the most impressive marketing claim.

What baby and adult axolotls eat

Newly hatched larvae have different requirements from pet-shop juveniles or adults. They initially respond to moving, very small live prey such as suitable newly hatched brine shrimp or small daphnia, as described by axolotl rearing resources. Their tiny size, frequent feeding, and need for exceptionally clean water make larval care a specialized project. Do not apply an adult earthworm schedule to larvae. A breeder should provide precise feeding information for the size and stage of the animal being sold.

Growing juveniles need food more frequently than adults, often daily when small. Use appropriately sized pieces and check each animal separately if several juveniles are housed in divided containers. Unequal growth can create a bite or cannibalism risk. As the animal grows, increase prey size gradually and reconsider feeding frequency. A routine that worked at four inches may overfeed an adult. Record weight or comparable overhead photographs rather than relying on memory of how the animal looked months earlier.

Many healthy adults eat every two or three days, but this is a starting point rather than a fixed prescription. Water temperature, body condition, reproductive status, and individual behavior affect appetite. An adult kept cooler may digest more slowly. Feed less often if the abdomen becomes persistently broad, and seek advice if the animal thins despite eating. A veterinarian can help distinguish nutrition from illness when changes are unexpected.

Read body condition without overreacting

View the axolotl from above in a similar position each time. The abdomen is often roughly comparable in width to the head in a well-conditioned adult, but sex, age, and recent feeding affect the outline. A single photograph immediately after a meal is not a good baseline. Look for gradual change over several weeks, along with activity and appetite. A sharply distended belly, persistent floating, or signs of pain are not ordinary overfeeding observations and deserve veterinary assessment.

Weight loss may reflect inadequate portions, competition, difficult-to-swallow food, heat stress, poor water quality, or disease. Review the feeding log and watch a complete meal. Does the animal notice food, strike but spit it out, or ignore it entirely? These are different clues. Check ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and temperature before trying rich treats. Changing several foods at once may make it harder to identify the original problem. Contact an amphibian vet if the animal continues to decline.

Some keepers use a weekly photograph to track size and condition. Take the image through the glass or from above without lifting the animal out of water. Consistent lighting and distance make comparisons more useful. A body-condition record is particularly helpful when an animal has stopped eating, because it shows whether weight is stable or dropping. It also prevents a normal short pause in appetite from causing unnecessary handling and repeated interventions.

Food hygiene and water quality

A food item that remains in the tank can decay even when the water looks clear. Remove leftover worms, pellets, and feces promptly with a clean baster or siphon. Check under hides and around filter intakes where fragments collect. Test ammonia and nitrite if food was lost or the tank smells unusual. An established biofilter has limits; a large decaying meal can overwhelm it. The water-parameters guide explains how to read the nitrogen tests together and when to change water.

Dedicate feeding tongs, dishes, and containers to the axolotl tank. Rinse them with safe water and keep soap, disinfectant residue, and aerosol sprays away from the aquarium. Frozen foods should be thawed according to package directions and not repeatedly refrozen. Do not use prey collected from areas where pest control products may be applied. If multiple animals are housed separately, avoid moving wet tools between tanks without cleaning; this reduces the chance of transferring pathogens.

Water quality also influences appetite. Detectable ammonia or nitrite, sustained warm water, or strong flow can make an animal refuse a perfectly suitable food. It is tempting to solve every missed meal by offering a new treat. First restore the environment, observe the animal, and document what it accepts. If a new food leaves fragments under hides, change how you serve it or remove the fragments after the meal.

When an axolotl stops eating: what to check

Begin with a timeline. When was the last normal meal? Was the animal moved, chilled, warmed, or exposed to a water change with untreated tap water? Is it swallowing and then regurgitating, or showing no interest at all? Check temperature and nitrogen tests immediately. Look for bloating, unusual buoyancy, skin lesions, injury, or visible swallowed substrate. Do not assume that a fasting animal merely wants a different flavor. These questions help distinguish a routine short pause from a developing health problem.

Offer a small familiar food once the environment is confirmed safe. Avoid repeated poking with tongs or leaving multiple foods to decay. If refusal continues for several days in an adult, occurs in a small juvenile, or is accompanied by weight loss or any physical sign, contact an amphibian veterinarian. The urgency rises with rapid deterioration. A veterinarian may ask for recent water results, diet history, photographs, and a description of substrate; prepare these before the call.

Do not force-feed or medicate without professional direction. Handling a weak axolotl can add stress and injure delicate skin. Advice copied from fish forums may involve products that are unsafe for amphibians. A useful home response is careful measurement, clean cool water, and prompt assessment. Bring the water readings and food history to the veterinarian if refusal continues.

A practical adult feeding example

Suppose an adult accepts clean earthworm pieces and maintains a stable body outline. You might offer a moderate meal every two or three days, observe how quickly it swallows, and remove the remains. On another day, you could substitute a suitable sinking carnivore pellet meal for variety. This is an example, not a prescribed number of worms or pellets: food size and individual metabolism differ. Record what was eaten and review the animal's condition over several weeks. If the abdomen becomes steadily broader, reduce portions; if the animal becomes thin, investigate rather than simply adding treats.

Use the same feeding location when possible. A shallow dish on a bare bottom or fine sand makes leftovers easy to find. If using tongs, approach slowly and avoid gripping a worm so tightly that the animal pulls against the tool. Give the axolotl time to swallow between pieces. It may ignore food when startled by hands in the tank. Keep the feeding session calm and short. If a piece disappears beneath a hide, retrieve it before it decomposes. A consistent method helps you notice when appetite truly changes.

A cool-water animal may digest more slowly than a tropical fish. Do not assume a missed meal means it must be fed again immediately. Check the water and observe. An adult that is active and normally shaped may simply not be hungry that day, whereas persistent refusal paired with weight loss or swelling calls for a veterinarian.

Special cases: newly acquired and recovering animals

A newly transported axolotl may need time to settle before it accepts food. Keep the water stable, provide a hide, and ask the source what it was eating. Offer a familiar appropriately sized item rather than changing the entire diet on the first day. Test the new tank frequently. If the animal remains alert but skips one adult meal, continue careful observation; if a juvenile is declining or any animal has lesions, swelling, or severe lethargy, contact an amphibian vet promptly. The feeding problem may be a husbandry or health issue rather than a matter of preference.

During recovery from illness, follow the veterinarian's feeding directions. An animal may need smaller portions, a different texture, or a temporary pause depending on diagnosis. Do not improvise force-feeding, supplements, or medications based on social-media advice. Keep notes on what is offered and actually swallowed. Remove uneaten food from a hospital container immediately because its small water volume can deteriorate fast. Ask how often to test and change water in that temporary setting.

If another animal has bitten the mouth or gills, food refusal may be related to injury. Separate animals and arrange assessment rather than repeatedly presenting food to a stressed patient. Correct the housing problem before resuming normal feeding.

What do axolotls eat? Foods compared by purpose

Earthworms are a strong staple for many adults because they provide a substantial whole-prey item and can be cut to size. Sinking carnivore pellets are convenient and easier to store, but quality and acceptance vary. Bloodworms are small and readily eaten, which makes them useful for tiny juveniles or occasional variety, yet that convenience does not make them a complete adult staple. Brine shrimp and daphnia are important in larval rearing at appropriate sizes but are not a substitute for adult meals. Treat each food according to the life stage and job it serves.

The label 'live' says nothing about chemical exposure or disease risk. Wild-caught prey can carry contaminants or pathogens. Commercial feeder fish may be poorly kept and can nip gills before being eaten. Insects with hard shells or chemicals from a garden are risky. Prepared food can also spoil if stored badly. Choose sources you can evaluate, wash or prepare items appropriately, and discard anything questionable. A plain, reliable diet usually serves the animal better than a constantly changing menu of exciting foods.

If a particular pellet is refused, try offering a small portion near the animal at its normal feeding time. Do not leave a handful overnight. An axolotl that readily eats worms can maintain a suitable diet while you test pellet acceptance slowly. Keep the accepted food as the staple while testing any new pellet in small amounts.

A feeding log that answers useful questions

A short log can include date, food, approximate portion, whether the animal struck and swallowed, water temperature, and unusual behavior. Record major water-test results when appetite changes. After several weeks the log shows the normal interval between meals for that animal. It also reveals whether refusal began after a hot period, filter failure, or food switch. This is more informative than counting a single missed meal. You do not need a complicated spreadsheet; a notebook or phone note is enough if entries are consistent.

Photograph body condition from above every few weeks without taking the axolotl out of water. Use similar lighting and distance. A gradual narrowing abdomen may be hard to notice day to day, while a series of images makes the trend visible. Do not diagnose disease from shape alone. If the log shows good intake but weight loss, bring the evidence to an amphibian veterinarian. If feeding is high and nitrate rises rapidly, portions or cleanup may need adjustment even if the animal appears hungry.

The log can also guide a temporary caregiver. State the normal food, amount, schedule, and where leftovers collect. Ask the caregiver to record what was actually eaten and to check temperature and filter operation. A few accurate observations are more useful than an instruction to 'feed until it stops.'

Axolotl diet FAQ

Can axolotls eat earthworms every day?

Small growing juveniles may need daily meals; adults usually need less frequent feeding. Adjust the amount to body condition.

Are bloodworms enough for an adult?

No. When people ask what do axolotls eat as adults, the answer is a staple like earthworms; bloodworms are a treat or food for small juveniles.

Can I use fish flakes?

Fish flakes are a poor fit for bottom-feeding axolotls and should not replace appropriate worms or sinking carnivore pellets.
