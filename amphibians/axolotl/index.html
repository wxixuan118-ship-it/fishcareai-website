Amphibian care

Axolotl Care Guide

Ambystoma mexicanum · Updated November 20, 2026

Axolotls are fully aquatic salamanders, not tropical fish. Good axolotl care starts with a cool, established aquarium, gentle flow and a feeding routine you can sustain. Use this overview to plan the whole setup, then follow the focused guides for exact decisions.

Quick reference. These are husbandry starting points. Test your own water and consult an amphibian veterinarian for illness or an individual animal's needs.

The care routine at a glance

Axolotl care starts with the environment

A tank is ready only when its biological filter processes waste reliably. Cycle it before bringing an axolotl home, and verify with a liquid test kit. A filter with diffused output protects delicate external gills from strong current. Add two smooth hides, a secure lid and a thermometer. Small loose gravel can be swallowed, so use a bare bottom or an appropriate fine substrate. For the full step-by-step build, follow the axolotl tank setup guide, and size the aquarium with the axolotl tank size guide.

A weekly axolotl care routine

Check the thermometer daily, remove uneaten food promptly, and record ammonia, nitrite and nitrate at least weekly. Replace some water with dechlorinated, temperature matched water as tests require. Observe behavior during feeding; a sudden change often signals water or health trouble. Before you bring an axolotl home

Start by deciding whether your room can support a cold-water aquarium through the hottest part of the year. Measure the room temperature over several days rather than relying on a thermostat setting. Aquarium water often warms under lights, near windows, or during a power outage. If the room stays warm, budget for reliable cooling and a backup plan before buying an animal. A fan can help in some homes, but evaporation and humidity affect its performance. A chiller is more predictable where summers are consistently hot. Set up the tank first and demonstrate that it can hold the desired temperature while the filter runs.

Choose a captive-bred animal from a responsible source and ask what it has been eating, its approximate age, and whether it has had any recent health problems. Ask for a clear photograph of the entire body and gills if buying remotely. An axolotl that is alert, proportionate, and free from obvious wounds is a better prospect than one with damaged skin or severe weight loss, although appearance alone cannot establish health. Do not acquire a wild animal. The species is threatened in its native Mexican habitat, and a home aquarium does not contribute to conservation simply by keeping an animal alive.

Identify an amphibian-experienced veterinarian before an emergency. Many clinics treat fish or reptiles but have limited experience with aquatic salamanders. Keep the clinic number, transport container, dechlorinator, thermometer, and test kit together. If the animal becomes ill, a record of water tests and recent changes can be more useful to the clinician than a vague description of the tank.

The first month in a new aquarium

During a fishless cycle, add an appropriate ammonia source and test until the filter consistently converts it to nitrate without leaving measurable ammonia or nitrite. This usually takes weeks rather than days. Water that looks clear can still be unsafe, and bottled bacteria do not substitute for measurement. Record the dose, test result, date, and any water change. When the cycle is verified, reduce accumulated nitrate with a partial water change and match the new water's temperature before introducing the axolotl. Preserve wet filter media during the move so the bacteria are not lost.

After arrival, keep lighting gentle and avoid repeatedly handling or rearranging the animal. Observe whether it can rest on the bottom, enter its hides, and take appropriately sized food. Test ammonia and nitrite more often than usual during the first weeks because the filter may respond differently to real waste than to the cycling dose. Remove uneaten food promptly and watch for a trend in nitrate. A stable routine tells you what is normal for that individual; sudden deviations become easier to notice when the baseline is documented.

Start with a species-only tank and leave the bottom open for movement. Small fish may nip exposed gills, while the axolotl may swallow fish that fit in its mouth. Other axolotls can bite limbs and compete for food. A species-only tank with room to move, two hides, and accessible bottom space is a clear starting point. Add complexity only when it solves a specific problem, such as shade or current diffusion, rather than to fill every square inch with decor.

Daily, weekly and monthly axolotl care

Each day, read the thermometer and look at the animal before feeding or cleaning. Note its posture, gill appearance, appetite, skin, and whether it is floating unusually. A single missed meal can happen, but a pattern matters. Check that the filter is moving water gently and that its intake is unobstructed. Remove visible waste with a turkey baster or siphon. Make sure the lid is secure; axolotls can jump, especially if startled. These observations take a few minutes and often reveal problems before a scheduled water test does.

At least weekly, test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate and record the results. Change a portion of the water as needed to control nitrate and remove dissolved waste, using a conditioner suitable for your tap supply. Match temperature and avoid sudden swings in pH or hardness. Rinse filter media only in removed tank water when flow slows, and keep enough mature media intact to protect the cycle. Do not replace every cartridge on a calendar schedule. Clean feeding tools and examine hides for trapped food or sharp edges.

Monthly, review the log rather than looking at each test in isolation. A slowly rising nitrate result may mean that water changes are too small or feeding waste is accumulating. A seasonal rise in temperature calls for a cooling adjustment before the tank reaches a stressful level. Check the animal's body condition with comparable photographs taken from above. A care schedule is useful only when it responds to measurements; copying another keeper's exact water-change percentage without considering your tank can miss a developing problem.

Temperature and seasonal planning

The useful target of roughly 16–18°C is deliberately cool. Merck's laboratory-animal temperature table lists 18–20°C for axolotls and African clawed frogs, while specialist husbandry sources often aim slightly cooler for axolotls. The important practical points are to avoid sustained heat and abrupt changes. A tank near a sunny window or radiator can become unsafe even if the room feels comfortable. An inexpensive separate thermometer helps catch a failed heater, fan, or chiller sensor. Never assume a tropical-fish preset is suitable.

Exact temperature targets, chiller and fan options, and what to do in a heat wave are covered in the axolotl water parameters guide.

Feeding and body condition

Offer food sized for easy swallowing. Earthworms from a clean, pesticide-free source are a useful adult staple, and suitable sinking carnivore pellets can help when live worms are unavailable. Small juveniles need smaller prey and more frequent meals than adults. Remove leftovers before they decay. Bloodworms may be accepted readily but are not a complete long-term adult diet. Avoid feeding wild-caught prey from unknown environments because chemical exposure and parasites are difficult to assess. The diet guide compares food choices and explains how to adapt a routine to age and condition.

Food choices, portions by age and what to do when an axolotl stops eating are covered in what do axolotls eat.

Axolotl behavior and common care myths

Axolotls often rest quietly, especially during daylight, and may become more active around feeding. Resting alone is not proof of illness. Look for a change from the individual's normal pattern paired with water results and physical signs. External gills can move gently as the animal ventilates; frequent frantic trips to the surface, persistent floating, or inability to remain on the bottom deserve attention. A brief surface gulp can occur, but repeated distress is different. Avoid interpreting every movement from a short online video without context.

Regeneration is remarkable but does not make injuries harmless. A bitten limb or damaged gill can become infected, and repeated injuries can reflect unsuitable tank mates or unsafe decor. Do not handle an axolotl to demonstrate regeneration. Its skin is sensitive, and unnecessary handling can damage the protective mucus layer. If you must move it, use a clean container containing tank water and support the body without squeezing. Follow veterinary guidance for wounds rather than applying a generic fish remedy.

An axolotl remains aquatic throughout normal life. It does not need a dry basking platform, a tropical heater, or a brightly lit community tank. The animal may occasionally surface, but the setup should prioritize bottom area and access to water throughout. If a seller describes a salamander that routinely climbs onto land, verify the species. Husbandry recommendations for other salamanders cannot simply be transferred to Ambystoma mexicanum.

A practical troubleshooting order

When something looks wrong, first confirm basic measurements: temperature, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and whether replacement water was treated. Next inspect the filter, recent cleaning, food left in the tank, and any new animals or decorations. Write down when the sign began and whether it is progressing. This order does not diagnose disease, but it can identify preventable stressors quickly. If water is unsafe, make a measured partial change with temperature-matched dechlorinated water while seeking advice for a distressed animal.

Use only a treatment chosen for the diagnosed condition. Salt, dyes, antibiotics, and other fish products can be harmful or obscure a diagnosis in amphibians. A cotton-like patch, redness, bloating, or persistent appetite loss may have different causes that require different care. Photograph the lesion in consistent light, record test results, and contact an amphibian veterinarian. Severe lethargy, bleeding, ulcers, or rapid deterioration should be treated as urgent. The disease guide gives a symptom-based first-response checklist, but it cannot replace an examination.

Once the immediate issue is addressed, review the system that allowed it to develop. Was cooling undersized for summer? Did a filter cartridge replacement remove too much biological media? Did an uneaten worm decay under a hide? Did a tank mate cause injury? Correcting the cause is more valuable than returning to the same conditions after treatment. Keeping a simple notebook turns each problem into actionable evidence for future care.

A complete pre-purchase checklist

Confirm the aquarium has enough floor area for an adult, a stable stand, a secure lid, and a filter whose current can be diffused. Run the complete system with the lights and cooling equipment in place. Check the water temperature during the hottest afternoon the room is likely to reach. Complete a fishless cycle and retain a written record showing ammonia and nitrite returning to zero after an ammonia dose. Check that nitrate can be brought down with a practical water-change routine. Keep a spare conditioner and a way to test water if the first kit expires or gives a puzzling result.

Before paying for the animal, identify a suitable food source, an amphibian veterinarian, and a responsible backup caregiver. Ask the breeder for the animal's current food, approximate age, and any known health history. Plan transport so the animal will not overheat. A healthy-looking juvenile still needs an adult-size plan. If you cannot keep the aquarium cool during summer or cannot perform regular testing and changes, delay acquisition until those constraints are solved. Doing so protects the animal and saves the cost of emergency changes after it arrives.

Keep a short written handoff for anyone who may care for the tank: feeding amount, what to remove afterward, target temperature, where test supplies are stored, how to dechlorinate replacement water, and which signs require a call. A complicated setup that only one person understands is fragile. Tape the feeding and emergency instructions inside the tank-supply cabinet.

A sample week of care

On a typical day, look at the axolotl before disturbing the tank. Read temperature, check the filter's gentle flow, and note whether the animal rests normally. On feeding days, offer suitable food, watch it swallow, and remove any remainder. During a weekly maintenance session, test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate and write down the numbers. Siphon visible waste and replace a measured portion of water with conditioned, temperature-matched water. The exact percentage should respond to the nitrate trend and waste load rather than a rigid rule copied from another aquarium.

Once a month, inspect the equipment you normally overlook: the lid gap behind hoses, filter intake guard, chiller tubing, stand, and any hide that might have become rough or unstable. Compare a photograph of the animal with earlier images for body condition and gill changes. Review whether the coming season requires a cooling change. If a test result breaks the usual pattern, repeat it and investigate immediately. A temperature rise or nitrate trend should change what you do that week.

If you travel, arrange for someone to check temperature and equipment even if the animal can safely go a short interval between meals. A failed cooling system can be more urgent than a missed feeding. Leave the veterinarian's number and clear instructions for when to contact you.

Axolotl care with a safety margin

Care recommendations often present a minimum and an ideal, and they may differ between sources. A minimum tank can work with attentive maintenance, while a larger long tank gives more floor and dilution. A temperature range may have an upper boundary, but maintaining water near that boundary offers little margin for a heat wave. Nitrate may be tolerated at different levels in different sheets; keeping it low reduces uncertainty. When practical, choose the option that is easier to keep stable in your home rather than the one that barely meets a quoted number.

The same logic applies to feeding and decor. A food might be edible without being a suitable sole staple. A stone might be larger than the mouth today but still be a poor choice if it has sharp edges. Two axolotls might coexist for months and still bite each other during a feeding strike. Look beyond whether an arrangement has worked once. Ask what could change, how quickly you would notice, and whether you have a safe response ready. For example, a chiller failure matters more than whether the tank is two gallons above a published minimum.

When a normal routine changes

A change in behavior does not automatically mean disease, but it deserves a measured response. If the animal refuses a meal, check temperature and water before offering several alternatives. If it floats unusually, note whether it can return to the bottom and whether its abdomen is swollen. If gills look different, compare earlier photographs and inspect current and tank mates. If skin shows a patch or wound, photograph it and contact a veterinarian when it persists, spreads, or occurs with lethargy. The goal is to gather evidence quickly without stressing the animal through repeated handling.

Check what happened immediately before the change: a new decoration, a filter clean, a larger water change, unusual food, power outage, or hot afternoon. Test the water even if it looks clear. Correct an unsafe reading with an appropriate partial change of dechlorinated, temperature-matched water and continue monitoring. Do not treat an unknown cause with a collection of fish medications. Amphibians absorb substances through their skin, and treatment needs to match the diagnosis. A clinician can use your timeline and water log to guide the next step.

After the issue resolves, keep observing for recurrence. A temporary return of appetite does not prove that the filter has recovered or that a skin problem is cured. Review the system and change the condition that led to trouble. Continue daily temperature checks and repeat water tests after the immediate issue.

Axolotl care FAQ

Are axolotls easy pets?

Axolotl care is manageable when cool water and a cycled tank are available, but heat control and frequent water testing make them more demanding than many beginner fish.

Can an axolotl live with fish?

A species-only aquarium is safest: fish may nip gills, be eaten or introduce disease.

Do axolotls need land?

Healthy axolotls remain aquatic throughout life and need an aquarium, not a land area.
