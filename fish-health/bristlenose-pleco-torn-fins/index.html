Bristlenose Pleco With Torn or Ragged Fins? Cave Fights, Tank Mates or Fin Rot

Ancistrus temminckii
🐟 Species: Bristlenose Pleco
🔬 Problem: Torn or Ragged Fins
📋 Category: physical
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated September 28, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: If you keep more than one bristlenose, torn fins most often mean two males are fighting over caves — nesting males are extremely territorial and fight with raised fins and flared cheek spines. Look for bristles: males have a thick crop of tentacles on the snout. Clean tears heal on their own once each male has his own cave, but white, red or receding fin edges are fin rot and need the water fixed today.

Bristlenose plecos (Ancistrus) are small — the ones commonly kept reach about 13 cm[1] — peaceful with other species, and among the easiest plecos to breed. That last point is why their fins get torn. Male Ancistrus are cave-nesters: each male picks a dark cavity in wood or rock, defends it against all intruders, especially other males, and guards the eggs and fry inside it.[2] Put two or three males in a tank with one good cave and they will fight over it, mostly at night when you are not watching.

Bristlenose males are armed for this. Bristlenose plecos have sharp, evertible cheek spines (enlarged odontodes, or skin teeth) that are usually better developed in males and are used in displays and combat; they also have odontodes along the fin spines, and mature males carry the fleshy snout tentacles ("bristles") that make them easy to recognise.[2] A torn dorsal fin, split tail or scraped cheek on a bristlenose is therefore often a fight injury rather than disease. The checks below tell you which it is, and how to stop it recurring.

Why Does My Bristlenose Pleco Have Torn Fins? The Common Causes

Males fighting over caves

Most Likely

This is the most common cause in tanks with more than one bristlenose. Nesting males are extremely territorial and aggressively defend their nest cavity from rival males.[2] Fights follow a set pattern: the two males line up side by side, head to tail, with the dorsal and tail fins raised and the cheek spines flared out; if neither backs down, they circle each other and attack the opponent's head.[2] Raised fins in close contact with a rival's cheek spines and armoured body are what get split and torn, and the loser often shows damage to the dorsal and tail fins and scrapes around the head.

Fights are worst when a male is guarding a clutch or fry. Parental males stay in the cave, clean the eggs and fan them with their pectoral fins for days, and a male that takes over another's nest may eat the eggs he finds there,[2] so there is a lot at stake. Tanks with a breeding pair and young males growing up, or several adult males and only one or two caves, are where torn fins appear most often.

Larger or nippy tank mates

Possible

A bristlenose is small and slow, and it is often kept in community tanks with fish that nip. Tiger barbs, some tetras and serpae-type fish pick at fins, and territorial cichlids — even small ones such as convicts guarding fry — will bite a pleco that comes near their territory at night. A bristlenose kept with a much larger pleco, such as a common or sailfin pleco, may also be pushed out of caves and injured.

Tank-mate damage shows as crescent-shaped bites or ragged edges that appear overnight, usually on the dorsal and tail fins, in a pleco that otherwise eats and behaves normally. It continues until the aggressor is removed.

Fin rot (bacterial infection)

Possible

Bacteria are always present in aquarium water and take advantage of stressed or injured fish. In catfish, Aeromonas infection typically causes fraying and reddening of the fins, sometimes with swollen, red fin bases and pale patches on the body that can become ulcers.[3] Columnaris, another common bacterial disease of warm-water fish, causes fin erosion that starts at the base of the fin and works outwards, and lesions around the dorsal fin.[4]

Fin rot differs from a fight wound in that it keeps getting worse: the edge looks ragged, white, grey or bloodshot and recedes towards the body over days. Poor water, heavy organic load, handling, temperature swings and low oxygen are the usual triggers of bacterial disease,[5] and a torn fin in a dirty tank is an easy way in, so fight damage and fin rot often occur together.

Snagged spines and physical injury

Less Common

The cheek spines and the odontodes on the fin spines[2] hook easily into net mesh. Pulling a snagged bristlenose free tears fins and strips the protective mucus from the skin, and handling in itself is a recognised stressor that weakens the fish's defences.[6] Sharp rocks, rough ornaments and tight gaps behind heaters or filters can also cause tears, particularly in a fish that is being chased.

Poor water quality

Less Common

Ammonia and nitrite do not tear fins directly, but they keep them from healing. Even low ammonia levels cause stress and tissue damage and make fish more prone to bacterial infections.[7] Bristlenose tanks are often heavily stocked once a pair starts breeding, and dozens of growing fry on top of the adults can overload a filter that coped before. Frayed fins on several fish at once point to a water problem.

How to Tell Why Your Bristlenose Pleco's Fins Are Torn

Work through these checks. They tell you whether the damage is a fight injury, a tank-mate problem or an infection, and what to change.

  1. 1
    Count the males

    Look at each bristlenose's snout. Mature males have a large crop of fleshy tentacles over the snout; the tentacles are best developed in breeding males.[2] Females and young males have far smaller and fewer tentacles, restricted to a narrow band around the edge of the snout.[2] If you have two or more males and fewer good caves than males, cave fights are the likely cause.

  2. 2
    Look at where the damage is

    Fight damage usually affects the raised dorsal and tail fins and often comes with scrapes on the head and cheeks, because fighting males attack each other's heads.[2] Bites that are crescent-shaped suggest another species. Damage along the edges of all fins, with redness, suggests fin rot.[3]

  3. 3
    Look closely at the fin edges and compare in two or three days

    Clean splits with normal-coloured edges are injuries; a thin clear rim along the edge is usually new tissue regrowing. White, grey, fuzzy or bloodshot edges, or redness at the fin base, suggest infection.[3][4] Photograph the fin and compare after 48–72 hours: injuries hold steady and then fill in, fin rot gets worse.

  4. 4
    Watch the tank after lights out

    Bristlenose plecos are most active in the dark. Watch for 20–30 minutes after the lights go down with a dim torch, and look for males lining up with fins raised and cheek spines flared,[2] a male chasing others from a cave, or other species picking at the pleco.

  5. 5
    Test the water

    Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature. Ammonia and nitrite should both be zero in an established tank.[7] Keep the temperature steady at around 23–27 °C and the pH in the 6.5–7.5 range. Water analysis and a history of recent changes are the first step in any veterinary workup.[8]

What to Do for a Bristlenose Pleco With Torn Fins

  1. 1
    Give every male his own cave, out of sight of the others

    Provide at least one cave per male, plus spares — clay caves, slate caves or short lengths of pipe that the fish can fit into completely, the kind of dark cavity males naturally choose for nests.[2] Place them at opposite ends of the tank and break up lines of sight with wood and plants. In a small tank, keep only one male.

  2. 2
    Separate or rehome extra males and aggressive tank mates

    If fights continue, move the losing male to another tank or rehome him. Grown-on young males from a breeding pair are a common source of trouble; remove them before they mature. Rehome fin-nipping or territorial species that are biting the pleco.

  3. 3
    Keep the water clean and let the fins regrow

    Keep ammonia and nitrite at zero with regular partial water changes; for any reading, reduce feeding and change 25–50% of the water with dechlorinated water at the same temperature until it is gone.[7] Fish fins regenerate well — in zebrafish, the best-studied example, an amputated fin is almost perfectly replaced[9] — and clean tears on a bristlenose usually fill in within a few weeks. Do not medicate a clean injury.

  4. 4
    If the edges are receding, white or red: treat as fin rot

    Correct the water first; removing sources of stress often corrects bacterial disease on its own.[3] Isolate the fish in a hospital tank if you can, and contact an aquatic veterinarian if the rot continues. Bacterial infections should be identified by culture, and sensitivity testing is recommended before an antibiotic is chosen;[5] follow the product label exactly and check for warnings about scaleless catfish.

  5. 5
    Handle with a container, not a net

    Catch bristlenose plecos by guiding them into a cup, a clear container or a cave you can lift out, rather than a net that their cheek and fin odontodes can snag in. Handling strips protective mucus and stresses the fish,[6] so keep it to a minimum while fins are healing.

Preventing Torn Fins in Bristlenose Plecos

Keep one male per tank unless the tank is large enough for each male to hold his own cave out of sight of the others, and always provide more caves than males, since nesting males defend their cavities aggressively.[2] Choose peaceful tank mates, move young males on from breeding tanks before they mature, and keep the stocking in line with the filter as fry grow. Keep ammonia and nitrite at zero with regular partial water changes,[7] avoid temperature swings and rough handling,[6] and quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them.[8] Use a container rather than a net when you catch them.

When Torn Bristlenose Fins Need Expert Help

Act today if: the fin edges are white, grey or fuzzy, the fin bases are red, the damage is spreading towards the body, or there are ulcers or pale patches on the skin — these point to bacterial infection rather than a fight wound.[3][4] Test and correct the water immediately. Talk to an aquatic vet if: fin rot continues once the water is clean, several fish are affected, or the pleco has stopped eating and is losing weight; an infection needs a laboratory diagnosis to choose the right treatment.[5] A male with clean tears after a cave dispute needs his own cave and clean water, not medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do male bristlenose plecos fight?
Yes. Nesting males are extremely territorial and defend their caves against rival males with a display of raised fins and flared cheek spines, which can escalate to circling and attacks on the head.[2] Fights are most common when there are fewer caves than males or when a male is guarding eggs or fry.
How can I tell a male bristlenose from a female?
Look at the snout. Mature males have a large crop of fleshy tentacles covering much of the snout, best developed in breeding males;[2] females have much smaller, fewer tentacles confined to the edge of the snout.[2] Males also usually have better-developed cheek spines.[2]
How many bristlenose plecos can I keep together?
It depends on the number of males and caves more than the number of fish. One male with one or more females is the easiest combination. Two or more males need a larger tank with a cave for each, placed well apart and out of sight of each other, because males defend their nest sites.[2]
Will my bristlenose's fins grow back?
Usually, yes. Fish fins regenerate well, with lost tissue almost perfectly replaced in the best-studied species,[9] and clean tears on a bristlenose typically fill in over a few weeks in clean water. If the edges keep receding, it is fin rot, not a healing injury.
My male bristlenose stays in his cave and is not eating. Is that because of his fins?
Probably not. A male guarding eggs or fry stays in his cave, fans the eggs with his pectoral fins, and seldom leaves — accounts describe parental males not feeding or taking food only occasionally.[2] The eggs hatch in 4–10 days and he guards the fry for about another week to 10 days.[2] Check the cave with a torch before assuming he is ill.
Can a bristlenose get caught in a net?
Yes, easily. The cheek spines and the odontodes along the fin spines snag in net mesh. Freeing a tangled fish tears fins and removes the skin's protective mucus,[6] so catch bristlenose plecos with a container or by lifting out the cave they are in.
