Black Spots on a Betta Fish: Colour Change, Healing or Disease?

Betta splendens
🐟 Species: Betta Fish
🔬 Problem: Black Spots
📋 Category: disease
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated September 28, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: Flat black patches on a betta that is eating and active are most often a normal colour change — especially in marble, koi, galaxy or candy bettas, whose pattern keeps shifting — or pigment coming back where fins or skin have healed. Test the water to rule out ammonia, which can darken a betta. Raised black lumps, or spots that grow quickly, are different and need a vet.

Black spots on a betta are alarming because they look like a disease, but in most cases they are pigment — the betta's own melanophores, the black pigment cells in the skin and fins, laying down colour in a new place. Bettas have been bred for an enormous range of colours and patterns, and some of the most popular types, sold as marble, koi, candy or galaxy bettas, have a mosaic pattern that is not fixed.[1] Pigment cells also move into skin and fins as they heal after damage from injury, infection or parasites.[2]

The job is to separate those harmless changes from the few causes that need action. The key questions are whether the spot is flat or raised, whether the fish is behaving normally, and what the water test says. Flat, painless colour in a lively betta is almost never an emergency. Darkening together with lethargy and gasping points to ammonia,[3] and a raised black lump that grows is a reason to see a vet, because bettas are unusually prone to pigment-cell tumours.[6]

Why Does My Betta Have Black Spots? The Common Causes

Marble Genes and Normal Colour Change

Most Likely

This is the most common explanation, particularly in pale, white, cellophane, koi or multicoloured bettas. A genome study of more than 700 domestic bettas described the mosaic colour pattern as one of the most striking in the species, sold under names including koi, candy, galaxy, lemon and marble, and found it is controlled by several genes rather than one.[1] Breeders have long reported that bettas carrying the marble trait change colour through their lives: black, blue or red patches appear, spread, fade or move over weeks or months, and a fish bought almost white can end up largely dark, or the reverse.

Marble changes are flat, sit within the scales or fin tissue rather than on top, and can appear on the body, head and fins. The betta keeps eating, flaring and building bubble nests as usual. Domestic bettas have a complex breeding history that includes crossing with wild Betta species,[1] so a fish not sold as a marble can still carry the trait and gain dark spots as it matures. None of this is a disease, it is not contagious, and there is nothing to treat.

Healing Pigment After Fin Damage or Injury

Most Likely

The second common cause is healing. Changes in fish skin pigmentation can follow trauma, parasite attachment or bacterial, viral and fungal skin infections, and experimental work shows the pigment cells actively move and redistribute as skin repairs.[2] In bettas this is usually seen as black or dark edges on fins that are growing back after fin rot or fin biting, or a dark patch where a scale was lost after the fish scraped itself on decoration.

The clue is location and timing: the dark area sits exactly where there was damage, and it appears as the damage stops getting worse. Healthy regrowth is often clear or pale at first, and the pigment follows behind it. Dark fin edges that are still receding, fraying or white and fluffy mean the fin rot is active, not healing, and the water needs attention.

Ammonia Burns and Poor Water Quality

Possible

Bettas are often kept in small, unfiltered bowls or tanks where ammonia builds up quickly. Ammonia damages gill tissue at very low concentrations of its toxic un-ionized form,[5] and a veterinary review lists darkening of body colour among the signs of ammonia poisoning, together with extra mucus, red gills, gasping at the surface and faster breathing.[3] Many keepers describe dark or blackened patches, often along fin edges and on the head, appearing in fish from dirty tanks — and as the damaged skin heals after the water is corrected, it can remain dark for a while.[2]

Suspect this when the spots appear alongside lethargy, clamped fins, loss of appetite, gasping or fin damage, and when the tank is small, has no filter or has not had a water change recently. Chronic exposure to low ammonia also makes fish more prone to bacterial infections,[4] which leads straight back to fin rot and the dark healing edges described above.

Pigment-Cell Tumours (Chromatophoromas)

Less Common

Bettas are one of the fish species in which pigment-cell tumours are reported most often. In a study of 71 tumours from farmed bettas, the masses appeared as whitish, grey or black friable lumps or plaques on the fins, body or head; most were iridophoromas (tumours of the light-reflecting cells), a few were mixed tumours, and 63% had invaded deeper tissue or spread. They were most common in young males and in royal blue and fancy copper bettas, suggesting an inherited tendency.[6] A separate case report described a malignant mixed tumour containing black pigment cells in a 10-month-old male blue betta, with several masses that enlarged rapidly.[7]

What sets a tumour apart is that it is raised: a lump, nodule or thickened plaque you can see in profile, not a flat colour change. It usually grows over weeks. Only a vet can confirm it, by sampling the tissue. Tumours are not contagious, but many are malignant, and the outlook depends on where the mass is and how fast it is growing.

Black Spot Disease (Trematode Parasites)

Less Common

True black spot disease is caused by the larval stage of certain flukes. The parasite needs three hosts: fish-eating birds shed the eggs, the larvae develop in water snails, and free-swimming larvae then burrow into fish and form cysts, which the fish surrounds with pigment — producing small black dots that look like black pepper sprinkled over the fins or flesh.[8] Fish can act as an intermediate host for many flukes of this type.[9]

Because the cycle needs both a suitable snail and a bird, it cannot keep going in a home aquarium, and black spot disease is rare in pet bettas. It is only realistic in a fish raised or kept outdoors in ponds or tubs, and the spots are pinhead-sized, many in number, and do not multiply once the fish is indoors. A few large, irregular patches are not black spot disease, and treating a marble betta for parasites only exposes it to unnecessary medication.

How to Tell What Your Betta's Black Spots Are

Work through these checks with the betta in good light. Most cases are answered by the first three, and none of them requires medication.

  1. 1
    Check whether the spot is flat or raised

    Look at the fish side-on and from above. Flat colour within the scales or fin is pigment — marble change, healing or ammonia darkening. A lump, nodule or plaque that stands up from the surface, or makes the outline of the fish or fin uneven, needs a vet to rule out a pigment-cell tumour.[6]

  2. 2
    Think about what the betta looked like when you bought it

    Pale, white, cellophane, koi, candy, galaxy or multicoloured bettas are the most likely to carry marble or mosaic genetics.[1] If the fish's colours have been shifting in other places too — blue or red patches appearing, spots fading — black spots are almost certainly part of the same process. Photographs taken every week or two will show whether patches are moving.

  3. 3
    Match the spots to any old damage

    Dark edges on fins that were torn, bitten or rotting, or a dark patch where there was a scrape or lost scale, are usually healing pigment.[2] Check that the fin edges are now smooth and clear regrowth is appearing, rather than still fraying.

  4. 4
    Test the water

    Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature. Ammonia and nitrite should both be zero, and bettas are most comfortable at about 24–28 °C. Any ammonia reading combined with darkening, gasping or lethargy points to ammonia burns.[3][4] Small unfiltered containers are the highest risk.

    Recommended: Water test kit
  5. 5
    Watch behaviour and appetite

    A betta that eats eagerly, swims, flares and builds bubble nests while it gains spots is showing a colour change. Spots with clamped fins, lethargy, hiding, rubbing against objects or loss of appetite point to water problems or illness. Rapid growth of a spot or swelling around it points to a tumour.[7]

  6. 6
    Consider where the fish came from

    Black spot disease is only realistic if the betta has lived outdoors with snails and wild birds around, and it shows as many tiny pepper-grain dots rather than a few patches.[8] If your betta has always lived indoors, this cause can be set aside.

What to Do About Black Spots on a Betta

  1. 1
    If it is a colour change: do nothing

    Marble and mosaic colour changes are normal and cannot and should not be treated.[1] Keep the water clean and the fish well fed; good condition makes colour look its best, but it will not stop the pattern shifting. Enjoy the changes — no two marble bettas end up the same.

  2. 2
    If it is healing: keep the water clean while the fins regrow

    Healing skin and fins need clean, warm, stable water. Change part of the water regularly, keep ammonia at zero, remove sharp decorations that may have caused the damage, and let the dark areas fade or settle in their own time.[2][4] No medication is needed for pigment in healed tissue.

  3. 3
    If ammonia is present: fix the water today

    Change 25–50% of the water with dechlorinated water at the same temperature, reduce feeding, and repeat until ammonia reads zero.[4] Move a betta living in an unfiltered bowl into a heated, filtered tank of at least 20 litres if you can. The dark patches from ammonia burns may take weeks to fade after the water is right, and some may remain.

  4. 4
    If there is a raised lump: see an aquatic vet

    A vet can take a small sample of a mass to find out what it is. Some skin tumours in bettas are restricted to the skin, but many invade deeper tissue or spread,[6] and one reported malignant case grew rapidly over a short time.[7] Surgery is sometimes possible for small masses on fins. Do not try to treat a lump with aquarium salt or antiparasitic medicines.

  5. 5
    If it really is black spot disease: usually leave it

    The cysts cannot complete their life cycle in an aquarium without snails and birds,[8] so they do not spread to tankmates, and light infections are mainly cosmetic. Keep the fish in good water and ask a vet only if the fish is heavily covered or unwell.

Preventing Harmful Black Spots in Bettas

You cannot and should not prevent genetic colour change, but you can prevent the spots that come from damage and poor water. Keep a betta in a heated, filtered tank rather than a bowl, test ammonia and nitrite regularly and keep them at zero, and do regular partial water changes.[4] Choose smooth decorations and silk or live plants so long fins are not torn, and avoid tankmates that nip fins. Clean water and fewer injuries mean fewer infections and fewer dark healing scars.[2] If you are choosing a betta and want its colours to stay fixed, avoid fish sold as marble, koi, candy or galaxy types.[1]

When Black Spots Need Expert Help

See an aquatic vet if: a black spot is raised, lumpy or growing week by week, or there are several nodules on the body or fins — bettas are prone to pigment-cell tumours, many of them malignant.[6][7] Act on the water today if: the spots appeared with lethargy, gasping, clamped fins or fin loss, which point to ammonia and poor water quality.[3] Flat black patches on an active, hungry betta, particularly a marble or koi type, do not need a vet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my white betta turning black?
Most often because it carries marble or mosaic colour genetics, which are common in white, pale and koi-type bettas.[1] These fish can gain and lose colour throughout their lives. If the fish is healthy and the spots are flat, it is a normal change, not a disease.
Are black spots on a betta contagious?
Colour changes, healing pigment and tumours are not contagious. True black spot disease is caused by a parasite, but it cannot spread between fish in an aquarium because it needs snails and fish-eating birds to complete its life cycle.[8]
Why are my betta's fin edges turning black?
Black fin edges usually mean the fins are healing after fin rot or damage, with pigment returning to the regrown tissue,[2] or they are part of a colour change. If the edges are still fraying, shrinking or look white and ragged, the fin rot is still active — test the water and improve it.
Can ammonia cause black spots on a betta?
Ammonia poisoning can darken a fish's colour and damages gills and skin,[3][4] and keepers often see dark patches on bettas from dirty, unfiltered tanks. Test the water; if ammonia is present, the black areas should stop spreading once the water is fixed, though they may take weeks to fade.
Should I treat my betta for black spot disease?
Usually not. Black spot disease is rare in indoor bettas, and flat black patches are far more likely to be colour change or healing pigment. Parasite medication given to a healthy marble betta only adds stress. If you are unsure, send clear photos to an aquatic vet before treating.
What does a betta tumour look like?
Pigment-cell tumours in bettas appear as white, grey or black lumps or plaques on the fins, body or head that stand up from the surface and usually grow over time.[6] They have been reported most often in young males and in royal blue and copper bettas. A vet needs to sample the lump to confirm what it is.
