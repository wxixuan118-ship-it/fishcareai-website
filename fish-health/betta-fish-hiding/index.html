Why Is My Betta Fish Hiding? Rest, Temperature, Tank Setup or Illness

Betta splendens
🐟 Species: Betta Fish
🔬 Problem: Hiding Constantly
📋 Category: behavioral
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated October 30, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: A betta fish hiding in plants or a cave is usually resting, cold or exposed, not sick. Wild bettas live in warm, shallow, densely planted paddies and marshes, and in a 2024 study pet bettas spent almost half their resting time on or against plants and shelters.[1][2] Check that the water is 24–28 °C and that the tank has cover and calm water first. A betta that hides and stops eating, clamps its fins, or shows gold dust or white spots is unwell.

A betta fish hiding for hours behind a plant or inside a cave looks worrying, but much of it is normal betta behaviour. Wild Betta splendens live in small, shallow, slow water such as paddy fields, ponds, marshes and lagoons in central Thailand.[1] There they rest among dense vegetation. They also breathe air from the surface through the labyrinth organ, so a resting betta often parks on a leaf just below the waterline.[10] Modern long-finned bettas rest more than wild fish, because big fins make swimming hard work.[4]

The useful question is not whether your betta hides, but when, where and with what else. A betta that rests in cover between active spells, eats eagerly and has open fins is fine. One that hides all day in a cold or bare tank is telling you about its environment. And a betta that hides while refusing food, clamping its fins or changing colour may be ill. This guide works through the causes in order of likelihood and shows how to tell them apart.

Why Is My Betta Fish Hiding? The Common Causes

Normal Resting and Sleeping

Most Likely

Bettas rest a lot, and they prefer to do it in cover. In a 2024 study, male bettas kept in furnished tanks spent about 47% of their resting time on or against plants or a hideout. One shy fish swam behind a plant whenever people approached.[2] Resting on a leaf near the surface, wedged into a plant, or lying in a cave is how a healthy betta behaves, especially at night and after meals.

Fin type matters too. A study of artificially selected bettas found that the larger the tail fin, the slower the fish's burst swimming speed.[4] Halfmoons and other heavy-finned varieties tire quickly, so they spend more time parked than a short-finned plakat. If your betta comes out to feed, explores in between rests and has fully spread fins, the hiding is rest, not illness.

Water Too Cold

Most Likely

Bettas are tropical fish. Wild bettas live in water of about 27–31 °C, and UF/IFAS advises that an unheated betta container should not sit in a room below 22 °C.[3] In cooler water a betta becomes sluggish, eats less and tucks itself away in a corner or at the bottom.

Cold is also a stressor that weakens a fish's immune defences.[5] A betta kept too cool for weeks is more likely to pick up infections, so warming the water is a fix in its own right and a way to prevent the illnesses further down this list. An unheated bowl or a small tank near a window or air conditioner is the usual culprit.

A Small, Bare Tank or Strong Filter Current

Most Likely

The 2024 study moved each betta between a 1.5-litre jar, small and medium tanks, and a 19-litre tank with or without plants and a shelter. In the large furnished tank the fish swam about 92 seconds more and rested about 109 seconds less in every ten minutes than in the jar.[2] In the bare tank they paced and bumped against the walls more, and foraged far less, than in the same tank with plants. They also avoided the area around the filter.[2] The authors concluded that every betta tank should have gravel, live plants and refuges.[2]

A betta in a bare tank therefore has two bad options: sit exposed, or squeeze into the only corner out of the current. Long-finned fish struggle most against flow.[4] If your betta always hides in the same calm spot, watch how the fins move near the filter outlet before blaming the fish.

A New Tank, Transport Stress or Poor Water

Possible

Shop bettas often spend weeks in small cups and shipping bags before they reach you.[3] Shipping and handling are recognised stressors in fish,[5] so a new betta may hide for several days before it starts exploring. Keep the lights dim, feed lightly and give it time.

If the tank itself is new, check the water. New tank syndrome, a build-up of ammonia or nitrite before the filter bacteria are established, usually happens within the first six weeks, and a tropical biofilter can take up to eight weeks to mature.[8] Bettas tolerate ammonia better than many aquarium fish, which is why they survive in dirty water for weeks.[6] That tolerance means a betta can look fairly normal while the water is poor, and hiding and resting may be the first sign. The toxic, un-ionised form of ammonia rises with temperature and pH, so a warm betta tank needs regular testing.[7]

Reflections, Tankmates or a Rival in View

Possible

Male bettas are territorial. A betta that can see its reflection in bare glass, or another male in a tank next door, may flare at it repeatedly and then retreat out of sight. Darker backgrounds, more plants and moving tanks apart usually end this within a day or two.

Tankmates can also be the problem. Fin-nipping species, or busy fish that crowd the surface at feeding time, can keep a slow, long-finned betta hidden for most of the day. Look for split or shortened fins and for a betta that only comes out once the other fish have settled.

Illness: Velvet, Ich or Other Infections

Less Common

Hiding is a common early sign of illness in any fish, but in a sick betta it rarely comes on its own. Velvet, caused by Piscinoodinium, can affect any freshwater fish. It gives the skin a fine brownish-gold dust and causes lethargy, which is why it is also called gold-dust or rust disease.[9] Ich typically shows as white dots on the skin, with lethargy, clamped fins and darker colouring.[9]

Shine a torch along the side of the fish in a dim room: the gold sheen of velvet is often easier to see that way. Hiding together with refusing food, clamped fins, rapid breathing, colour loss or visible spots means the fish needs closer attention. Hiding alone, in a fish that eats and swims normally, does not.

How to Tell Why Your Betta Is Hiding

Work through these checks in order. Most hiding bettas are explained by the first three, and none of those needs medication.

  1. 1
    Note when and where it hides

    Resting on a leaf near the surface, in a plant or in a cave, between spells of swimming and especially at night, is normal.[2][10] Hiding all day, including when you bring food, is the kind that needs an explanation.

  2. 2
    Measure the water temperature

    Use a thermometer, not the heater's dial. Below about 24 °C a betta is likely to be sluggish and withdrawn; wild fish live at 27–31 °C.[3]

  3. 3
    Test ammonia and nitrite

    Any reading above zero in a betta tank is worth acting on, because bettas can look normal for a long time in poor water.[6][7] This matters most in tanks set up within the last two months.[8]

  4. 4
    Look at cover and current

    Count the plants and hiding places, and watch the fins near the filter outlet. A bare tank, or one where the betta is pushed around by flow, explains a betta that always sits in the one sheltered corner.[2][4]

  5. 5
    Look closely at the fish

    Check that it eats, that the fins are spread, and that there are no white dots, gold dust, torn fins or swelling. Shine a torch from the side to look for velvet.[9]

What to Do About a Betta Fish Hiding All Day

  1. 1
    Heat the water to 24–28 °C

    Add an adjustable heater sized for the tank and check it with a separate thermometer. Raise the temperature over a day rather than all at once. Most cold, withdrawn bettas become more active within a few days of being warmed.[3]

  2. 2
    Give it cover close to the surface

    Add live or soft artificial plants, a cave and a leaf or floating plant near the waterline where it can rest and still reach air.[2][10] Keep gravel on the bottom. Bettas use cover more when they have it, and spend less time pacing the glass.[2]

  3. 3
    Calm the current

    Turn the filter down, baffle the outlet with a sponge, or switch to a gentle sponge filter. Long-finned bettas should be able to hold position anywhere in the tank without effort.[4]

  4. 4
    Correct water quality

    If ammonia or nitrite is above zero, change 25–50% of the water with temperature-matched, dechlorinated water and keep testing every day or two until both stay at zero.[7][8]

  5. 5
    Remove stressors

    Cover reflective glass, block the view of any neighbouring male, and rehome tankmates that nip fins or steal food.

  6. 6
    Treat illness only once you see signs

    Velvet and ich need specific treatment. Velvet usually needs repeated treatments to break its life cycle, and the outlook is guarded.[9] Do not add medication to a betta that is only hiding.

Preventing Hiding and Stress in Bettas

Keep a betta in a heated, filtered tank larger than a jar, with gravel, live plants and refuges, and gentle flow.[2] Hold the water at 24–28 °C,[3] keep ammonia and nitrite at zero with weekly partial water changes, and only add a betta to a tank whose filter has matured.[8] Leave a gap between the water and the lid so it can breathe at the surface,[10] and choose calm tankmates, or none.

When a Hiding Betta Fish Needs Expert Help

Act the same day if: the betta is lying on the bottom gasping, cannot right itself, or the water test shows ammonia or nitrite. That is a water-quality emergency, not shyness.[7] Talk to an aquatic vet if: the betta has stopped eating for more than a few days, shows gold dust, white spots, swelling or raised scales, or keeps getting worse after you have fixed temperature and water quality.[9] A betta fish hiding in cover that still eats and swims normally does not need a vet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a betta fish to hide?
Yes, in moderation. In a 2024 study bettas spent almost half their resting time on or against plants and shelters.[2] Hiding is a concern when it lasts all day, including at feeding time, or comes with other symptoms.
Why is my new betta hiding?
New bettas have usually spent weeks in cups and bags, and transport is stressful.[3][5] Expect a few days of hiding. Keep the water warm and clean, the lights dim, and feed lightly.
Why does my betta rest on leaves at the top of the tank?
Bettas breathe air from the surface, so resting just below the waterline saves them swimming up for each breath.[10] Long-finned bettas tire faster and rest more.[4]
What water temperature does a hiding betta need?
Keep the water at 24–28 °C. Wild bettas live at about 27–31 °C, and a betta in cold water becomes sluggish and withdrawn.[3]
Does a betta need hiding places?
Yes. Researchers who compared furnished and bare tanks recommended gravel, live plants and refuges in every betta tank.[2]
