Bristlenose Pleco Pale or Losing Colour? Normal Colour Change vs Stress and Illness

Ancistrus temminckii
🐟 Species: Bristlenose Pleco
🔬 Problem: Pale or Faded Color
📋 Category: physical
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated September 28, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: A bristlenose that turns light on pale sand, under bright light or at night and darkens again within minutes to hours is showing normal background colour change, not illness. Paleness that stays for days, or comes with hiding, rapid breathing, a sunken belly, white patches or excess slime, points to stress, poor water or disease — test the water first.

Bristlenose plecos (Ancistrus) are camouflage specialists. Like many fish, they can change shade within minutes by moving pigment inside their skin cells, a process that serves background matching, and that is controlled by light, hormones and the nervous system.[1] A bristlenose resting on dark driftwood can look almost black, and the same fish on pale sand or the white back of a filter can look washed-out grey or mottled beige. Owners often notice this for the first time when the fish is out in the open or after the lights come on, and assume it is sick.

So the first question is not what disease the fish has, but how long the paleness lasts and what comes with it. Colour that returns once the fish moves back to cover is normal. Colour that stays pale for days is the fish telling you something — most often that it is stressed, that the water is poor, that it is not getting enough to eat, or, less commonly, that it has parasites or an infection. The checks below separate those.

Why Is My Bristlenose Pleco Pale? The Common Causes

Normal Background and Light-Driven Colour Change

Most Likely

Rapid colour change in fish is mediated by chromatophores — pigment cells whose dark granules cluster at the centre of the cell to lighten the skin, or spread out to darken it — and one of its main functions is matching the background.[1] Bristlenose have a mottled brown-and-grey pattern with pale spots, and when they lighten the pattern can look faded or blotchy. Common triggers are a light-coloured substrate, bright overhead lighting, the fish resting on a pale surface such as glass or a filter intake, and the change between night and day.

Over weeks, a fish kept on a consistently light or dark background can also adjust the amount of pigment in its skin. Background light conditions and social interactions are recognised drivers of this slower, morphological colour change in teleost fish.[2] That means a bristlenose kept in a bright, bare tank may simply be a lighter fish than one kept among dark wood and leaves. If your pleco is active at night, grazes normally, has a full belly and darkens when it returns to cover, the pale colour is behaviour, not illness.

Stress: New Tank, No Cover or Harassment

Most Likely

Bristlenose are cave dwellers; males in particular select dark cavities in wood or rock and defend them.[3] A fish without a cave or a dark crevice to retreat into, one kept under strong light, or one being chased by other plecos or boisterous tankmates is under constant low-level stress, and stressed fish often look pale and blotchy. The same is true in the first days after purchase or a move.

Stress is not harmless: it compromises the fish's natural defences so that it cannot effectively protect itself from pathogens, and prolonged stress severely limits the immune system.[4] The stressors recognised by fish health specialists include poor water quality, crowding, aggression, bright light, handling and shipping.[4] A pale bristlenose that is also hiding all the time, or is repeatedly pushed out of every cave by a larger male, should be treated as a stressed fish.

Poor Water Quality

Possible

Plecos produce a lot of waste for their size and are often kept in tanks that are under-filtered for them. Ammonia is the most common hidden problem: at low concentrations it causes stress and damages the gills, fish exposed to it over time are more prone to bacterial infections, and because it is colourless and odourless the only way to detect it is to test the water.[5] The toxic un-ionised fraction increases as pH and temperature rise,[5] so a warm tank at the top of the bristlenose's 23–27 °C range with a pH above 7 is less forgiving.

Water-quality paleness usually affects more than one fish, often comes with faster breathing or fish sitting near the surface or the filter outflow, and follows an event such as a filter clean, a missed water change, overfeeding or a dead fish or snail left in the tank. It improves within a day or two of fixing the water.

Underfeeding and Poor Nutrition

Possible

Nutrition is one of the primary factors behind longer-term colour change in fish,[2] and a hungry pleco is a surprisingly common problem. Many bristlenose are bought to eat algae and then given nothing else, or live in tanks where faster fish take every wafer before the pleco finds it. In the wild, loricariids feed on detritus and algae,[8] and even the wood-rasping species gain little from the wood itself: plecos fed wood in trials lost weight.[9] A bristlenose in a clean, algae-poor tank with only driftwood to graze may be slowly starving.

The tell-tale sign is the belly. Look at the fish from underneath while it is on the glass: a well-fed bristlenose has a gently rounded belly, while an underfed one has a flat or concave belly and a thin area behind the head. Pale colour together with a sunken belly means the fish needs more food, delivered after lights-out when it can find it.

Parasites and Infection

Less Common

Several external parasites cause paleness. With gill and skin flukes (monogeneans), fish become pale as their colours fade and breathe rapidly with swollen, pale gills; ciliates such as Chilodonella and trichodinids cause loss of condition, and Chilodonella often causes excess mucus.[6] White spot (Ichthyophthirius) is easy to recognise by its distinct white dots. Bacterial disease should be suspected when pale colour comes with white or grey patches, frayed fins, reddened areas or ulcers.

Chronic infection is a rarer cause. Mycobacteriosis, for example, produces variable, nonspecific signs that include paleness, emaciation, skin ulcers and bulging eyes.[7] Infection is most likely in a recently bought fish, in a tank where new fish were added without quarantine, or where the water has been poor for some time.

How to Tell Why Your Bristlenose Is Pale

Start with the simplest explanation. Most pale bristlenose are explained by the first two checks and need nothing more than cover and time.

  1. 1
    Note when and where the fish looks pale

    Is it pale only when it sits on sand, glass or the filter, only just after the lights come on, or only at night? Does it darken again when it moves onto wood or back into a cave? Colour that comes and goes with the background and the lighting is normal physiological colour change.[1] Take a photo in the morning and another in the evening for a couple of days; if the colour varies, it is behaviour, not disease.

  2. 2
    Watch its behaviour and breathing

    A healthy bristlenose hides in the day, comes out at dusk and grazes steadily. Rapid gill movement, gasping, sitting at the surface or near the filter outflow, repeated rubbing against surfaces, or clamped fins alongside the pale colour point to water quality or gill parasites rather than camouflage.[5][6]

  3. 3
    Check the belly from underneath

    Wait until the fish is on the front glass and look at its underside. A flat or sunken belly and a thin head area mean it is not getting enough food. A rounded belly in an active fish means diet is not the problem.[2]

  4. 4
    Test the water

    Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature. Ammonia and nitrite should both read zero in an established tank, and any ammonia is more toxic at higher pH and temperature.[5] Aim for about 23–27 °C and pH 6.5–7.5 for bristlenose, with good water movement and oxygenation.

    Recommended: Water test kit
  5. 5
    Look closely at the skin, fins and gills

    Under good light, check for white dots, white or grey fuzzy patches, a cloudy film of mucus, frayed fins, red streaks or ulcers. Any of these alongside paleness makes parasites or infection much more likely, and a skin scrape or gill examination by an aquatic vet can confirm which.[6][7]

  6. 6
    Give it cover and a quiet day

    Add a cave or tube per bristlenose, dim or shorten the lighting, and see whether the colour returns within 24 hours. A fish that darkens once it has a proper hiding place was stressed or trying to match a pale background.

What to Do for a Pale Bristlenose Pleco

  1. 1
    Provide caves, wood and shade

    Give each bristlenose at least one cave, pipe or tight crevice, with an extra one if you keep more than one male, since males defend cavities against other males.[3] Driftwood, leaf litter, floating plants and a darker substrate give the fish somewhere dark to settle and reduce the background-driven lightening. Keep the tank out of direct sunlight and avoid very bright lighting over bare areas.

  2. 2
    Fix the water first if the test shows a problem

    If ammonia or nitrite is detectable, do a partial water change with dechlorinated water at the same temperature, cut feeding for a day or two, and check that the filter is running and has not been over-cleaned. Repeat testing daily until both read zero. Reducing ammonia exposure lowers the risk of the bacterial infections that follow chronic stress.[5]

  3. 3
    Feed the pleco, not just the tank

    Offer sinking food after lights-out, when bristlenose feed and faster fish are resting: blanched courgette, cucumber, peas or green beans, plus algae wafers a few times a week. Leave vegetables in overnight and remove leftovers in the morning. Do not rely on driftwood or tank algae alone to feed a bristlenose.[8][9]

  4. 4
    If you see parasites or lesions, get them identified

    White spot, flukes and bacterial infections each need a different treatment. Have a skin or gill sample examined by an aquatic vet where possible — a wet mount under the microscope is how most external parasites are identified[6] — and if you use an over-the-counter product, choose one labelled for the problem you have identified and follow the product label exactly. Many parasite medications are harmful to snails and shrimp, so remove them first if the label says so.

  5. 5
    Reduce long-term stress

    Rehome aggressive tankmates, avoid overcrowding, and keep routine changes gradual. Good water quality, good nutrition and good sanitation are the core of stress prevention.[4] Quarantine new fish for at least 30 days before adding them to the main tank, so a new arrival does not bring parasites to a pleco that is already under strain.[10]

Keeping a Bristlenose Well Coloured

Give bristlenose the environment they are built for: a cave per fish, dark wood and cover, moderate lighting, good water movement and a temperature in the middle of their 23–27 °C range. Feed a vegetable-based diet in the evening and check the belly shape regularly. Keep ammonia and nitrite at zero with routine partial water changes, and test whenever colour changes suddenly.[5] Quarantine new fish for a month.[10] Expect some day-to-day variation in shade — a bristlenose that lightens on sand and darkens on wood is doing exactly what its skin is designed to do.[1]

When a Pale Bristlenose Needs Expert Help

Act today if: the fish is pale and breathing fast, gasping, lying on its side, or has stopped grazing entirely; test the water immediately and do a partial water change if anything is out of range.[5] Talk to an aquatic vet if: the paleness persists for more than a few days despite good water and cover, you see white patches, excess mucus, ulcers or red streaks, or the fish is losing weight while eating — these point to parasites or infection that need a diagnosis.[6][7] A bristlenose that is pale only on sand or under bright light, and darkens again in its cave, does not need a vet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my bristlenose change colour?
Fish change shade by moving pigment granules inside skin cells called melanophores, spreading them to darken and clumping them to lighten. This rapid colour change is used for background matching and is controlled by light and by hormonal and nervous signals.[1] A bristlenose on dark wood will be darker than the same fish on light sand a few minutes later.
Why is my bristlenose pale at night?
Bristlenose are most active after dark, when they leave their caves and graze in the open on the substrate, glass and decor. Out of cover and under changing light, they adjust their shade to the new background.[1] Colour that is paler at night and returns in the day, in a fish that is feeding and active, is normal rather than a sign of illness.
My new bristlenose is very pale. Is it sick?
Usually it is stressed. Transport, handling and a new tank are all recognised stressors,[4] and a new bristlenose that has not yet found a cave will often sit pale in a corner. Give it a cave, dim the lights and test the water; most new fish colour up within a few days. If it stays pale for more than a week, has white spots or patches, or will not eat, look for disease.
Is my bristlenose an albino or turning white?
Albino, lemon and other light-coloured bristlenose varieties are common in the trade and are pale from birth, with pink or red eyes in albinos. A normally coloured fish that is turning white, fuzzy or blotchy is different and needs checking for parasites, bacterial infection and water quality.[6][7]
Can poor diet make a pleco lose colour?
Yes. Nutrition is one of the main drivers of longer-term colour change in fish,[2] and underfed bristlenose tend to look dull and pale, with a sunken belly. Plecos cannot live on driftwood or tank algae alone;[9] feed vegetables and algae-based food after lights-out.
