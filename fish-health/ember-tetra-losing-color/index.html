Ember Tetra Losing Color or Pale: Why It Happens and How to Get the Orange Back

Hyphessobrycon amandae
🐟 Species: Ember Tetra
🔬 Problem: Losing Color
📋 Category: physical
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated September 28, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: Pale ember tetras are most often new, young or female fish that have not coloured up yet, or fish that are stressed by a small group, big tankmates or a bright, bare tank. Colour normally builds over a few weeks in a settled, planted tank with a varied diet. Only worry if one fish is pale and thin, hiding alone or swimming oddly.

The ember tetra is one of the smallest tetras in the hobby — about 2 cm long — and comes from the Araguaia River basin in central Brazil,[1] where it lives in quiet side-channels and oxbow lakes over leaf litter in soft, weakly acidic water.[2] Its whole appeal is its glowing orange-red, and because that colour is so uniform, a dull, beige or almost translucent ember is easy to notice and easy to worry about.

Most of the time, a pale ember is a fish that has not had the chance to colour up. Shop fish are often small juveniles, recently shipped, and kept in bright, bare tanks; females are naturally less intense than males;[2] and like other small tetras, embers fade at night and when frightened, because the pigment in their colour cells can be pulled in and spread out again as conditions change.[3][4] Colour that never develops or fades over weeks points instead to the tank: too few companions, intimidating tankmates, water problems, or a diet short of the carotenoid pigments that fish cannot make for themselves.[5] Disease is the least likely cause, but it is the one to rule out when a single fish is affected.

Why Is My Ember Tetra Losing Its Color? The Common Causes

Young, Female or Newly Bought Fish That Haven't Coloured Up Yet

Most Likely

This is the most common reason new owners search for this problem. Ember tetras in shops are often sold at a small size, have just been through shipping and holding, and are usually kept in plain, brightly lit tanks — all of which keep them pale. It is normal for a new group to look washed-out, even pinkish-beige, for the first week or two, and for the orange to deepen gradually over the following weeks as they settle and mature.

Sex matters as well. Adult males are more intensely coloured, especially when in spawning condition, while females are rounder-bodied and noticeably less vivid.[2] In a mixed group it is entirely normal for some embers to be bright and others more subdued. The key signs that a pale ember is healthy are simple: it eats readily, schools with the others and has a well-rounded body.

Stress, Night-Time Fade and Feeling Exposed

Most Likely

Fish change colour physiologically by moving pigment inside their colour cells. Research on the red skin of two related tetras (the neon and cardinal) showed that the red pigment clumps and the skin blanches at night or in the dark, driven mainly by melatonin, and that the same cells also pale in response to norepinephrine, the messenger of the fight-or-flight response.[3] Chromatophores in general let fish adjust their colour to the background around them and to encounters with other fish.[4] In practice, embers look faded first thing in the morning, when the light is switched on suddenly, after a chase, and in a tank with a pale substrate and little cover — and colour up again once they feel secure.

Group size and tankmates are the biggest long-term stressors for such a tiny fish. In a study of neon tetras, aggression and darting were lowest and feeding fastest in the largest groups, and groups of ten shoaled the most.[6] Embers are peaceful and rather shy, and a minimum of eight to ten is recommended because they become less shy in a proper group.[2] A handful of embers kept with larger, active fish such as barbs, larger tetras or bettas that chase them will often stay pale indefinitely.

A Diet Short of Carotenoids

Possible

Red, orange and yellow colours in fish depend largely on carotenoid pigments. Most animals, fish included, cannot make carotenoids from scratch, so the amount that ends up in their tissues depends on the amount in their food.[5] In a feeding trial on another small red Hyphessobrycon tetra, fish fed diets supplemented with astaxanthin or beta-carotene stored the pigment almost entirely as astaxanthin (over 98% of their body carotenoids), and body carotenoid levels rose as the dietary dose increased.[7] We found no study measuring pigments in ember tetras specifically, but their orange-red is the kind of colour that depends on what they eat.

Embers are micropredators that eat tiny invertebrates in the wild, and they do best on small dry foods backed up by daily live or frozen foods such as Artemia nauplii, Moina or other small crustaceans.[2] A group fed only one cheap flake for months will often look dull even in an otherwise good tank. Diet changes act slowly: expect to see a difference over several weeks, not days.

Poor or Unstable Water

Possible

Water problems usually show up in behaviour before colour, but fading is often part of the picture. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes new tank syndrome — ammonia and nitrite from an immature filter — as a problem of the first six weeks or so, in which fish become lethargic, stop eating and frequently die; and old tank syndrome, where infrequent water changes let alkalinity run out and the pH fall, sometimes suddenly.[8] Embers bought for a newly set-up nano tank are especially exposed to the first.

Embers come from soft, weakly acidic water,[2] and most farmed fish adapt to a range of conditions. What matters most is stable, clean water at about 22–28 °C. If several embers are pale, sluggish and off their food at the same time, test the water before doing anything else.

Disease: Neon Tetra Disease and Chronic Infections

Less Common

Neon tetra disease, caused by the microsporidian Pleistophora hyphessobryconis, infects the skeletal muscle of tetras and many other aquarium fish; Merck describes abnormal swimming from muscle damage and notes that microsporidia are considered untreatable.[9] The parasite has been reported from many aquarium fishes and has been transmitted experimentally from an infected neon tetra to zebrafish.[10] In a small orange fish, the tell-tale sign is a whitish, opaque patch in the muscle that stays pale in daylight and spreads, usually in one fish at a time.

Mycobacteriosis is another chronic cause of paleness, along with emaciation, sores, bulging eyes and a bent spine, and it cannot be eliminated with treatment.[11] Both diseases pick off individuals over weeks rather than dulling a whole group overnight, which is how to tell them apart from the environmental causes above.

How to Tell Why Your Ember Tetras Are Pale

Go through these checks in order. Most pale embers are explained by the first three, and none of them needs medication.

  1. 1
    Check the time of day and the lights

    Look at the fish in the middle of the day, after the light has been on for at least an hour. Tetras' red skin blanches in the dark,[3] so embers seen at dawn, just after lights-on or by torchlight at night are expected to look pale.

  2. 2
    Work out how new and how old the fish are

    Embers bought in the last two to four weeks, and small juveniles, are often pale while they settle. Compare body shapes: rounder, less vivid fish are usually females, and slimmer, brighter ones are males.[2]

  3. 3
    Is it the whole group or one fish?

    All the embers pale together points to the environment — lighting, background, group size, tankmates, water or diet. One ember pale while the rest are bright points to that individual: bullying, injury or disease.[9][11]

  4. 4
    Look at the tank from the fish's point of view

    Count the embers (fewer than eight is a problem[2]), note any larger or faster tankmates, and look at the substrate, background and cover. A pale bottom, bright light and few plants tend to make small tetras pale,[4] while a dark substrate and plenty of plants help them colour up.[2]

  5. 5
    Test the water

    Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH. Ammonia and nitrite should be zero. A tank set up in the last six weeks, or one that has gone a long time without water changes, is the classic setting for water-quality problems.[8]

  6. 6
    Review the diet and watch the pale fish for a week

    Note what they eat and how often. Then check whether any pale ember is thin, swims jerkily, stays apart from the shoal or develops a whitish patch in the muscle — signs that need the disease causes taken seriously.[9]

How to Bring Back an Ember Tetra's Orange Color

  1. 1
    Give new fish time in a quiet, dark-bottomed, planted tank

    Keep lighting moderate and on a regular timer, add live or floating plants and some leaf litter or wood, and use a dark substrate — the set-up recommended for this species.[2] Avoid rearranging the tank while they settle. Most new embers show noticeably more colour within two to four weeks.

  2. 2
    Keep a proper group with gentle tankmates

    Keep at least 8–10 embers, more in bigger tanks; small shoaling tetras are calmer and feed better in larger groups.[6] Remove or rehome fish that chase them, and choose similarly small, peaceful companions.

  3. 3
    Feed a varied, colour-rich diet

    Feed a good-quality micro-pellet or crushed flake that lists carotenoid sources, plus small live or frozen foods such as baby brine shrimp, daphnia or Moina several times a week.[2] Fish depend on food for these pigments,[5] and in a related tetra body carotenoid levels rose with dietary content;[7] give it several weeks to show.

  4. 4
    Correct and stabilise the water

    Fix any ammonia or nitrite with partial water changes and reduced feeding, and then keep up regular weekly changes so the pH stays stable and does not drift down as buffering is used up.[8] Keep the temperature steady within about 22–28 °C.

  5. 5
    Isolate a single sick-looking fish

    If one ember is pale and wasting, swims abnormally or has a spreading white patch in the muscle, remove it to protect the others. Neon tetra disease and mycobacteriosis have no reliable treatment,[9][11] so the goal is to stop spread — do not add new fish for several months and ask an aquatic vet to confirm the cause if more fish are affected.

Keeping Ember Tetras Bright

Buy a group of ten or more at once, from a tank where the fish look active and well fed, and quarantine them for a few weeks before adding them to an established tank. Give them what they come from: a mature, heavily planted tank with a dark substrate, subdued light, floating plants and soft to moderately hard, stable water.[1][2] Feed small amounts of a varied diet with regular live or frozen foods,[2][5] keep tankmates small and peaceful, and keep up regular partial water changes so water quality never becomes the hidden stressor.[8]

When a Pale Ember Tetra Needs Expert Help

Act the same day if: several embers are pale, listless, gasping or not eating, particularly in a new tank — test the water and change some of it, because ammonia and nitrite problems kill quickly.[8] Talk to an aquatic vet if: embers are wasting away one after another, develop white patches in the muscle, bent spines or sores, or swim erratically. These point to neon tetra disease or mycobacteriosis, which are confirmed in the laboratory and cannot be cured; the aim is to protect the rest of the tank.[9][11] A group that is pale in the morning and orange by midday, or a new group slowly colouring up, needs patience, not treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take for ember tetras to color up?
Newly bought embers usually show clearly more colour within two to four weeks in a quiet, planted tank with a dark substrate and a varied diet. Full colour in males can take longer, and it is strongest when they are in spawning condition.[2]
Why are some of my ember tetras paler than others?
Usually because they are females. Adult male embers are more intensely coloured, especially in breeding condition, while females are rounder and more subdued.[2] A pale fish that is also thin, alone or swimming oddly is a different matter.
Why do my ember tetras lose their color at night?
It is normal. The red skin of tetras blanches in the dark as pigment clumps inside the colour cells, mainly under the influence of the night-time hormone melatonin.[3] The colour comes back after the lights have been on for a while.
What food brings out color in ember tetras?
A varied diet including small live or frozen foods such as baby brine shrimp, daphnia and Moina, alongside a quality micro-pellet that contains carotenoids.[2] Fish cannot make carotenoid pigments themselves and rely on their food for them.[5] Results take weeks.
Can too few ember tetras make them lose color?
Yes, indirectly. Small shoaling tetras are more stressed, dart more and feed less readily in small groups,[6] and stressed fish are paler. Embers are recommended in groups of at least eight to ten, when they become noticeably less shy.[2]
Is my pale ember tetra sick?
Probably not if it is eating, schooling and well-rounded. Suspect illness if one fish becomes pale and thin, stops eating, swims erratically or develops a whitish patch in the muscle; these can be signs of neon tetra disease or mycobacteriosis, neither of which is treatable.[9][11]
