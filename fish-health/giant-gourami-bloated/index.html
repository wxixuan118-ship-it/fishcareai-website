Giant Gourami Bloated or Swollen? Diet, Eggs or Infection — How to Tell

Osphronemus goramy
🐟 Species: Giant Gourami
🔬 Problem: Bloated or Swollen
📋 Category: physical
⚡ Urgency: monitor

Updated September 28, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team

Short answer: A giant gourami that is swollen but still eating and active is most often overfed on protein-rich pellets, meaty food or feeder fish — this is a plant-leaning omnivore. A large, several-year-old female can also be full of eggs. Swelling with bulging eyes, lifted scales, red blotches, ulcers or a fish that stops eating points to bacterial infection and needs action today.

The giant gourami (Osphronemus goramy) is not a big version of a dwarf or honey gourami. It reaches up to about 70 cm, breathes air as an obligate air-breather, and is an omnivore that eats aquatic plants as well as animals.[1] In its native Southeast Asia it is farmed as a food fish, and the aquaculture literature describes it as an herbivorous species that readily eats leaves such as taro, cassava and kale.[2] In home aquaria and ponds, however, giant gouramis are often raised on the same high-protein pellets, prawns and feeder fish given to other large fish, and they beg constantly. That mismatch is the most common reason a pet giant gourami looks bloated.

The next question is age and sex. Giant gouramis mature slowly — in one hatchery study, juveniles were grown for five years before being used as mature broodfish — and a ripe female carries thousands of large eggs.[3] Once food and eggs are ruled out, the causes worth knowing about are the ones that dominate giant gourami health in aquaculture: bacterial infections, especially Aeromonas, and the megalocytivirus ISKNV.[6][8] The steps below help you tell them apart.

Why Is My Giant Gourami Bloated? The Common Causes

Overfeeding and a Diet Too Rich in Protein

Most Likely

Giant gouramis are big, intelligent fish that learn to beg at the front of the tank, and owners respond. In farming, even juveniles grown for food are fed a measured ration of pellets containing about 30% protein,[2] and broodfish in one study were given taro leaves at about 1% of body weight a day alongside their pellets.[3] In comparison, a pet giant gourami fed several meals a day of high-protein cichlid pellets, shrimp, meat or feeder fish is taking in far more concentrated food than it needs, and can become visibly round, heavy in the belly, and fat.

The pattern is typical: the belly is evenly rounded, it is largest after feeding, the fish is alert and still begging, and droppings are plentiful or, when it is constipated, sparse. A short fast and a change toward plant foods will bring the belly down within days. A fish that stays swollen after that, or that is swollen and not eating, has something else going on.

A Mature Female Full of Eggs

Possible

Giant gouramis take several years to mature. In a study of broodstock in Indonesia, juveniles were reared for five years before 20 mature fish were selected for spawning.[3] Females in that study carried roughly 1,000 eggs per kilogram of body weight and laid 2,000 to 2,650 eggs per nest, each egg about 2.4 mm across.[3] A female carrying that many eggs has a noticeably fuller, deeper belly for weeks before spawning.

The male does the building: over five to seven days he constructs a nest of plant fibres, after which the female lays her eggs in it and tends them there.[3] FishBase notes that the nest is made of plant fibres, not bubbles as sometimes claimed.[1] In an aquarium without a mate or nesting material, a ripe female may stay full for a long time. A young giant gourami — the size most people keep in tanks — is unlikely to be carrying eggs, so this explanation fits mainly large adult females that are eating and behaving normally.

Bacterial Infection and Dropsy

Possible

Aeromonas hydrophila is the bacterial pathogen most often studied in giant gourami. In fish from Indonesian farms, isolates from diseased gouramis killed 40–100% of experimentally infected fish, causing ulcers, haemorrhages at the base of the fins, on the body and mouth, and bulging eyes.[6] In challenge studies the infection produced most of the major clinical signs and mass mortality, although selectively bred lines were more resistant.[7]

In any fish, fluid collecting in the abdomen — dropsy — is a sign of serious internal disease rather than a diagnosis in its own right.[4] Bacterial outbreaks in aquarium fish are frequently associated with poor water quality and organic loading,[5] which is a real risk for giant gouramis kept in tanks that are too small for their size and waste output. Swelling together with red blotches, ulcers, lifted scales, bulging eyes or a loss of appetite should be treated as infection, not diet.

Megalocytivirus (ISKNV, "Giant Gourami Iridovirus")

Less Common

Infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV) is a proven killer of giant gouramis. In Indonesia it has been the main pathogen in giant gourami outbreaks since 2011; a strain named giant gourami iridovirus caused 93% mortality within 12 days in experimentally infected fish, whose signs were lethargy, loss of appetite, pale or darkened colour and haemorrhages, with swollen spleen and kidney inside.[8] ISKNV was also isolated from farmed giant gouramis in India during large-scale mortality.[9]

Across ornamental fish, megalocytivirus infections can cause a distended body cavity along with lethargy, darkening, pale gills and heavy losses, and there is no effective treatment.[10] For a single long-kept pet giant gourami in a closed tank, this is an unlikely cause of bloating. It becomes a real possibility in a young fish recently bought from a farm or importer, especially if several newly bought fish are sick or dying.

Tumours and Internal Disease in Older Fish

Less Common

Giant gouramis are long-lived, and older fish can develop internal problems. In other large pond fish, reproductive tumours cause a swollen abdomen with gradual loss of body condition,[4] and a slowly enlarging, sometimes lopsided belly in an older giant gourami that does not change with feeding raises the same possibility. Chronic bacterial infection such as mycobacteriosis can also cause fluid in the abdomen with emaciation, and cannot be cured.[5] These need an aquatic vet, who may use imaging or sampling to make a diagnosis.

How to Tell Why Your Giant Gourami Is Bloated

Work through these in order. Most swollen giant gouramis that are still eating are explained by the first two checks.

  1. 1
    Write down everything it eats in a day

    List every meal, treat and feeder fish, including food meant for tankmates. Several meals of pellets, prawns or meat a day is a diet problem in a fish that naturally eats a great deal of plant matter.[1][2]

  2. 2
    Consider its age, size and sex

    A fish that is still juvenile-coloured and under a few years old is very unlikely to be carrying eggs. Only large, mature adults do, and broodstock in aquaculture are typically several years old.[3] Sexing giant gouramis by eye is unreliable, so treat egg-bearing as possible only in big adults.

  3. 3
    Look for signs of infection

    Check the whole body under good light: red blotches or streaks, ulcers, haemorrhages at the fin bases or mouth, bulging eyes, and scales lifting away from the body. These are the typical signs of Aeromonas infection in giant gourami.[6] Note also whether the fish has stopped eating or is lethargic, which were early signs in ISKNV outbreaks.[8]

  4. 4
    Test the water

    Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature. A giant gourami produces a great deal of waste, and ammonia rises quickly in an undersized tank; it is colourless and odourless, so testing is the only way to know it is there, and chronic exposure makes fish more prone to bacterial infection.[11] Aim for about 22–28 °C and pH 6–7.5.

    Recommended: Water test kit
  5. 5
    Fast for two to three days and watch

    A healthy adult giant gourami tolerates a short fast easily. If the belly shrinks, it was food. If it stays the same in an active, eating adult, eggs remain possible. If it grows, or the fish becomes lethargic, stops eating or develops any of the signs above, treat it as infection.

What to Do for a Bloated Giant Gourami

  1. 1
    If it is diet: fast, then shift to plant foods

    Stop feeding for two to three days, then feed once a day. Make leafy greens and vegetables the backbone of the diet — lettuce, kale, blanched spinach, peas, courgette and aquatic plants such as duckweed — with a quality pellet a few times a week rather than every meal. Farmed giant gouramis eat leaves such as kale and cassava as part of their diet,[2] and broodfish were given leaves daily.[3] Cut out feeder fish and fatty meats.

  2. 2
    If she is carrying eggs: no treatment needed

    A ripe female who is eating and active needs no medication. Keep the water clean and stable. Breeding a pair takes a very large tank or pond and nesting material, since the male builds a nest of plant fibres;[1][3] without that, a ripe female may simply stay full-bellied for some time while otherwise healthy.

  3. 3
    If it looks infected: fix the water and call a vet

    Do a large partial water change, check the filter, and reduce stocking if the tank is overcrowded. A giant gourami is too large to move to a hospital tank easily, so correcting the main tank is often the practical first step. Antibiotic choice should be based on culture and sensitivity testing where possible,[5] and an aquatic vet can take samples and prescribe an effective drug; follow their instructions or the product label exactly.

  4. 4
    If new fish are dying: isolate and get a laboratory diagnosis

    If a recently bought giant gourami, or several new fish, sicken and die, stop adding fish and have a dead fish examined by a fish health laboratory. Megalocytivirus is diagnosed from internal organs, it cannot be treated, and depopulation followed by disinfection is recommended when it is confirmed; use separate nets and equipment for each tank in the meantime.[10]

Preventing Bloating in Giant Gouramis

Feed a giant gourami like the plant-leaning omnivore it is: mostly greens and vegetables, pellets as a supplement, no feeder fish, and one modest meal a day for adults however hard it begs.[1][2] House it in a tank or pond large enough for a fish that can reach 70 cm, with powerful filtration and frequent partial water changes to keep ammonia at zero.[11] Quarantine every new fish for at least 30 days before adding it to your system,[12] because both Aeromonas and ISKNV are serious diseases of this species in farms, and farms are where young giant gouramis come from.[6][9]

When a Bloated Giant Gourami Needs Expert Help

Act today if: the swelling comes with red blotches, ulcers, bleeding at the fin bases or mouth, bulging eyes, lifted scales, or a fish that has stopped eating and is lethargic — these fit bacterial infection, which can kill a large proportion of affected giant gouramis.[6][7] Talk to an aquatic vet if: the swelling does not go down after a week of reduced, plant-based feeding, the belly is lopsided or slowly growing in an older fish, or recently bought fish are dying — tumours, chronic infection and ISKNV all need a proper diagnosis.[4][8] An adult that is round, active, begging and eating well needs a diet change, not medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a giant gourami eat?
Mostly plants. In the wild and on farms it is an omnivore with a strong vegetarian leaning that eats aquatic weeds and leaves as well as animal food.[1][2] Leafy greens, vegetables and aquatic plants should form most of the diet, with pellets as a supplement. Feeder fish and fatty meats are not needed and contribute to bloating.
Is my giant gourami pregnant?
Giant gouramis lay eggs, so they are never pregnant, but a mature female can be full of eggs — around a thousand per kilogram of body weight, or a few thousand per spawning.[3] Only large adults carry eggs; a young fish with a round belly is almost always overfed.
How old is a giant gourami before it can breed?
Several years. In an Indonesian broodstock study, fish were reared for five years to sexual maturity before spawning.[3] Most giant gouramis kept in home aquaria are younger than that.
Can giant gouramis get iridovirus like dwarf gouramis?
Yes. Infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus, the same megalocytivirus group that includes dwarf gourami iridovirus, has caused major outbreaks in farmed giant gouramis in Indonesia and India, with very high mortality.[8][9] There is no effective treatment.[10] The risk is highest in young fish recently imported or bought from farms.
Can a bloated giant gourami recover?
If the cause is diet, almost always — a fast and a change to plant foods bring the belly down within days. Bacterial infection can often be treated if caught early and the water is corrected, but it can kill many affected fish once advanced.[6][7] Viral infection and advanced dropsy have a poor outlook.[4][10]
