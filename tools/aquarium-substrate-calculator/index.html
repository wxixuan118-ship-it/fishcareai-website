How Much Substrate Do I Need for My Aquarium?

The relationship is simple: tank footprint × substrate depth = substrate volume. The footprint is the inside length multiplied by the inside width — the area of glass the substrate actually covers. Multiply that area by the depth of bed you want and you have the volume you need to buy.

This is why a fish tank substrate calculator asks for dimensions rather than gallons. Substrate lies on the bottom pane, so the tank's height contributes nothing to the amount you need. Two aquariums rated at the same capacity can require very different quantities: a 20 gallon long has a 30 × 12 inch footprint, while a 20 gallon high is 24 × 12 inches. At a 2 inch bed depth that is 11.8 litres versus 9.4 litres — about 25% more substrate for the same nominal tank size.

So when you ask "how much substrate do I need," the honest answer is that it depends on the shape of the bottom of your tank and how deep you want the bed to be. Measure the inside dimensions with a tape measure — outside measurements include the glass thickness and the trim, and on a rimmed tank that can add half an inch or more to each side.

One more practical point: the volume the calculator returns is the volume of the bed after it settles flat. Rocks, driftwood and large decorations displace some substrate, while a sloped or terraced aquascape needs a little more than a flat bed of the same average depth. That is what the 10% purchase buffer in the result panel is for.

Aquarium Gravel Calculator

Used as an aquarium gravel calculator, this tool works out gravel quantity the same way it handles every other substrate: it multiplies the tank footprint by the bed depth to get a volume, then multiplies that volume by gravel's bulk density to estimate weight. Gravel is sold by weight, so the weight figure is the one you take to the shop; the volume figure is the one that stays true no matter which brand you buy.

Typical gravel depths

Most freshwater community tanks run 1–2 inches of gravel. That is enough to anchor decorations, hide the base of the tank and give beneficial bacteria a surface to colonise, without creating pockets deep enough to go anaerobic. Planted tanks with gravel usually go deeper, 2–3 inches, so root systems have somewhere to sit. Below about an inch, gravel tends to shift and expose the glass whenever fish dig or you run a siphon.

Why gravel density varies

Aquarium gravel is usually quartz or river stone with a solid density around 2.6 g/cm³, but a bag of gravel is not solid rock — roughly 40% of it is air between the grains. That gives a bulk density near 1.6 g/cm³ for standard 2–5 mm gravel. Finer grades pack more tightly and come in slightly heavier per litre; large, irregular or coated decorative gravels can be lighter. Damp gravel weighs more than dry gravel straight from the bag.

Treat the number as an estimate

Because bulk density shifts with grain size, shape, coating and moisture, two 20 lb bags of "aquarium gravel" from different manufacturers will not fill the same space. The calculator's weight output is a good planning figure, typically within about 10% for common gravels, and the purchase buffer is there to absorb the rest. If a product lists both weight and volume on the bag, entering that as a custom density gives you the most accurate result.

Aquarium Sand Calculator

The same tool doubles as an aquarium sand calculator — select Sand from the substrate list and the density switches from a gravel value to a sand value of about 1.55 g/cm³. The geometry does not change; only the weight per litre does.

  • Shallow beds work better. Sand grains sit tightly together, so 1–2 inches is the usual range. Deep, undisturbed sand can develop anaerobic pockets, which is why sand beds are generally kept shallower than gravel beds unless you are deliberately running a deep sand bed in a marine system.
  • Planted tanks. Sand alone holds few nutrients, so planted setups often cap a nutrient-rich soil layer with sand, or use root tabs. If you are layering, run the calculator once for each layer's depth and add the results.
  • Compaction. Sand settles and compacts more than gravel, so a freshly poured bed loses a little depth in the first few weeks. Stirring lightly during water changes, or keeping burrowing snails or Malaysian trumpet snails, keeps it turned over.
  • Density differences. Fine silica sand, play sand, aragonite sand and black diamond blasting sand all weigh different amounts per litre. Aragonite is around 1.45 g/cm³ and also raises hardness and pH, which suits African cichlid and marine tanks but not soft-water community tanks.

Once your bed is in, keep an eye on the water while it clears — the Water Parameter Checker is a quick way to confirm pH and hardness have not shifted after adding a mineral-based substrate such as crushed coral or aragonite.

What Substrate Depth Should I Use?

Aquarium substrate depth is the input people are least sure about, so here are the ranges most setups fall into.

Common aquarium substrate depth ranges by setup type.
Aquarium setupTypical substrate depth
Bare-bottom / minimal0–0.5 in (0–1.3 cm)
Decorative gravel1–2 in (2.5–5 cm)
Standard freshwater aquarium1–2 in (2.5–5 cm)
Planted aquarium2–3 in (5–7.6 cm)
Deep-rooted planted aquarium3–4 in (7.6–10 cm)
Sand bed1–2 in (2.5–5 cm)

Note: these are general starting points, not strict rules. Substrate depth depends on plant species, aquascape design, livestock, and substrate type. Burrowing fish and loaches need enough depth to dig without hitting glass; bare-bottom setups are common in breeding and quarantine tanks precisely because they are easier to keep clean.

Gravel vs Sand vs Aquarium Soil

Weight is only half the decision. Here is how the four most common substrate families compare in practice.

Practical comparison of common aquarium substrates.
Substrate Best for Typical depth Advantages Considerations
Gravel General freshwater community tanks 1–2 in Cheap, easy to vacuum, water flows through it, anchors decorations well Food and waste fall between grains; inert, so rooted plants need root tabs
Sand Corydoras, loaches, cichlids, natural biotopes 1–2 in Safe for barbel-feeding bottom dwellers, natural look, waste stays on top where it is easy to remove Compacts over time, can cloud the water when disturbed, needs gentle cleaning technique
Aquarium soil Planted and aquascaped tanks 2–3 in Holds nutrients for roots, lightweight per litre, often buffers pH downward for soft-water plants and shrimp Most expensive option, breaks down over 1–3 years, can leach ammonia when new so tanks need cycling
Crushed coral African cichlids, brackish and marine tanks, hard-water species 1–2 in Raises carbonate hardness and stabilises pH, durable, does not break down Unsuitable for soft-water fish and plants; coarse pieces trap debris

Mixing is normal. A common approach is a nutrient layer capped with 1 inch of sand or fine gravel — calculate each layer separately and add the volumes together.

How Is Aquarium Substrate Calculated?

Nothing here is hidden. These are the exact formulas the calculator runs.

Flat substrate bed

Substrate Volume = Tank Length × Tank Width × Substrate Depth

Sloped substrate bed

Average Depth = (Front Depth + Back Depth) ÷ 2
Substrate Volume = Tank Length × Tank Width × Average Depth

Estimated weight

Estimated Weight = Substrate Volume × Bulk Density

Worked example

A 20 gallon long aquarium with a flat gravel bed:

  • Tank footprint: 30 × 12 inches
  • Substrate depth: 2 inches
  • Volume: 30 × 12 × 2 = 720 cubic inches
  • Converted: 720 × 16.387 cm³ = 11,799 cm³ = 11.8 litres (3.12 US gallons)
  • Weight at a gravel bulk density of 1.60 g/cm³: 11.8 × 1.60 = 18.9 kg ≈ 41.6 lb
  • With a 10% buffer: about 46 lb (20.8 kg) to buy

Change only the substrate and the weight changes with it. The same 11.8 litre bed is roughly 18.3 kg (40.3 lb) in sand, about 15.9 kg (35.1 lb) in crushed coral, and only about 8.8 kg (19.5 lb) in baked aquarium soil. Volume is the constant; weight follows the material.

Methodology and accuracy

Substrate volume is calculated from the aquarium footprint and desired bed depth. Weight estimates use approximate bulk density and may vary by product, grain size, moisture content, and packing.

The volume half of this calculation is pure geometry and is exact for the dimensions you enter — length × width × depth, with unit conversions carried out at full precision internally and rounded only for display (1 in = 2.54 cm, 1 L = 1,000 cm³ = 0.264172 US gal, 1 kg = 2.20462 lb). The weight half is an estimate, because bulk density is a property of the specific product in the bag rather than of "gravel" or "sand" as categories. The densities listed above are typical dry values for common aquarium substrates; if your bag states both a weight and a volume, entering that figure under Custom density will give you a closer answer.

The depth ranges and substrate comparisons on this page are practical recommendations drawn from common freshwater aquarium practice, not measured results. They are starting points to adapt to your own plants, livestock and aquascape.
