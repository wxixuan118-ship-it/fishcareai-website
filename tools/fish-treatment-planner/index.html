Rules That Apply to Every Treatment

  • Fix the water first. Test ammonia and nitrite before the first dose. Medicine works badly in poor water, and many signs of disease are really water problems. The fish symptom checker helps you rule that out.
  • Take carbon out of the filter. Activated carbon removes many medications from the water. Put fresh carbon back after the course.
  • Add aeration. Several medications and higher temperatures lower the oxygen in the water.
  • Finish the full course. Stopping when fish look better is the most common reason ich and bacterial infections come back.
  • Use one medication at a time. Mixing products can be toxic. If you need to switch, change most of the water and run carbon for a day first, unless the labels say otherwise.
  • Watch invertebrates and sensitive fish. Shrimp and snails should come out before most ich and parasite treatments, and scaleless fish are often given half doses. Check the label.

Main Tank or Hospital Tank?

Treat the main tank for parasites that live in the whole tank, such as ich and velvet: the free-swimming stage is in the water, so every fish needs treating. Use a hospital tank for antibiotics and for a single sick fish. Antibiotics in the main tank can kill the filter bacteria that keep ammonia at 0, and a bare hospital tank makes it easier to see the fish and keep the water clean. In a hospital tank without an established filter, test ammonia every day.

FAQ

How long should I treat my fish for?

Follow the course length on your medication's label and finish it even if the fish look better. As a guide, ich takes 7–14 days of daily treatment in a tropical tank, velvet 10–14 days, and most antibacterial courses 5–10 days.

Should I do a water change between doses?

Many labels ask for a partial water change before each new dose, and it helps keep the water clean while fish are stressed. Check your product's label: some say to dose again without a change.

Can I treat fish in the main tank?

For ich and velvet, yes, because the parasite is in the whole tank. Antibiotics are better used in a hospital tank, since they can harm the filter bacteria in the main tank.

Why doesn't this planner tell me how much medicine to add?

Because the right amount depends on the exact product, its strength and your real water volume. The label on your medication is the only reliable source for the dose. This planner handles the timing so you don't miss a dose or a recheck.

What should I do after the treatment ends?

Change 30–50% of the water, put fresh carbon back in the filter and watch the fish for two weeks. Weakened fish can pick up a second infection, and ich can return if a few parasites survived.
