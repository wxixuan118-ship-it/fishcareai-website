How Much Salt Does a Koi Pond Need?

The arithmetic is simple once the volume is right. A litre of water weighs a kilogram, so a 0.1 percent solution is 1 gram of salt per litre — or, in the units most pond keepers use, about 0.83 lb of salt per 100 US gallons for every 0.1 percent. The familiar rule of thumb, 2.5 lb per 100 gallons to reach 0.3 percent, falls straight out of that.

Salt required
litres × (target % − current %) ÷ 100 = kilograms of salt
US gallons × (target % − current %) × 0.0835 = pounds of salt

Two details cause almost every dosing error.

The volume figure. Doses are proportional to volume, so a 25 percent error in your gallon estimate is a 25 percent error in the dose. Include the filter chambers and any bog or settlement stage in the loop — they are full of the same water. If you have never measured the system properly, the koi pond calculator is the place to start.

The salt already in there. Salt does not evaporate, degrade or get consumed. Topping the pond up after a hot week adds water and dilutes the salt; evaporation alone concentrates it. The only reliable way to know your current level is to measure it, and adding a full dose on top of an unmeasured existing level is how ponds end up well past 0.5 percent by accident.

What salt does

Salt works in two useful ways. At around 0.1 percent, chloride ions compete with nitrite at the gill, which reduces nitrite uptake while a filter problem is being fixed — genuinely valuable during a cycling crash. At 0.3 percent and above it disrupts the osmotic balance of several common external protozoan parasites, and it also reduces the osmotic work a stressed or damaged fish has to do to hold its internal salt balance, which is why it is often used as supportive care after handling or transport.

What salt does not do

Salt is not a general medicine. It does not treat bacterial infections, it is unreliable against flukes at pond-safe concentrations, it does nothing for ammonia, and it will not fix an overstocked or under-filtered pond. Using it as a routine additive also means you have no headroom left when you genuinely need it, and it gradually rules out most pond plants. Treat it as a specific tool for specific situations.

How to Add Pond Salt Safely

  • Use the right salt. Pure sodium chloride with no iodine, no anti-caking agents and no additives. Pond salt, solar salt and plain water-softener salt are all commonly used; table salt and rock salt for de-icing are not.
  • Dissolve it first. Mix each dose in a bucket of pond water until fully dissolved, then pour it into a well-circulated area such as the return flow. Dry crystals sinking onto a resting fish can burn it.
  • Split the dose. Adding the full amount over two or three days lets the fish adjust osmotically instead of being moved in one step.
  • Aerate. Salt slightly reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of water, and a fish being treated is already stressed. Run extra aeration throughout the treatment.
  • Watch the fish. Gasping, flashing, sudden loss of balance or fish hanging at the surface means stop adding and increase aeration.
  • Record what you added. Write down the date, the volume dosed and the concentration. The next dose depends on it.

Measuring and removing salt

Measure with a salinity meter or a dedicated pond salt test kit. Guessing from what you added is unreliable once you have topped up, changed water or had rain.

The only way to remove salt is to remove water. Evaporation leaves the salt behind and raises the concentration; rain and top-ups dilute it. To bring a pond down from 0.3 percent, do partial water changes and re-measure — a 25 percent water change with fresh water reduces the salt level by roughly 25 percent. Do not re-dose to compensate for evaporation, only for the water you actually removed.

When Not to Use Pond Salt

  • With pond plants you want to keep. Most aquatic plants suffer above about 0.1 percent, and many die at 0.3.
  • With salt-sensitive fish. Sturgeon in particular tolerate salt poorly compared with koi and goldfish. If a pond contains sturgeon, look for another approach.
  • Alongside medications without checking. Some pond treatments interact with salt, and combinations that each seem mild can be harmful together. Read the label of anything you are already dosing.
  • As a permanent additive. Holding a pond at treatment strength year-round removes the option of using salt when something is actually wrong.
  • Instead of fixing water quality. If ammonia or nitrite is high, the problem is the filter or the stocking level. Salt at 0.1 percent buys time against nitrite; it does not solve the cause.

Methodology and accuracy

Salt requirement is calculated as litres of water × (target percentage − current percentage) ÷ 100 = kilograms of sodium chloride, which follows directly from the density of fresh water at 1 kg per litre. Pounds are converted at 1 kg = 2.20462 lb, and volumes at 1 US gallon = 3.785411784 litres and 1 imperial gallon = 4.54609 litres. The arithmetic is exact for the volume you enter.

Percentage by weight is used throughout, which is the convention in pond keeping: 0.1% is equivalent to 1 part per thousand, or roughly 1,000 ppm. Salinity meters may read in ppt, percent or specific gravity, so check which scale yours uses before comparing readings against a target.

The concentrations and their typical uses described on this page reflect general pond-keeping practice rather than a clinical protocol. Tolerance varies between species, individual fish, water temperature and water chemistry, and published guidance from koi clubs and aquatic vets differs on the details.

Doses assume pure sodium chloride. Salt sold with anti-caking agents or as a blended product will not deliver the same concentration by weight, and additives can be harmful in their own right.

This is a dosing calculator, not veterinary advice, and it cannot diagnose a health problem. If koi are visibly unwell, losing condition or dying, consult an aquatic vet or an experienced koi health specialist.
