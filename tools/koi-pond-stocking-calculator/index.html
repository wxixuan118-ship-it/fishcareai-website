How Many Koi Per Gallon?

The figure most koi keepers work to is 250 US gallons per mature koi. On a pond with a bottom drain, a properly sized biological filter and dedicated aeration, that can come down to roughly 150 to 190 gallons per fish. On a pond running a basic box or pressure filter with no air pump, it should go up to 300 gallons or more.

Those numbers look generous next to aquarium stocking rules, and they need to be. A mature koi is a 2 to 4 pound animal that eats heavily in summer and produces waste in proportion. Ponds also run without the safety net an indoor tank has: they heat up in a July afternoon, hold less oxygen when they do, and get no help from a thermostat.

Stocking density is really a statement about three limits at once:

  • Biological load. Ammonia and nitrite have to be processed as fast as the fish produce them. This is the limit filtration can move.
  • Dissolved oxygen. Warm water holds less oxygen and heavily stocked ponds consume more, which is why summer nights are when overstocked ponds fail. Aeration moves this limit; a bigger filter does not.
  • Physical space. Koi are strong horizontal swimmers, and crowding shows up as stunted growth, poor body shape and more frequent skin and gill problems. Nothing but volume moves this limit.

Filtration and aeration buy you room on the first two. That is why this calculator adjusts for them, and why the adjustment is bounded — no filter turns a 1,000 gallon pond into a 2,000 gallon one.

The inches-of-fish rule

The other common rule is 10 US gallons per inch of koi. It is easier to apply to a mixed group, but it is more permissive than the per-fish rule at large sizes: 10 gallons per inch puts five 20 inch koi in 1,000 gallons, while 250 gallons per fish puts four. The calculator shows both so you can see the gap, and the sensible move is to plan to the lower of the two.

How Many Koi in a 1000 Gallon Pond?

A 1,000 US gallon pond holds four mature koi at the standard 250 gallons per fish. With a bottom drain, a large biological filter and a dedicated air pump, five is workable. On a basic box filter with no aeration, two to three is the honest number.

The juvenile column is the trap. Sixteen 5 inch koi will look perfectly comfortable in a 1,000 gallon pond for a season or two, and every one of them is heading for 18 inches. Buying to the juvenile figure is the single most common route to an overstocked pond, because the crowding arrives gradually, two or three years after the decision that caused it.

A 1,000 gallon pond is also right at the koi minimum in the first place. It works, but the margin is thin: water quality moves quickly in a small volume, temperature swings further, and there is no headroom to add a fish you fall for later. If you are still at the planning stage, size the pond at 2,500 gallons or more and the stocking question gets much easier.

Signs Your Koi Pond Is Overstocked

Overstocking rarely announces itself with a single dead fish. It shows up as a pond that is harder to keep stable than it should be.

  • Fish at the surface at dawn. Gasping or hanging near the waterfall in the early morning is the classic low-oxygen signature, because dissolved oxygen bottoms out just before sunrise.
  • Ammonia or nitrite that will not stay at zero. A mature filter on a correctly stocked pond reads zero for both. Persistent traces mean the biological load has outgrown the filter.
  • Nitrate climbing between water changes. The end product of the nitrogen cycle accumulates faster the more fish you carry.
  • Green water or string algae you cannot beat. More fish means more dissolved nutrients feeding algae, regardless of UV.
  • Stunted or poorly shaped fish. Crowded koi grow more slowly and put on less body volume than the same bloodline in a larger pond.
  • Recurring skin, fin and gill problems. Chronic crowding suppresses the immune response and keeps parasite loads circulating.

If you are seeing these, test first. The water parameter checker will tell you whether ammonia, nitrite, nitrate or pH is the immediate problem, but if the readings keep drifting back after every water change, the fix is fewer fish or more water rather than more chemistry.

Other fish count too

Goldfish, orfe, sturgeon and grass carp all add to the biological load. Count a goldfish as roughly a third of a koi, an orfe as about half, and note that sturgeon have their own demands — they need a large, oxygen-rich pond, a smooth bottom, and sinking food they can actually find, and they are a poor fit for a pond that is already at its koi limit.

Methodology and accuracy

Stocking figures are derived from gallons-per-fish rules of thumb widely used in koi keeping: 60 US gallons for juveniles up to 6 inches, 130 for growing fish of 7 to 12 inches, 250 for adults of 13 to 18 inches, and 400 for jumbo koi of 19 inches and over. These are planning conventions rather than measured carrying capacities, and different koi clubs and dealers publish figures that vary either side of them.

The filtration adjustment multiplies the gallons-per-fish requirement by 1.4 for basic filtration, 1.0 for a good sized biological filter, and 0.75 for an advanced setup with a bottom drain, mechanical pre-filtration, a large biofilter and dedicated aeration. The multiplier is deliberately bounded: filtration changes the biological and oxygen limits, not the amount of swimming space.

The inches-of-fish cross-check uses 10 US gallons per inch of koi, taking the midpoint of each size band. It is shown alongside the main result because the two rules disagree at larger sizes, and the lower figure is the safer plan.

Volume conversions are exact: 1 US gallon = 3.785411784 litres, 1 imperial gallon = 4.54609 litres. The depth check flags ponds under 3 feet, which is the depth generally treated as the minimum for koi rather than a value used in the stocking arithmetic.

This calculator estimates capacity. It is not a substitute for testing your own water — a pond can be within these figures and still show ammonia or nitrite if the filter is immature, damaged or undersized.
