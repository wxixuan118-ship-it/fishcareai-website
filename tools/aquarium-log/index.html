What to Record in an Aquarium Log

Five readings tell you most of what is happening in a freshwater tank. The ranges below are general targets for tropical community fish; check the water parameter checker for your species.

ReadingTargetAct whenWhat it tells you
Ammonia0 ppmAbove 0.25 ppmWaste the filter is not processing: an uncycled tank, overstocking, overfeeding or a dead fish.
Nitrite0 ppmAbove 0.25 ppmThe second stage of the nitrogen cycle is not keeping up. Common in new tanks and after a filter is cleaned too hard.
NitrateUnder 20 ppm40 ppm or moreHow fast waste builds up between water changes. A steady climb means changes are too small or too far apart.
pHStable, usually 6.5–7.8A shift of 0.5 or moreSwings matter more than the exact number. A slow fall often means carbonate hardness (KH) is running out.
TemperatureStable, usually 75–80°F (24–27°C) for tropical fishA change of 4°F (2°C) or moreHeater failure, a hot room or a cold night. Sudden swings stress fish and can trigger ich.

Record water changes and notes too. A nitrate reading only makes sense next to the date and size of the last water change, and a note such as “added 6 tetras” explains an ammonia bump a few days later.

How Often to Test Aquarium Water

  • Cycling a new tank: every day or every other day, until ammonia and nitrite both read 0 and nitrate is rising.
  • First weeks after adding fish: every 2 to 3 days. The filter needs time to catch up with the extra waste.
  • During a disease treatment such as ich or fin rot: every day or two. Some medications affect filter bacteria, and sick fish handle poor water badly.
  • Established, stable tank: once a week, before the water change.

If you are not sure how many fish your tank can support, the aquarium stocking calculator suggests a water-change schedule for your stocking level.

How to Read the Trends in Your Log

Nitrate climbs every week

The water changes are not removing as much as the fish produce. Change more water each time, change it more often, feed less or reduce the stocking.

Ammonia appears a few days after new fish

A mini-cycle. Stop adding fish, feed lightly, and change 25 to 50 percent of the water when ammonia reads above 0.25 ppm until it settles back to 0.

pH falls slowly over weeks

Usually a sign that the buffering capacity (KH) is being used up. Regular water changes restore it; test KH if the drop keeps happening.

Readings are fine but fish look unwell

Look at temperature swings and your notes for recent changes, then check the symptoms in the fish health guide.

Aquarium Log FAQ

What should I record in an aquarium log?

At minimum: the date, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature, plus how much water you changed. Add a short note when something changes, such as new fish, a new filter, a medication or a fish that stopped eating.

How often should I test my aquarium water?

Test every day or two while a new tank is cycling, for the first few weeks after adding fish, and during any treatment. An established tank with stable readings can be tested once a week.

Is my aquarium log data saved?

Yes, in this browser only. Readings are stored on your device and are not uploaded. Clearing site data or switching browsers removes them, so export the CSV if you want a copy.

Why does this log keep only 7 days?

Seven days covers the decisions you make this week: whether ammonia is rising after new fish or whether nitrate is climbing between water changes. Full history, several tanks and reminders are in the FishCare AI app.

What nitrate level should trigger a water change?

Many keepers aim to keep nitrate under 20 ppm and treat 40 ppm as the point to change more water or change it more often. Sensitive species and shrimp do better at the low end.

How This Aquarium Log Works

Readings are stored in your browser with localStorage and never leave your device, so FishCare AI cannot see or recover them. The log keeps the last 7 days and drops older readings when you save.
