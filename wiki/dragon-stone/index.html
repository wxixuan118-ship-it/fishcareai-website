Are Dragon Stones Good for Aquariums?

Dragon stone (Ohko stone) is one of the few aquascaping rocks that is genuinely inert. It will not move your pH, GH or KH — but it is soft, dusty and full of detritus traps, so it rewards careful preparation.

Are Dragon Stones Good for Aquariums?

Yes — dragon stone is one of the safest hardscape rocks you can put in a freshwater aquarium. It is chemically inert, meaning it does not release carbonates into the water and does not raise pH, carbonate hardness (KH) or general hardness (GH). That makes it suitable for soft-water tanks, planted tanks, shrimp tanks and most community setups without any water-chemistry planning.

The reasons to hesitate are physical, not chemical. Dragon stone is hardened clay: it is soft enough to chip with a fingernail, it arrives packed with clay dust that clouds a tank if it is not rinsed out, and its signature pits and caverns collect fish waste and uneaten food.

Short version: good rock, high prep. Dry-brush and rinse it, soak it for a day or two, build the layout on the bare glass rather than on loose substrate, and siphon the crevices during water changes.
Confirm what you actually bought. Retailers sometimes mislabel grey, vein-streaked seiryu stone as dragon stone. Dragon stone is red-brown and pitted; seiryu is grey with white calcite veins and it does raise pH and KH.

What Is Dragon Stone (Ohko Stone)?

Dragon stone, sold as Ohko stone, is a sedimentary clay rock. Weathering has eroded softer material out of the surface, leaving the honeycomb of holes, ledges and hollows that gives the rock its name and its sense of scale in a small tank.

Visually it reads as eroded riverbank or cliff face, which is why it dominates nature-style and jungle-style aquascapes. The colour ranges from tan through rust to dark brown, and it darkens when wet. Individual pieces vary enormously, so buying by weight from a photo is unreliable — you want a few large character pieces plus smaller filler, not a box of similar mid-sized lumps.

Does Dragon Stone Change pH, GH or KH?

No. Dragon stone contains no meaningful calcium carbonate, so it does not buffer water upward. Aquarists who have measured it in bare test containers report no measurable movement in pH, GH or KH over extended periods, which is the behaviour you want from a rock in a soft-water or CO₂-injected tank.

The vinegar test

If you are not certain what a rock is, put a few drops of white vinegar on a dry surface. Carbonate-bearing rock (seiryu, limestone, most “texas holey rock”) fizzes visibly. Dragon stone does not react. Do the test on a dry piece before it goes anywhere near the aquarium.

What inert does not mean: inert is about chemistry, not cleanliness. An unrinsed piece of dragon stone will still cloud your water with clay for days. Inert also does not mean sterile — rock collected outdoors still needs cleaning and inspection.

Dragon Stone vs Seiryu Stone

These are the two most popular aquascaping rocks and they behave completely differently in water. The choice is usually decided by your livestock, not your layout.

A common failure looks like this: a soft-water Caridina setup is built on seiryu stone, and within a few weeks pH has climbed from the low 6s into the mid 7s and the shrimp are stressed. Dragon stone removes that entire class of problem. See the seiryu stone profile if you are weighing the two.

Planted and CO₂ tanksNo carbonate release means KH stays where you set it, so CO₂-driven pH swings stay predictable.

Poor fit: hard-water tanksAfrican rift-lake cichlids and marine tanks want carbonate-releasing rock. Dragon stone contributes nothing there.

The Real Downsides

It is soft and it chips

Dragon stone snaps and crumbles far more easily than seiryu or lava rock. Thin ledges break under the weight of a piece stacked on top, and edges chip when two pieces knock together in a bucket. Handle it dry and move it one piece at a time.

It arrives full of clay

The pits hold packed clay and dust. Putting an unrinsed piece straight into a filled tank produces a brown haze that can take days to clear and clogs filter media in the meantime.

The pits trap waste

The same hollows that make the rock look good collect uneaten food, plant debris and mulm. Left alone in a heavily stocked tank this becomes a slow nitrate source. It is a maintenance task, not a defect — but it is a recurring one.

It is bought by weight

Dragon stone is dense and typically priced per pound or kilo, so a large scape is expensive. Buy fewer, better-shaped pieces rather than filling the footprint with rock.

How to Prepare Dragon Stone

Brush it dry. Work a stiff brush into every pit and hollow over a bin. Most of the loose clay comes out dry, and this is far faster than trying to rinse it out later.
Rinse under running water. Direct the flow into the crevices and keep going until the runoff is clear. A turkey baster or a length of airline is useful for blasting out deep pockets.
Soak for 24 to 48 hours. Leave the pieces in a bucket of water. Remaining fines settle out, and you can confirm the stone has stopped shedding before it goes in the tank.
Skip the boil. Boiling is unnecessary for inert rock and thermal shock cracks clay stone. If you want to sanitise a used or collected piece, a dilute bleach soak followed by a thorough rinse and a dechlorinator soak is safer than heat.
Test if you are unsure. Vinegar on a dry surface should produce no fizzing. If it fizzes, it is not dragon stone.

Building a Stable Layout

Rock stability matters more than rock choice. A collapse can crack the glass, crush a fish or bury a filter intake.

  • Place stone before substrate. Set the large pieces on the bare glass — or on a foam mat — then fill substrate around them. Rock resting on loose soil shifts as the substrate settles.
  • Build wide, not tall. Dragon stone stacks poorly because the contact points are small and the rock is soft. Broad, low arrangements are stable; tall towers are not.
  • Bond, do not balance. Cyanoacrylate gel or aquarium-safe cement holds pieces together far more reliably than gravity.
  • Leave open water. Fill roughly a third of the footprint, not all of it. Fish need swimming room and you need siphon access.
  • Check the edges. Freshly broken dragon stone can be sharp. Blunt any exposed edge near a spot where fish will dart, especially in tanks with long-finned fish or bottom dwellers with barbels.

Long-Term Maintenance

Dragon stone needs no chemical monitoring of its own, but it does change your cleaning routine. During water changes, run the siphon over the pitted faces and pulse a turkey baster into the deep hollows to lift trapped mulm into the water column where the filter or siphon can take it.

Over months the rock softens visually as biofilm, algae and moss colonise it — this is normal and is what makes an established dragon stone scape look better than a new one. Track your parameters as usual with the water parameter checker, and see the aquarium water parameters guide for target ranges. If nitrate creeps upward in a rock-heavy tank, trapped detritus is a likely contributor.

Dragon Stone FAQ

Are dragon stones good for aquariums?Yes. Dragon stone is chemically inert, so it does not raise pH, GH or KH, and it is safe for fish, shrimp and plants once the clay dust has been rinsed out. It suits soft-water and planted tanks especially well. The trade-offs are practical rather than chemical: the rock is soft and chips easily, and its pits trap detritus that needs siphoning.
Does dragon stone raise pH?No. Dragon stone is a hardened clay sedimentary rock with no meaningful carbonate content, so it does not raise pH, carbonate hardness or general hardness. This is the main difference from seiryu stone, which contains calcium carbonate and does push pH and KH upward over time.
Do you need to boil or cure dragon stone?Boiling is not necessary and can crack the rock. Dry-brush the pits to knock out loose clay, then rinse under running water until it runs clear. A 24 to 48 hour soak in a bucket lets the remaining fines settle and lets you confirm the stone is not shedding.
Is dragon stone safe for shrimp?Yes, and it is usually the better choice for Caridina shrimp because it will not lift pH or hardness out of their range. The pitted surface also gives shrimplets grazing surface and cover. Rinse thoroughly first, since fine clay can irritate a tank of small shrimp.
Dragon stone or seiryu stone — which should I use?Use dragon stone for soft-water, planted and shrimp tanks where stable low hardness matters. Use seiryu stone only when a rise in pH and KH is acceptable or wanted, such as tanks with hard-water livebearers. In an unbuffered soft-water tank, seiryu can move pH by a full point or more within weeks.
Does dragon stone break down in water?It does not dissolve, but it is soft. Edges chip when pieces are knocked together and thin ledges snap under weight. Handle it dry, build from the bottom up, and rest large pieces on the bare glass or a foam mat rather than on loose substrate.

Editorial Method and Safety Notes

This profile separates chemical suitability (does the rock alter water chemistry?) from practical suitability (how much work is it?), because those two questions have different answers for dragon stone. Retailer product copy was not treated as independent evidence for water-chemistry claims.

Verify any unfamiliar rock with a vinegar test before it enters a stocked tank, and check stacked hardscape for stability before adding livestock. If pH or hardness moves unexpectedly after a scape change, test the water first rather than dosing.
