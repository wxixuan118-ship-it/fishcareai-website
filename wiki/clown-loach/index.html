Clown Loach Care Guide: Adult Size, Tank Setup & Groups

Chromobotia macracanthus — Bleeker, 1852
🏠 Origin: Borneo & Sumatra, Indonesia
📏 Size: Up to 10–12 in (25–30 cm)
⏳ Lifespan: 10–20+ years
⭐ Care level: Intermediate

The Clown Loach is one of the most recognizable freshwater fish in the hobby — a bold, orange-and-black striped bottom dweller with a playful, social personality. It's also one of the most commonly mis-sold "beginner" fish: the 2–3 inch juveniles at the pet store grow into 10–12 inch adults that need a genuinely large, long-term aquarium and a proper group of their own kind.

Before you buy: Clown loaches can live 10–20+ years and require a minimum of 125 gallons for an adult group of 5–6. Plan for the adult, not the juvenile you see in the store.

Taxonomy & Classification

First described in 1852 as Cobitis macracanthus, this species was moved through the genus Botia before being placed in its own genus, Chromobotia, in 2004. It belongs to the family Botiidae. Borneo and Sumatran populations differ subtly in fin coloration and banding and may eventually be split into separate species.

Natural Habitat

Clown loaches are native to the river systems of Borneo and Sumatra, where they inhabit slow-moving, tannin-stained rivers and streams with soft, slightly acidic water. During the rainy season, they migrate into flooded forest areas to spawn, a behavior that's part of why captive breeding is so difficult to replicate.

Tank Setup & Adult Size

Juveniles (2–4 in) can be housed temporarily in a 75-gallon tank, but adults need 125 gallons minimum, with 150–200+ gallons ideal for a proper group of 5–6. Tank footprint matters as much as volume — a long, low tank gives more usable floor space than a tall one. Use soft sand or fine gravel (clown loaches dig and forage constantly), provide caves, driftwood, and dense planting for security, and invest in strong filtration rated well above the tank's nominal size, since adults produce significant bioload.

Diet & Feeding

Omnivores with a big appetite: offer sinking pellets or wafers as a staple, plus frozen bloodworms, brine shrimp, and shrimp pellets (a particular favorite). Clown loaches will readily eat snails, but shouldn't be purchased purely as a snail-control fish — they'll clear an infestation in weeks, then need years more of dedicated care.

Tank Mates

Peaceful and social, clown loaches do best with other larger, robust community fish that can handle warm water: larger tetras, robust barbs, peaceful gouramis, bristlenose plecos, and other loaches. Always keep in a group of at least 5 — a lone or paired clown loach is shy and stressed, while a proper group displays confident "pack" behavior including resting piled together and harmless play-fighting.

Breeding

Breeding clown loaches in a home aquarium is exceptionally rare and essentially unheard of outside of specialized commercial farms in Southeast Asia, which use hormone induction to replicate the seasonal flood-migration triggers wild fish rely on to spawn. Virtually all clown loaches in the trade are farm-raised from induced spawns, not home-bred.

Common Health Issues

  • Ich — clown loaches are notoriously the first fish in a tank to show white spot disease; keep ich medication on hand and treat promptly at the first sign
  • Skinny disease (nematode infection) — rapid weight loss despite normal eating; treat with an antiparasitic such as levamisole
  • Stress from undersized tanks — stunted growth, lethargy, and disease susceptibility in fish kept long-term in tanks smaller than their adult needs
Normal, not sick: Clown loaches commonly rest lying on their sides on the substrate — alarming to new owners, but normal behavior, not a sign of illness.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big do clown loaches actually get?

Most captive adults reach 8–10 inches, with some individuals reaching 10–12 inches or more in very large, long-established tanks.

Can I keep a clown loach in a 20-gallon tank?

Only temporarily as a small juvenile. Plan for a 125+ gallon upgrade as it grows — this is not a permanent small-tank fish.

Why is my clown loach lying on its side?

This is normal resting behavior for the species, not illness, as long as the fish is otherwise eating and behaving normally.
