10 Gallon Fish Tank: Dimensions, Weight, Stocking & Best Fish

A standard 10 gallon fish tank measures about 20 × 10 × 12 inches (51 × 25 × 30 cm), weighs roughly 12 lb (5.5 kg) empty and about 120 lb (54 kg) once filled with water, gravel and décor, and comfortably holds about 8 nano fish — or one betta with a small supporting crew. It holds about 38 liters, and unlike most sizes it really does have one standard shape: almost every rimmed 10 gallon sold in North America, including the starter kits, uses the same 20 × 10 inch footprint.

That makes it the most bought aquarium in the hobby and one of the most misunderstood. Ten gallons is a fine home for a betta, a school of ember tetras or a cherry-shrimp colony, and a poor one for goldfish, mollies or anything that grows past about 3 inches. It is also less forgiving than a bigger tank: temperature, ammonia and pH all move faster in 38 liters than in 200, so the stocking and maintenance below lean cautious on purpose.

Why 20 inches decides what lives in a 10 gallon tank

The gallon figure tells you how much waste the water can dilute; the 20-inch length tells you which fish can actually swim in it. A fish that darts the length of the tank in a second — a zebra danio, a giant danio, a group of mollies — runs out of room in 20 inches long before it runs out of water. Fish that hover, pick at plants or stay in a tight school — ember tetras, chili rasboras, pygmy corydoras, a betta — barely notice the length. That is why this page recommends small, slow-moving species rather than simply “anything under 2 inches”.

The 10-inch depth matters too. It is enough for a simple slope of substrate and a few plants, not for a layered aquascape. If planted nano tanks are your goal, the rimless 45 × 27 cm and 45 × 30 cm tanks trade 2 inches of length for 1–2 inches of extra depth, which makes a noticeable difference to the layout. For more length in a similar footprint, the next step is a 20 gallon long (30 × 12 × 12 in).

How Much Does a 10 Gallon Fish Tank Weigh?

Freshwater weighs about 8.34 lb per US gallon (3.78 kg per gallon), so a full 10 gallons of water would be 83 lb. A running tank holds a little less water than its label once gravel and décor are in, but adds the weight of the glass, the substrate and the rock — and the total is what the furniture has to hold.

Call it about 120 lb — roughly the weight of an adult on a 20 × 10 inch patch of furniture. Gravel is the variable people underestimate: it can add a third to the weight of the water. A bare-bottom quarantine tank with a sponge filter comes in close to 90 lb; a planted tank with a deep bank of gravel and a few large stones can pass 130 lb.

Placement safety: any solid desk, dresser or purpose-built stand will carry a 10 gallon tank, but it must be level and the whole 20 × 10 inch base must be supported — no overhanging edges. Avoid glass-topped tables, wall shelves and flat-pack furniture with a hollow top panel, which can sag under 120 lb over months. Keep it out of direct sunlight and away from heaters and air vents; in a tank this small, a sunny window can push the temperature up several degrees in an afternoon.

How Many Fish Can Live in a 10 Gallon Tank?

A standard 10 gallon tank supports about 8 nano fish — one school of a species that stays around an inch long — or one betta with 5–6 nano tank mates, or a colony of shrimp with a few small fish. That assumes a filter rated for the tank and weekly water changes. The tank does not hold goldfish, mollies, angelfish or anything that grows past about 3 inches, however small it is in the shop.

The old “one inch of fish per gallon” rule gets slim nano fish roughly right and everything else wrong, because waste output rises much faster than length: a 3-inch fish has around twenty times the body mass of a 1-inch fish, not three times. In a tank this small, there is also no margin for the mistake — overstocking a 10 gallon shows up as ammonia within days, not months.

What actually sets the limit in 10 gallons

  • Adult size. Buy for the fish it becomes. Common plecos, bristlenose plecos, mollies and fancy goldfish are all sold at 1–2 inches and all outgrow a 10.
  • Swimming length. With 20 inches of run, fast schooling fish pace the glass. Pick species that hover or school tightly.
  • One centerpiece only. A betta, a honey gourami or a dwarf group of livebearers — not two of these. Small tanks leave no room to escape a territorial fish.
  • Schooling minimums. Six is the floor for tetras, rasboras and corydoras. If six of a species will not fit, the species does not fit.
  • Bioload and feeding. In 9 gallons of actual water, a single uneaten pinch of food matters. Feed small amounts once or twice a day.
  • Breeding. Mixed-sex guppies, endlers or platies will overstock a 10 gallon with fry within a few months. Keep males only.

Practical examples for a standard 10 gallon

These combinations were scored with our fish tank stocking calculator set to 10 gallons, using adult size and bioload with a correctly rated filter:

  • Works well (≈ 80%): 8 neon tetras, with room for shrimp or a nerite snail. Neons are borderline in 20 inches — a group of chili rasboras or ember tetras is a calmer fit.
  • Works with a placid betta (≈ 66%): 1 betta + 6 chili rasboras. Add the rasboras first and the betta last.

10 Gallon Fish Tank Stocking Ideas

Six stocking plans for a standard 20 × 10 × 12 inch 10 gallon fish tank, plus one non-display use. Each one fills the tank to a sensible level — not the maximum — because small tanks punish overstocking quickly. None repeats the plans on our 50, 55 or 100 gallon pages.

Betta and Floor Crew

One betta at the surface, a tiny cory group below
Centerpiece1 male betta
Bottom6 pygmy corydoras
Clean-up1–2 nerite snails
HardscapeFine sand, a few broad leaves near the surface for resting

Why it works: The betta owns the upper water and the pygmy corys stay low, so they rarely meet. Both are fine at 76–80°F with gentle flow, and the snails handle soft algae without adding much bioload. The most reliable way to give a betta tank mates in 10 gallons.

Watch for: Some bettas attack anything that moves. Have a backup plan — a spare tank or a divider — and add the betta last, after the corys have settled.

Planted Nano School

Two tiny species, one green tank
Schooling8 ember tetras
Bottom6 pygmy corydoras
InvertebratesCherry shrimp optional
HardscapeSand, java fern and anubias on wood, a carpet of moss

Why it works: Both species stay under an inch and occupy different levels, so the tank looks full and calm. The stocking calculator puts this mix at about 96% — full, but stable with weekly water changes. Low-light plants keep the setup simple.

Watch for: This is the ceiling for a 10. Do not add a centerpiece fish on top; swap the corys for one honey gourami if you want one.

Shrimp Colony

The lowest-maintenance 10 gallon you can build

Why it works: Cherry shrimp breed freely in a planted 10 and produce almost no waste, so the tank stays clean with modest maintenance. Chili rasboras are small enough to leave adult shrimp alone and add movement in the water column.

Fish That Are Not Suitable for a 10 Gallon Tank

Most fish that fail in a 10 gallon tank fail for one of two reasons: they grow too big, or they swim too fast for 20 inches. Many of them are sold in the same shop section as nano fish, at the same size. The alternatives below are general ranges rather than absolute minimums.

✕

Goldfish (fancy and single-tail)

Even fancy goldfish reach 6–8 inches with a heavy waste output; single-tails grow to a foot. A 10 gallon is a temporary tank at best.

20 gallons for the first fancy goldfish, 40 for a single-tail — see the goldfish tank size calculator.

✕

Molly

Common mollies grow to 4–5 inches, breed constantly and produce a lot of waste for their size.

A group of 5 in 20 gallons, 30 more comfortably.

✕

Zebra danio

Small enough to look right, but a school of six sprints end to end all day and runs out of room in 20 inches.

A 20 gallon long for a school of 6.

✕

Angelfish

Sold at coin size, grows to 6 inches long and 8–10 inches tall. A 12-inch-tall tank cannot hold an adult.

29 gallons for a pair, and a taller tank.

✕

Bristlenose and common pleco

A bristlenose reaches 4–5 inches with a large waste output; a common pleco reaches 18–24 inches.

20–30 gallons for a bristlenose; 150+ for a common pleco. Use nerite snails or otocinclus for algae in a 10.

✕

Tiger barb

Needs a group of six or more to keep its nipping spread out, and a group that size is too active and too big for 10 gallons.

A school of 6–8 in 29–30 gallons or more.

✕

Swordtail

Males grow a long sword and swim fast; females reach 4 inches. Groups breed quickly.

A 20 gallon long minimum for 5.

✕

Rainbow shark and bala shark

A rainbow shark is a 6-inch territorial fish that claims the whole floor; bala sharks are schooling fish that reach a foot.

A 4-foot tank for a single rainbow shark; 125+ gallons for a bala group.

Borderline: a single dwarf gourami can live in a 10 but is territorial and prone to disease, so a honey gourami is the safer centerpiece. Neon tetras fit by bioload but are more active than ember tetras and sensitive in an immature tank — stock them only after the tank has run for a couple of months. A single pea puffer can work in a heavily planted species-only 10, but it will eat any shrimp and snails.

How to Set Up a 10 Gallon Fish Tank

Setting up a 10 gallon fish tank follows the standard freshwater sequence — our how to set up a fish tank guide covers the fundamentals. The steps below add what changes when the tank is small: stability matters more than equipment size.

  1. Choose the locationPick solid, level furniture that supports the whole 20 × 10 inch base, near an outlet, away from windows, radiators and air vents. Leave 3–4 inches behind the tank for a hang-on-back filter and cords.
  2. Rinse and place the tankRinse with plain water only — no soap. If the tank came from a kit, check the hood fits flat and the seals have no gaps before adding anything.
  3. Add substrateAbout 20–30 lb of rinsed gravel or sand gives a 1–1.5 inch layer over the 20 × 10 inch floor. Use sand if you plan to keep pygmy corydoras; plant substrate if you want rooted plants.
  4. Install filter and heaterA hang-on-back filter rated for 10–20 gallons or a sponge filter with an air pump. Place a 50 W adjustable heater near the filter outlet so warm water circulates. For a betta or shrimp tank, baffle the outlet with a sponge to slow the flow.
  5. Add décor and plantsLow-light plants such as java fern, anubias, cryptocorynes and mosses suit the stock lighting of most 10 gallon kits. Attach java fern and anubias to rock or wood rather than burying the rhizome. See the plant care guide.
  6. Fill and condition the waterFill slowly over a plate so the substrate stays in place. Dose dechlorinator for about 9 gallons of actual water — see preparing water for a fish tank.
  7. Cycle the tankAdd an ammonia source and wait for the filter bacteria to establish — usually 3–6 weeks. Our how to cycle a fish tank guide and the cycle tracker walk through it day by day.
  8. Confirm water parametersAmmonia and nitrite at zero within 24 hours of dosing, nitrate present: the tank is cycled. Check pH and hardness against the species you want. See aquarium water parameters.
  9. Add fish in stagesStart with half the planned stock — usually the school — and wait two to three weeks before adding the rest. In a 10 gallon, adding everything at once is the most common cause of new tank syndrome.
  10. Monitor the first monthsTest ammonia, nitrite and nitrate twice a week for the first month and weekly after that. Small tanks show problems sooner, which also means you catch them sooner if you test.

Equipment for a 10 Gallon Aquarium

A 10 gallon needs small equipment, but the right sizes are not always what ships in the kit box. These are the targets to check whatever you buy.

🌀Filter

Aim for four to six times the volume per hour — about 40–60 GPH of rated flow — from a hang-on-back filter labelled for 10–20 gallons, or a sponge filter run by a small air pump. Slightly oversizing the filter's media is good; oversizing its flow is not, because bettas, shrimp and nano fish all struggle against a strong current in a 20-inch tank. A pre-filter sponge on the intake protects shrimp and fry. Kit filters are usually adequate in flow but small in media — adding a sponge to the intake doubles the space for bacteria.

🌡️Heater

A 10 gallon in a 70°F room heated to 78°F needs about 25–50 W; a single adjustable 50 W heater covers almost every home. Avoid fixed-temperature “preset” heaters, which can hold a tank several degrees off target, and avoid oversized heaters — a 100 W unit that sticks on can overheat 9 gallons of water within hours. A separate thermometer, checked daily, is essential. The cool-water plan above may need no heater at all.

💡Lighting

Any 18–20 inch LED shows fish colour well. Low-light plants (java fern, anubias, cryptocorynes, mosses) grow under most kit lights. Run the light for 6–8 hours a day on a timer; more than that in a small tank is the most common cause of algae.

🪨Substrate

About 20–30 lb of gravel or sand gives a 1–1.5 inch layer across 20 × 10 inches. Sand suits pygmy corydoras and other bottom-sifters; a nutrient-rich plant substrate suits rooted plants in a planted nano tank. A quarantine tank is best left bare.

🪵Stand

A running 10 gallon weighs about 120 lb. A purpose-built 20 × 10 inch stand is ideal, but any solid, level desk or dresser that supports the whole base will do. Avoid glass tops, hollow-core flat-pack furniture and wall shelves, and check that the surface is level — a tank that leans puts uneven stress on the seams.

Water Parameters for a 10 Gallon Aquarium

A 10 gallon tank has no “correct” water chemistry of its own — temperature, pH and hardness are set by the fish you choose. A betta and pygmy corydoras want 76–80°F; white cloud minnows and cherry shrimp are happier at 64–74°F. Pick species whose ranges overlap with your tap water rather than adjusting the water to fit the fish.

What the small volume changes is speed. A 10 gallon loses heat faster than a 50 if the heater fails, reaches toxic ammonia faster after an overfeeding or a dead fish, and can swing pH overnight if the water has little buffering (KH). That is the main reason beginners struggle with small tanks: the same mistake that a big tank absorbs becomes an emergency in 38 liters. Ammonia and nitrite at zero, and nitrate kept low through water changes, are the only universal targets.

Start from your tap water: test GH, KH and pH with a liquid kit, then pick species to match. Our fish tank water guide and what kind of water for a fish tank explain the options; if a test result worries you, the how to lower nitrates and cloudy aquarium water guides cover the common fixes.

10 Gallon Fish Tank Maintenance

A 10 gallon is quick to maintain — a water change takes ten minutes with a bucket — but it needs that maintenance more regularly than a large tank. Use this framework and let your nitrate readings adjust it.

  • Partial water changes. 25% weekly (about 2–2.5 gallons) is a good starting point. If nitrate rises above about 20–40 ppm between changes, change more or more often. A lightly stocked shrimp tank may hold low nitrate with changes every 10 days. The water change schedule tool works out the right rhythm for your readings.
  • Temperature-match new water. Two gallons of cold tap water can drop a 10 gallon tank by several degrees. Match the temperature by hand or with a thermometer before pouring.
  • Substrate cleaning. Vacuum open areas weekly with a small gravel siphon. Leave planted areas and shrimp-heavy areas mostly undisturbed.
  • Filter maintenance. Rinse sponges in a bucket of old tank water every 2–4 weeks; never under the tap and never all the media at once.
  • Top-ups. Evaporation concentrates minerals quickly in a small tank. Top up with dechlorinated water a couple of times a week.
  • Testing. Ammonia, nitrite and nitrate weekly for the first three months, and whenever fish behave oddly. The water parameter checker explains what each result means; the aquarium log keeps track of readings.

Feeding drives everything above. Small, once- or twice-daily portions that disappear within a minute or two are plenty — the fish feeding calculator sizes portions for the species in your tank.

Is a 10 Gallon Fish Tank Right for You?

The 10 gallon is usually someone's first tank — and often the one that decides whether they stay in the hobby. It rewards careful, modest stocking and punishes the shop's “starter community” of one of everything.

✅ Advantages

  • Fits almost anywhere. A 20 × 10 inch footprint and about 120 lb running weight suit desks, dressers and bedrooms.
  • One standard size. Lids, lights, stands and backgrounds labelled “10 gallon” almost always fit.
  • Cheap to run. A 50 W heater, a small filter and 2–3 gallons of water a week.
  • Ideal for a betta or a shrimp colony. The best-suited species do better here than in smaller tanks.
  • A useful second tank. Quarantine, hospital or grow-out tank once you have a bigger display.

⚠️ Considerations

  • Small margin for error. Temperature, ammonia and pH change faster than in 20 gallons and up.
  • Short stocking list. About eight nano fish, or a betta with a small crew — not a mixed community.
  • 20-inch length. Excludes active schooling fish such as zebra danios and most barbs.
  • Kit equipment varies. Included filters and lights are often minimal; plan to upgrade.
  • Easy to outgrow. Many keepers want a bigger tank within a year — a 20 long costs little more.

Choose a 10 gallon if you want a betta, a nano school, a shrimp colony or a quarantine tank, and you are willing to test and change water every week. Choose a 20 gallon long instead if you want a small community with more than one species of active fish — it is far more forgiving for a first tank. Choose a 5 gallon only if space is very tight and you plan a single betta or a few shrimp.

10 Gallon Fish Tank FAQ

What are the dimensions of a 10 gallon fish tank?

A standard 10 gallon fish tank is about 20 inches long, 10 inches front to back and 12 inches tall (51 × 25 × 30 cm). Almost every rimmed 10 gallon and starter kit uses this footprint; a kit hood adds 2–3 inches of height. Rimless metric tanks of about the same volume measure 45 × 27 × 30 cm or 45 × 30 × 30 cm.

How much does a 10 gallon fish tank weigh?

About 11–14 lb (5–6.5 kg) empty and about 100–130 lb (45–59 kg) filled and running: roughly 12 lb of glass, 75 lb of water (9 actual gallons at 8.34 lb), 25–35 lb of gravel and a few pounds of décor and equipment. A typical setup comes to about 120 lb.

How many fish can I put in a 10 gallon tank?

About 8 nano fish that stay around an inch long, such as ember tetras or chili rasboras, or one betta with 5–6 nano fish, or a cherry shrimp colony with a few small fish. That assumes a filter rated for the tank and weekly water changes. Fish that grow beyond about 3 inches do not belong in a 10 gallon.

How many guppies can live in a 10 gallon tank?

About 5–6 adult guppies, ideally all males. Mixed-sex groups breed every month and will overstock a 10 gallon within a few months. The guppy tank size calculator works out other group sizes.

Can a betta live in a 10 gallon tank?

Yes — a 10 gallon is one of the best sizes for a single male betta. It holds temperature and water quality much better than a 5 gallon, with room for a few calm tank mates such as pygmy corydoras, chili rasboras or nerite snails. Never keep two male bettas together.

What is the best fish for a 10 gallon tank?

A single male betta, a school of 8–10 ember tetras or 10–12 chili rasboras, 6–8 pygmy corydoras, an all-male group of endler's livebearers or guppies, or a honey gourami over a nano school. Cherry shrimp are also an excellent choice.

Can goldfish live in a 10 gallon tank?

No. Even fancy goldfish grow to 6–8 inches and produce a lot of waste; they need about 20 gallons for the first fish and 10 more for each additional one. Single-tail goldfish need 40 gallons or a pond.

What size heater do I need for a 10 gallon tank?

A 25–50 W heater; a single adjustable 50 W model suits almost every room. Avoid oversized heaters — a 100 W unit that sticks on can overheat a 10 gallon quickly. Always use a separate thermometer.

What size filter do I need for a 10 gallon tank?

About 40–60 GPH of rated flow, from a hang-on-back filter rated for 10–20 gallons or a sponge filter with an air pump. For a betta or shrimp tank, choose gentle flow or baffle the outlet.

How often should I change the water in a 10 gallon tank?

Start with 25% (about 2–2.5 gallons) weekly and adjust to your nitrate readings. Match the new water's temperature and dechlorinate it. Lightly stocked shrimp tanks may need changes only every 10 days.

Is a 10 gallon tank good for beginners?

It can be, with modest stocking and weekly maintenance — a betta or a single nano school is a good first tank. But small tanks react quickly to mistakes, so many beginners find a 20 gallon long easier to keep stable.

Compare Other Aquarium Sizes

The 10 gallon sits between the 5 gallon betta tank and the 20 gallon sizes most keepers move on to. Here is how it compares to its neighbours.

5 Gallon Fish Tank
≈ 16 × 8 × 10 in · ≈ 19 L

Half the water and much less stable. Suitable for one betta or a few shrimp; no room for a school. The 10 gallon is the better buy for nearly the same price.

20 Gallon Fish Tank
20 high ≈ 24 × 12 × 16 in · 20 long ≈ 30 × 12 × 12 in

Twice the water and, in the long version, 10 more inches of swimming length. Opens the door to zebra danios, mollies, a betta community and small mixed groups.

29 Gallon Fish Tank
≈ 30 × 12 × 18 in · ≈ 110 L

A tall 30-inch tank that fits an angelfish pair, larger schools and a planted community — the usual upgrade for a keeper who has outgrown a 10.

55 Gallon Fish Tank
≈ 48 × 13 × 21 in · ≈ 208 L

The classic 4-foot community tank, more than five times the water. Much more stable, much heavier — about 600 lb running.

How FishCare AI Makes Tank Recommendations

The species, group sizes and stocking combinations on this page are based on the same factors the Aquarium Planner and Fish Compatibility Checker weigh for every species in our database:

  • Adult size — we plan around the fish an animal becomes, not the juvenile sold in stores.
  • Swimming behaviour — cruising, hovering and darting species need different lengths and depths.
  • Territorial requirements — cichlids and some catfish claim floor space that has to exist in the footprint.
  • Schooling needs — shoaling species are counted as groups, never as individuals.
  • Bioload — body mass, diet and messiness drive the filtration and water-change budget.
  • Aquarium footprint — length, width and surface area, not just gallons.
  • Compatibility — temperament, size difference and fin-nipping risk between species.
  • Water requirements — temperature, pH and hardness overlap across every fish in a combination.

For the 10 gallon fish tank specifically, recommendations assume the standard 20 × 10 × 12 inch footprint. Species were excluded if their adult size, activity or group requirement outgrows a 20-inch tank, even where they are commonly sold for one. Stocking percentages come from our fish tank stocking calculator set to 10 gallons with a correctly rated filter; per-species numbers match the species tank size calculators.

Dimensions and empty weights are typical figures for the size class, not specifications for any particular product; filled weights are estimates built from the assumptions shown. Husbandry ranges are general guidance drawn from mainstream aquarium practice and our editorial policy, not veterinary or scientific advice. Individual fish, tank layouts and local water vary — check any plan against your own parameters before buying fish.
