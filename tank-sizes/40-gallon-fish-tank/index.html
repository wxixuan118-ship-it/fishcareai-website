40 Gallon Fish Tank: Dimensions, Weight, Stocking & Best Fish

A 40 gallon breeder fish tank measures about 36 × 18 × 16 inches (91 × 46 × 41 cm), weighs roughly 60 lb (27 kg) empty and about 470 lb (213 kg) once filled with water, gravel and décor, and comfortably holds 15–25 small-to-medium community fish. It holds about 151 liters. A less common 40 long is 48 × 12 × 16 inches — a 4-foot tank with a narrow floor.

The breeder is the shape most people mean by “40 gallon”, and it is one of the most recommended tanks in the hobby. Its 18-inch front-to-back depth gives it the same floor as a 50 gallon, which makes it unusually good for bottom dwellers, dwarf cichlids, goldfish and aquascaping, while its low 16-inch height keeps it easy to reach into and easy to light.

40 Gallon Fish Tank Dimensions

Two different tanks are sold as “40 gallon”. The breeder is short and wide, with an 18-inch depth that is rare at this volume; the 40 long is a 4-foot tank with a narrow 12-inch floor. They suit different fish, and their lids, lights and stands are not interchangeable.

Why the breeder shape is so popular

The breeder's 36 × 18 inch floor is 648 square inches — the same as a 50 gallon and 80% more than a 29. Floor area is what bottom dwellers, territorial dwarf cichlids and shrimp actually use, and it is also the surface where gas exchange happens. At the same time, the 16-inch height means light reaches the bottom easily and your arm reaches the substrate without getting the sleeve wet.

The trade-off is length. At 36 inches, the breeder is a foot shorter than a 55, so fish that cruise in long lanes — larger rainbowfish, Denison barbs, giant danios — are better served by the 4-foot 40 long or a 55 gallon. The 50 gallon uses the same footprint and adds 3 inches of height for taller fish and aquascapes.

How Much Does a 40 Gallon Fish Tank Weigh?

Freshwater weighs about 8.34 lb per US gallon (3.78 kg per gallon), so 40 gallons of water would be 334 lb. A running breeder holds a few gallons less, but its wide floor carries a lot of gravel, and the total is what the stand and floor must hold.

Call it about 470 lb. The gravel is a bigger share than in most tanks: the breeder's wide floor takes as much substrate as a 50 gallon. A bare-bottom breeder — common for goldfish and breeding setups — comes in near 400 lb; an aquascape with deep soil and large stones can pass 520 lb.

Placement safety: use a stand made for a 36 × 18 inch aquarium — a 36 × 12 stand for a 30 gallon will not support the breeder's edges. Level it in both directions before filling. Ground floors are rarely a concern; on upper floors, place it across the joists near a load-bearing wall, and consult a builder if you are unsure. How Many Fish Can Live in a 40 Gallon Tank?

A 40 gallon breeder supports roughly 15–25 small-to-medium community fish — for example a dwarf cichlid pair, one or two schools and a bottom group — or a single-species tank such as two or three fancy goldfish. That assumes a filter rated for the tank and weekly water changes.

What actually sets the limit in a 40 breeder

  • Floor area. The breeder's 36 × 18 inch floor is its strength: two bottom groups, or a cichlid territory plus corydoras, fit where a 29 holds one.
  • Length. Thirty-six inches suits most small schools but not large cruisers.
  • Height. Sixteen inches is enough for an angelfish pair by the calculator's rule, but tall aquascapes are limited.
  • Territory. One pair of dwarf cichlids, or one pair of kribensis, per breeder. Two pairs usually fight.
  • Bioload. Goldfish and cichlids produce far more waste than tetras; size filtration accordingly.
  • Maintenance. Weekly 25% changes let you stock toward the upper figure.

Practical examples for a 40 breeder

  • Works well: 1 pair of German blue rams, 12 cardinal tetras and 8 sterbai corydoras.
  • Works well: 3 fancy goldfish with a filter rated for 80+ gallons — the calculator allows 2–3 fancies at this size.
  • Works well: 1 pair of angelfish, 10 lemon tetras and 6 bronze corydoras.
  • Works well: 8–9 female bettas in a densely planted tank — the calculator's easiest sorority size.
  • Does not work: an oscar or a group of silver dollars — both outgrow the footprint quickly.

40 Gallon Fish Tank Stocking Ideas

Six stocking plans for a 36 × 18 × 16 inch 40 gallon breeder. They make use of the wide floor and low height, and none repeats the plans on our 10, 20, 29, 50, 55 or 100 gallon pages.

Ram and Cardinal Soft-Water Tank

A warm, planted dwarf-cichlid display
Centerpiece1 pair of German blue rams
Schooling12 cardinal tetras
Bottom8 sterbai corydoras
HardscapeSand, flat stones for spawning, plants at the back

Why it works: Rams, cardinals and sterbai corys all thrive at 80–82°F in soft, slightly acidic water. The breeder's floor gives the rams a territory and the corys a separate foraging area, and the cardinals keep the mid-water busy.

Watch for: Rams are sensitive to nitrate and new tanks — add them last, months after cycling, and keep up weekly changes.

Why it works: The goldfish calculator allows two to three fancies in a breeder and calls it the best starter goldfish tank: the wide surface helps oxygenation and the low height makes cleaning easy. See the goldfish care guide.

Watch for: Goldfish produce a lot of waste — 30–40% weekly changes are common. No tropical tank mates.

How to Set Up a 40 Gallon Fish Tank

Setting up a 40 breeder follows the standard freshwater sequence — our how to set up a fish tank guide covers the fundamentals. The steps below add what changes for a wide, low tank.

  1. Choose the location and standThe breeder is 18 inches deep — measure the alcove and check there is room for a hang-on-back filter behind it. Use a stand made for a 36 × 18 inch aquarium.
  2. Level the empty tankLevel side to side and front to back; the wide base makes small errors easy to miss.
  3. Add substrateAbout 70–80 lb of gravel or sand for a 2-inch layer — more than people expect. Sand suits corydoras, kuhlis and rams; bare bottom suits goldfish.
  4. Install filter and heaterA canister or a hang-on-back filter rated for 40–75 gallons. With 18 inches of depth, aim the outlet across the tank so water reaches the front corners. A 200 W heater, or two 100 W, in the flow.
  5. Add hardscape and plantsThe wide floor allows a real foreground, midground and background. Give cichlids caves or flat stones at one end and open sand at the other. See the plant care guide.
  6. Fill and condition the waterFill over a plate and dose dechlorinator for about 36 gallons. See preparing water for a fish tank.
  7. Cycle the tankEstablish filter bacteria first — usually 4–6 weeks. See how to cycle a fish tank and the cycle tracker.
  8. Confirm water parametersAmmonia and nitrite at zero within 24 hours of dosing, nitrate present. Check GH and KH if you plan rams or other soft-water fish — see aquarium water parameters.
  9. Add fish in stagesSchool and bottom group first, cichlids or centerpiece last. Adding everything at once risks new tank syndrome.
  10. Monitor the first monthsTest weekly for two months. Sensitive fish like rams should wait until the tank has been stable for several months.

Equipment for a 40 Gallon Aquarium

A 40 breeder takes mid-size equipment, and its shape makes some choices easier: lights reach the bottom easily, and there is room behind the tank for hang-on-back filters.

🌀Filter

Aim for four to six times the volume per hour — about 160–240 GPH of rated flow — from a canister or a hang-on-back filter labelled for 40–75 gallons. Goldfish and cichlid tanks should size to the top; many breeder owners run two hang-on-backs, one at each end. In an 18-inch-deep tank, point the outlet across the width so the front corners do not stagnate.

🌡️Heater

A 40 gallon in a 70°F room heated to 78°F needs about 150–200 W; one 200 W heater or two 100 W heaters (for redundancy) suit most homes. Rams at 82–84°F may need the full 200 W in a cool room. Goldfish tanks need none.

💡Lighting

A 36-inch fixture fits. The 16-inch height is the breeder's advantage for plants: a mid-range LED reaches the substrate, so carpeting plants are realistic without a top-end light. Run 6–8 hours on a timer.

🪨Substrate

About 70–80 lb of gravel or sand for a 2-inch layer across 36 × 18 inches — the same as a 50 gallon. Sand suits corydoras, loaches and dwarf cichlids; goldfish keepers often go bare-bottom.

Water Parameters for a 40 Gallon Aquarium

A 40 breeder has no “correct” water chemistry of its own. Rams want soft, warm water; goldfish want cool water; boesemani rainbowfish want hard, alkaline water. Pick species whose ranges overlap with your tap water rather than adjusting the water to fit the fish.

The breeder's wide surface helps with oxygen, which is one reason it suits goldfish and heavily stocked tanks. Its volume is large enough to stay stable but small enough that weekly changes are quick. Ammonia and nitrite at zero, and nitrate kept low through water changes, are the universal targets.

Test GH, KH and pH from the tap before choosing fish. Our fish tank water guide and what kind of water for a fish tank explain the options; how to lower nitrates covers the most common problem.

40 Gallon Fish Tank Maintenance

The breeder is one of the easiest tanks to maintain: its low height means you reach the substrate without a ladder or long tools. Use this framework and let nitrate readings adjust it.

  • Partial water changes. 25% weekly (about 9 gallons); 30–40% for goldfish or cichlids. The water change schedule tool sets the rhythm from your readings.
  • Substrate cleaning. The wide floor collects waste — vacuum open areas weekly.
  • Filter maintenance. Rinse sponges in old tank water every 3–4 weeks; with two filters, clean them on alternate weeks.
  • Algae. Scrape the glass weekly; keep the light to 6–8 hours.
  • Testing. Ammonia, nitrite and nitrate weekly for three months; the water parameter checker explains results and the aquarium log tracks them.
  • Top-ups. The wide surface evaporates quickly — top up twice a week.

Is a 40 Gallon Fish Tank Right for You?

The 40 breeder is the tank many experienced keepers buy when they have room for only one: big enough for almost any community of small fish, small enough for a spare room.

✅ Advantages

  • Wide floor. The same 36 × 18 inch footprint as a 50 — ideal for bottom dwellers and dwarf cichlids.
  • Easy to maintain. The low 16-inch height means easy reach and easy lighting.
  • Goldfish-friendly. The calculator's best starter goldfish tank.
  • Angelfish pair. The calculator's recommended pair tank.
  • Versatile. Communities, sororities, cichlid pairs, aquascapes and grow-out tanks.

⚠️ Considerations

  • Only 36 inches long. Large cruising schools want a 4-foot tank.
  • Heavy for its volume. About 470 lb running, partly because of the gravel.
  • Deep footprint. Needs 18 inches of depth, plus filter space behind.
  • Shape confusion. The 40 long uses different lids, lights and stands.
  • Short for tall aquascapes. The 50 gallon adds 3 inches of height on the same footprint.

Choose a 40 breeder if you want dwarf cichlids, an angelfish pair, fancy goldfish, a betta sorority or a wide aquascape. Choose a 29 gallon if space is limited to 30 inches. Choose a 55 gallon if your fish need a 4-foot run.

40 Gallon Fish Tank FAQ

What are the dimensions of a 40 gallon fish tank?

A 40 gallon breeder is about 36 inches long, 18 inches front to back and 16 inches tall (91 × 46 × 41 cm); some brands list 16–17 inches depending on the rim. A 40 gallon long is about 48 × 12 × 16 inches. Both hold about 151 liters.

What is a 40 gallon breeder tank?

A short, wide 40 gallon tank with a 36 × 18 inch footprint — the same floor as a 50 gallon. The name comes from its use for breeding fish, but its wide floor and low height make it one of the most popular community and goldfish tanks.

How much does a 40 gallon fish tank weigh?

About 55–65 lb (25–30 kg) empty and about 440–500 lb (200–227 kg) filled and running: roughly 60 lb of glass, 300 lb of water (36 actual gallons), 70–80 lb of gravel and some décor. A typical setup comes to about 470 lb.

How many fish can I put in a 40 gallon tank?

Roughly 15–25 small-to-medium community fish, for example a pair of German blue rams, 12 cardinal tetras and 8 sterbai corydoras. That assumes a filter rated for the tank and weekly water changes.

How many goldfish can live in a 40 gallon tank?

Two to three fancy goldfish, or one single-tail such as a comet, according to the goldfish tank size calculator. The breeder's wide shape makes it the best starter goldfish tank, but comets eventually need a pond.

How many angelfish can live in a 40 gallon tank?

Three to four, according to the angelfish tank size calculator — but a single bonded pair with tank mates is the most reliable setup. The breeder is the calculator's recommended pair tank.

How many guppies can live in a 40 gallon tank?

About 20–23 adult guppies — enough for a breeding colony or a mixed livebearer community.

What size heater do I need for a 40 gallon tank?

About 150–200 W; one 200 W heater or two 100 W heaters for redundancy. Warm-water fish like rams may need the full 200 W in a cool room.

What size filter do I need for a 40 gallon tank?

About 160–240 GPH of rated flow from a canister or a hang-on-back filter rated for 40–75 gallons. Many breeder owners run two hang-on-backs for goldfish or cichlids.

Is a 40 breeder or a 55 gallon better?

They suit different fish. The 40 breeder has a wider floor (18 inches) and is easier to maintain; the 55 is a foot longer (48 inches) and suits active schools and larger rainbowfish. For dwarf cichlids, goldfish and aquascaping, the breeder is often the better choice.

How FishCare AI Makes Tank Recommendations

The species, group sizes and stocking combinations on this page are based on the same factors the Aquarium Planner and Fish Compatibility Checker weigh for every species in our database:

  • Adult size — we plan around the fish an animal becomes, not the juvenile sold in stores.
  • Swimming behaviour — cruising, hovering and darting species need different lengths and depths.
  • Territorial requirements — cichlids and some catfish claim floor space that has to exist in the footprint.
  • Schooling needs — shoaling species are counted as groups, never as individuals.
  • Bioload — body mass, diet and messiness drive the filtration and water-change budget.
  • Aquarium footprint — length, width and surface area, not just gallons.
  • Compatibility — temperament, size difference and fin-nipping risk between species.
  • Water requirements — temperature, pH and hardness overlap across every fish in a combination.

For the 40 gallon fish tank specifically, recommendations assume the 36 × 18 × 16 inch breeder. Species that need more than 36 inches of swimming length were excluded or flagged, while species that use floor area were favoured. Dimensions and empty weights are typical figures for the size class, not specifications for any particular product; filled weights are estimates built from the assumptions shown.
