Badis Fish Care Guide with the Chameleon Fish and Black Tiger Badis

Badis badis

Updated October 24, 2026 · FishCare AI Editorial Team · 4 sources

Quick answer: Badis badis is a small, slow, colour-changing predator, not a typical community fish. A pair needs a tank with at least a 31 × 12 in base, cool to moderate water of 59–77 °F, a cave for each male, and live or frozen foods such as brine shrimp and Daphnia, because most refuse flakes.[2] The "black tiger badis" is a different fish, the miniature Dario tigris, formally described in 2022.[3][4]

The badis (Badis badis), sold as the chameleon fish, is a small predator from the Ganges river system that can change colour within moments.

Its range runs from the Yamuna in Himachal Pradesh down the Ganges to its delta in Bangladesh, with records from Nepal, the Mahanadi system and parts of Assam.[2][1] It favours slow, often turbid water with submerged plants and is often found among water lilies.[2] The species was described by Hamilton only from notes and drawings, so in 2002 Kullander and Britz designated a neotype from West Bengal to fix its identity against similar-looking relatives.[2]

That 2002 revision split the family into many more species and created the genus Dario, which is why badid names in the trade are confusing.[2] This guide covers care for the chameleon fish and explains what you are actually buying when a shop sells a "black tiger badis".

Badis Tank Size and Setup

A pair or small group fits in a tank with a base of 80 × 30 cm (31 × 12 in) or more, about 70 litres.[2] Build a structured tank on a sand or fine gravel substrate with water-worn rocks and pebbles, driftwood twigs, floating plants and leaf litter. Filtration and lighting can be modest.[2]

Caves matter most. Males claim them as spawning sites, and breeders use half coconut shells or upturned clay pots with an entrance cut in.[2] Spread the caves across the tank rather than grouping them, so each male holds his own area and rivals rarely meet.[2]

Badis Water Parameters

Seriously Fish gives 15–25 °C (59–77 °F), pH 6.0–7.5 and 3–15 dGH. FishBase records a wider tolerance of 14–30 °C.[1][2] Many people keep this species warmer than it prefers by putting it in a standard 80 °F tropical community tank. A room-temperature tank in the low to mid 70s °F is closer to its natural conditions, and it may not need a heater at all.

What Badis Eat

Badids are micropredators that eat small crustaceans, worms, insect larvae and zooplankton. In aquaria they often refuse dried food entirely.[2] Offer small frozen or live foods such as brine shrimp, Daphnia and glassworm.[2] Seriously Fish gives one specific warning: all badids tend to become obese and more disease-prone when fed bloodworm (chironomid larvae) or Tubifex, so leave both out of the diet.[2]

They are shy, deliberate feeders. In a tank with fast eaters they lose out, which is the main reason they do best in a species tank or with very calm company.

Badis Tank Mates and Behaviour

This is a slow, retiring fish that larger or pushier species easily intimidate or outcompete for food.[2] Do not keep it with territorial bottom-dwellers unless the tank is very large, and never mix it with other Badis species, which may hybridise.[2] Cherry and other Neocaridina or Caridina shrimp may be eaten.[2]

It is not a schooling fish. Rival males can be aggressive towards each other in a small tank, so buy one pair or one male with several females. In larger tanks a group works, provided each male can hold a territory.[2] Small, peaceful midwater fish such as chili rasboras or celestial pearl danios can share the tank if feeding is managed.

What Is a Black Tiger Badis?

The fish sold as "black tiger badis" or "black tiger dario" is not a form of Badis badis. For years it was treated as a colour variant of the flame red dwarf species, Dario hysginon, from northern Myanmar.[4] Aquarists noticed that the two would not interbreed. In 2022 Britz, Kullander and Rüber described it as a separate species, Dario tigris, from mountain streams south of Mogaung in the Ayeyarwaddy basin.[3][4]

Dario tigris is far smaller than the chameleon fish and needs a small, densely planted tank, tiny live foods, and no competition from larger fish. It is a different care project. Importers note that frightened D. tigris and D. hysginon look alike, so mixed shipments do happen.[4] Buy coloured-up fish, and check that the females have the spotted nape.

Sexing and Breeding Badis

Females are smaller, rounder and duller, with no blue in the fins. Mature males develop longer dorsal, anal and tail fins.[2] Badis are cave spawners that form temporary pair bonds.[2]

  1. Breeding males darken almost to black, their fins turn bright blue, and they court females passing their caves. Males often lock jaws with a female and pull her towards the cave.[2]
  2. A willing female enters and lays a typical clutch of 30–100 eggs. The male then chases her out and guards and fans the brood alone.[2]
  3. Eggs hatch in two to three days, and the fry become free-swimming at six to eight days old.[2]
  4. About a week later the fry leave the cave, and the adults, including the father, may start to eat them. Move the fry to a rearing tank and feed microworms, then brine shrimp nauplii.[2]

Badis FAQ

Why is it called the chameleon fish?
The chameleon fish changes colour quickly, especially when breeding or stressed, from pale with faint bars to almost black.[2]
Is the black tiger badis the same as the chameleon fish?
No. Black tiger badis are Dario tigris, a miniature species from northern Myanmar described in 2022.[3][4]
Will a chameleon fish eat flakes?
Usually not. Plan on frozen or live brine shrimp, Daphnia and glassworm, and avoid bloodworm and Tubifex.[2]
Can it live with shrimp?
Adult shrimp may survive, but shrimplets and small Neocaridina or Caridina are likely to be eaten.[2]
